\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This primer explains, from the ground up, every piece of advanced mathematics
and physics used in the manuscript \emph{Coupling-gated cortical coherence}
and its Supplemental Material. It is written for readers with a
general interest in the mind who want to follow the argument without prior
knowledge of the specialist vocabulary or reading Lean~4 source
or chasing down each cited result. Its parts explain how boundaries can arise,
why finite memory loses information, what forgetting costs in heat, how an
agent changes its future inputs, how rhythms can align, and how local
descriptions might assemble into one state that can represent itself.
They then explain the computer-checked proofs, simulations,
brain-recording analysis, physical calibration and proposed interpretation in
terms of experience. Explanations begin with the underlying idea and concrete
examples before introducing notation. Most topics follow one rubric: what the object is,
why the framework needs it, what is actually proved, and what is not.
No proof is reproduced in full; two are sketched because their structure is
unusual. The aim is literacy, not mastery.

This document is a companion, not part of the publication. Where it differs
from the manuscript or the Supplemental Material, those are authoritative.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the argument is, and where the mathematics sits}

The manuscript follows three requirements of its proposed account:
coherence, compatibility and self-representation. Coupling can sustain
collective timing; compatible local descriptions can form one global state;
and an encoding within that state can reconstruct it. The second and third
requirements need objects and assumptions beyond the phase model.

The cortex is the brain's outer neural sheet. Neurons communicate through
electrical activity, including brief pulses called spikes; a population rhythm
is repeated activity across a group of cells. EEG, or electroencephalography,
records voltage differences at the scalp. The framework asks whether physical
interactions can help these rhythms keep time together, and what more would
be required for coordinated activity to carry one experienced situation.

The physical hypothesis concerns extracellular geometry and recovery after
awakening. Extracellular means outside cells: the hypothesis concerns how
the spaces between cells affect their electrical interaction. Coupling means
how strongly one rhythm changes another's timing. The prediction first asks
what happens when that strength is held fixed long enough for the statistics
to settle; this is its \emph{stationary} prediction. In recovery, coupling
changes over time. Its rate of change is the \emph{ramp speed}, and a system
needs time to respond to that change.

There is also \emph{nonidentifiability}: different causes can give the same
measured pattern. Stronger interaction, less random timing jitter (\emph{phase
diffusion}), and a shared external influence (a \emph{common drive}) can produce
identical settled distributions of timing around a cycle. The test
therefore needs phase dynamics and evidence distinguishing the interaction
mechanism, as well as a concentration--coherence relation.

The representational argument asks what coherent activity carries.
Here a phase is a position within a repeating cycle, like a clock hand's angle.
Equal phases can coexist with incompatible spatial profiles: two regions can
keep time together while assigning different values to a shared location. A plasticity
control---a simulation that lets connection strengths change---preserves
coherence while losing alignment with a specified template, or target pattern;
that alignment statistic is distinct from overlap compatibility. The self
proposal then asks whether the state assembled from local descriptions
accurately represents itself. Identifying the relevant cover and encoding
requires independent evidence. A \emph{cover} is a collection of overlapping
regions that together include the whole system; an encoding records its state,
and a readout reconstructs a state from that record. Uniqueness within a specified cover and map
does not choose a subject.

Thermodynamics studies energy, heat and the constraints on their conversion.
Its results constrain a possible implementation of these
requirements. Finite capacity and a symmetry-breaking boundary are independent
starting objects, and their links to erasure, information and a continuum
coupling require assumptions. Lean~4 checks two branches: one for passive
predictive memory, and one for a specified process that acts on its environment.
Each uses eight connecting hypotheses and supplied structures, and they share
the passage from coarse-graining to the same fixed state. Their conclusion
and its identification with experience have different statuses:
the first is conditional mathematics, the second a philosophical commitment.

\subsection{How to read this primer}

The primer introduces vocabulary in the text, using ordinary examples before
formal statements. The headings \textbf{What it is}, \textbf{Why the framework
needs it}, \textbf{The statement} and \textbf{What it does not give} separate
the idea, its purpose, the result and its limits. Worked walkthroughs sometimes
need several steps between those headings. The notation table is a reference
to return to, rather than a list of definitions to memorize first.

\textbf{In Lean} notes locate the computer-checked results. Lean is software
that checks whether a proof follows from its stated assumptions; Mathlib is
its library of mathematical definitions and theorems. Names in typewriter font
are searchable source identifiers, not additional concepts to learn. A
\emph{hypothesis} is an assumption for a result; a \emph{theorem} is a proved
consequence; a \emph{witness} is an example satisfying the assumptions; and a
\emph{counterexample} shows why a proposed stronger statement fails.

A structure or class is a record of named data and conditions. A field of
that record is one entry, and an instance is an example meeting its conditions.
To discharge a hypothesis means to supply its proof. The later formal-method
part explains why packaging assumptions this way matters.

The part on formal methodology explains how physical postulates enter the
conditional composition as assumptions on specified objects. This is the
technical basis for keeping the manuscript's physical hypotheses distinct from
its mathematical conclusions.

\section{Notation}

\subsection{How to read the symbols}

A function or map is a rule assigning an output to each input: $f(x)=x^2$
assigns $9$ to $3$. A scalar is a single number; a vector is an ordered list
of numbers, pictured as an arrow when it has two or three components. A matrix
is a rectangular table of numbers. A field assigns a value to each location,
like a temperature map. A kernel $K(x,y)$ assigns an interaction strength to
each \emph{pair} of locations. The substrate is the set of locations being
modelled. A functional assigns a number to a whole function or field, such as
its total energy.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[Sets and membership.] $x\in X$ means that $x$ belongs to the set $X$;
$|X|$ counts its elements when it is finite. $U\subseteq X$ means every member
of $U$ belongs to $X$. $U\cap V$ is their overlap; $U\cup V$ combines them.
$\R$ denotes real numbers, $\Z$ integers, and $S^1$ a circle. An interval
$[a,b]$ includes its endpoints; $(a,b)$ excludes them.
\item[Sums and averages.] $\sum_i x_i$ means add the listed values.
$N^{-1}\sum_i x_i$ divides their sum by their count. An expectation
$\E[X]$, also written $\langle X\rangle$, is an average weighted by the
probability of each outcome. Absolute value $|x|$ ignores a number's sign;
for a vector, length is written $\|x\|$ (or $|x|$ for a complex number).
\item[Integrals and measures.] $\int f(x)\,d\mu(x)$ is a weighted total over
locations. The measure $\mu$ specifies the weights: it can count sites or
measure lengths, areas or probabilities. When $\mu$ has total weight one,
the integral is an average. A density tells how that weight is spread out;
its integral over a region gives the weight in that region.
\item[Change and limits.] $\Delta t$ is a time interval and $\Delta x$ the
change during it. $dx/dt$, or $\dot x$, is instantaneous change per unit time.
$\partial f/\partial x$ varies $x$ while holding the other inputs fixed.
$x(t)\to L$ means the values approach $L$. $x\propto y$ means a constant
multiple; $x\sim y$ means their ratio approaches one in the specified limit.
$O(h^2)$ means an error bounded by a constant times $h^2$; $o(h^2)$ means
an error whose ratio to $h^2$ tends to zero.
\item[Exponentials and logarithms.] $e^x$ is the exponential function, and
$\log x$ or $\ln x$ is its inverse: $\log(e^x)=x$. A logarithm converts
multiplication into addition. A square root $\sqrt{x}$ is the nonnegative
number whose square is $x$.
\item[Angles and complex numbers.] A full turn is $2\pi$ radians, a half turn
$\pi$. The coordinates of a unit arrow at angle $\theta$ are
$(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. The compact notation
$e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}=\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta$ packages those two
coordinates; $\mathrm{i}^2=-1$. This imaginary unit is different from a
subscript $i$ labelling an oscillator. No physical quantity is imaginary.
\end{description}

\section{Spontaneous symmetry breaking}
\label{sec:ssb}

Think of a landscape with two equally deep valleys. The landscape does not
prefer left to right, but a ball resting in one valley has picked a side.
Energy gives the landscape its height; a minimum is a lowest point. A local
minimum is lowest nearby, while a global minimum is lowest among all allowed
states. This example assumes the ball can lose energy so that it settles.

\what
A physical system is described by an energy functional. A \emph{symmetry} of
that functional is a transformation---a rotation, a reflection, a sign
flip---that leaves the energy unchanged. Symmetry is \emph{spontaneously
broken} when the energy functional has a symmetry that its lowest-energy
states do not: the laws are symmetric, the outcomes are not. The standard toy
model is the double well
\[
 V(v) = (v^2-1)^2 ,
\]
which is invariant under $v\mapsto -v$ but has two minima, at $v=-1$ and
$v=+1$. A ball in this potential must settle in one of them; whichever it
picks, the reflection symmetry is gone from the outcome while remaining in the
law.

The set of all lowest-energy states is the \emph{vacuum manifold}
$\mathcal{V}$. For the double well it is the two-point set $\{-1,+1\}$. In
richer models it can be a circle, a sphere, or a more complicated space, and
its shape is what governs everything in the next section. ``Vacuum'' here
means lowest energy, not empty space. A manifold is a space that locally looks
like an ordinary line, plane or higher-dimensional coordinate space; the
traditional phrase also covers the simple set of minima in this example.

The spatial total depends on how locations are weighted. \emph{Almost
everywhere} means except on a set of zero weight under the chosen measure.
For ordinary length on a line, an isolated point has zero weight. A
\emph{Dirac measure} instead puts all weight on one chosen point. In a
two-point toy space, an energy average using that measure sees one point and
ignores the other; minimizing the average need not minimize the ignored value.

\why
The framework's first move is that a conscious agent must be a
\emph{bounded} thing---something with an inside and an outside, separable from
its environment. Rather than assuming a boundary, the framework wants one to
be forced by physics. Symmetry breaking supplies the mechanism: if different
regions of a substrate settle into different minima, something has to happen
in between, and that something is a boundary.

\statement
Two results are proved, and the difference between them matters. The
\emph{pointwise vacuum} result says that a field globally minimizing the
potential energy lies in the vacuum manifold \emph{almost everywhere}---not
everywhere. The almost-everywhere is not slack in the proof; it is necessary.
On the double well over a two-point spacetime carrying a Dirac probability
measure, the minimizing field sits at a vacuum value on the support of the
measure and at $0$ off it, so the pointwise version of the claim is false of
the very field the theorem applies to.

\scope
No conserved quantity---one staying constant during motion---is constructed
from this. Noether's theorem connects certain continuous symmetries to conserved
quantities, but is not proved here. A symmetry group is a collection of
transformations closed under combination and reversal; a metric measures
distance. The symmetry group and the metric on the
substrate appear in the definitions but are consumed by no theorem.

\section{Topological defects: the Kibble classification}
\label{sec:kibble}

\emph{Topology} studies what survives stretching and bending without tearing
or joining. A connected space is in one piece; a connected component is one
of its separate pieces. A continuous field has no abrupt jumps. If its value
changes from $-1$ on one side of an interval to $+1$ on the other, it must
pass through $0$. If only $-1$ and $+1$ are energy minima, somewhere between
the sides the field must leave the set of minima. That intervening region is
the simplest example of a \emph{defect}.

To describe more complicated defects, imagine drawing a loop on the space of
allowed minimum values. Can it shrink to a point while staying in that space?
A loop around a circle cannot; a loop drawn inside a filled disk can.
\emph{Homotopy} means continuous deformation. The symbols $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$
classify deformations of loops and spheres respectively; $\pi_0$ records
connected components. ``Non-contractible'' or ``not null-homotopic'' means
that the relevant loop or sphere cannot shrink to a point there. The defect
names below use the usual three-dimensional spatial setting.

\what
Suppose a field takes values in the vacuum manifold $\mathcal{V}$ almost
everywhere across some region of space. Different parts of the region may have
chosen different vacuum values. Whether the field can interpolate smoothly
between them depends on the \emph{topology} of $\mathcal{V}$---specifically on
its homotopy groups \citep{kibble1976}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item $\pi_0(\mathcal{V})\ne 0$ ($\mathcal{V}$ is disconnected): \textbf{domain
walls}. Two regions in different connected components cannot be joined without
leaving $\mathcal{V}$ somewhere.
\item $\pi_1(\mathcal{V})\ne 0$ ($\mathcal{V}$ has non-contractible loops):
\textbf{vortices} (line defects).
\item $\pi_2(\mathcal{V})\ne 0$: \textbf{monopoles} (point defects).
\end{itemize}

$\pi_0(\mathcal{V})$ is just the set of connected components---the crudest
possible topological invariant, and the only one the framework uses.

\why
A domain wall separates regions choosing different minima. This supplies a
candidate physical boundary for the framework. A forced wall need not enclose
a bounded region: a plane dividing space also separates two domains. Further
physical assumptions are needed to identify it with an agent's boundary.

\statement
The result taken is the $\pi_0$ case, and it is startlingly cheap:

\begin{quote}
Let $\phi$ be a continuous field on a \emph{connected} substrate, and let
$M$ be the candidate set of allowed minimum values within its value space.
If $\phi(x_1)$ and $\phi(x_2)$ lie in different
connected components of $M$---equivalently, if no preconnected subset of $M$
contains both---then $\phi$ takes some value outside $M$.
\end{quote}

\noindent
The entire proof is: a continuous image of a connected set is connected. The
image $\phi(\text{substrate})$ is connected; if it stayed inside $M$ it would
be a connected subset of $M$ containing points of two different components,
which is impossible. So it leaves $M$. That exit point is the wall.

Note what is \emph{absent} from the hypotheses: no energy potential, no
Lagrangian, no symmetry group, no dynamics. Only continuity and connectedness.

An extension fills in values inside a region while retaining its prescribed
boundary values. A contractible interior can be continuously shrunk to a
point; a disk is an example. There is a second, differently directed result, the \emph{extension
obstruction}: boundary data that is not null-homotopic admits no continuous
extension over a contractible interior. This says a defect \emph{obstructs
extension}; it does not say a defect must exist. The two directions are easy
to conflate and the development keeps them apart deliberately.

\scope
Higher defects ($\pi_1$ vortices, $\pi_2$ monopoles) are recorded as available
and not taken; the argument uses domain walls alone. And the theorem forces a
\emph{topological} separation. A register is a physical store of distinguishable
states. E12 supplies a field realizing the boundary premise; E23 identifies
that boundary with a register whose update erases information.

\section{Finite capacity}

Suppose a device has four distinguishable states, labelled $00$, $01$, $10$
and $11$. It can record the answers to two yes/no questions, or two bits.
Three such questions have eight possible answer strings, so four states
cannot distinguish them all. This is a counting limit before it is a claim
about heat, neurons or computation.

A probability distribution assigns each possible outcome a nonnegative
weight, with all weights summing to one. A uniform distribution gives equal
weight to every outcome. Entropy averages the surprise $-\log p$ of seeing
an outcome of probability $p$: rare outcomes are more surprising. Using
base-two logarithms measures entropy in \emph{bits}; natural logarithms give
\emph{nats}. One unbiased yes/no answer carries one bit, or $\ln2$ nats.

A map is \emph{injective} if distinct inputs always give distinct outputs:
no records merge. It is \emph{surjective} if every possible output is reached:
no state is missed. A \emph{bijection} has both properties. The pigeonhole
principle says more objects than boxes forces at least two into one box.

\what
Shannon entropy $H(p)$ measures, in bits or nats, how much uncertainty a
probability distribution carries---equivalently, how much information you gain
on average by learning which outcome occurred. On a finite set $X$ it is
bounded:
\[
 H(p) \;\le\; \log|X| ,
\]
with equality exactly when $p$ is uniform. A system with $|X|$ distinguishable
states cannot hold more than $\log|X|$ nats, however cleverly it encodes.

A second, purely combinatorial fact does most of the work. If an environment
delivers a stream of $k$ perturbations drawn from an alphabet $P$, there are
$|P|^k$ possible histories. If $|P|^k > |X|$, then by the pigeonhole principle
the map from histories to internal states cannot be injective: two distinct
histories leave the system in the same state. This holds \emph{whatever} the
update rule is, and even when every individual step of it is reversible.

\why
A bounded agent is bombarded by its environment. It cannot retain everything.
Something is discarded, and the next section prices that.

\scope
Finite capacity is \emph{assumed}, not derived---nothing here proves cortex
has a finite effective register. And finite capacity does not imply that every
update erases; it implies only that \emph{some} history pair collides once the
stream is long enough. The hypothesis is also fenced: on an \emph{infinite}
state space the corresponding step fails outright, as \id{Nat.succ}
witnesses---it misses $0$ yet is injective.

\section{Landauer's principle}
\label{sec:landauer}

An eraser resets either $0$ or $1$ to $0$. From the final register alone you
cannot recover which value it held. A reversible operation, such as swapping
$0$ and $1$, retains that distinction. \emph{Logical irreversibility} is this
loss of distinguishability in the update rule. Physical reversibility asks
whether the complete device and its surroundings retain the information.

A heat bath is a large environment treated as staying at a fixed temperature.
Heat is energy transferred through microscopic thermal motion; work is energy
transferred through a controlled change, such as moving a piston. Dissipation
is energy expenditure that is not recovered as useful work in the process.
Thermodynamic entropy measures how many microscopic arrangements are compatible
with a macroscopic description. The constant $k_B$ converts information in nats
to entropy units, and absolute temperature $T$ is measured in kelvin.

\what
\citet{landauer1961} observed that logically irreversible operations have a
thermodynamic price. Erasing one bit of information---mapping two
distinguishable states onto one---requires dissipating at least $k_B T\ln 2$
joules of heat into the environment. The reasoning: the microscopic laws are
reversible, so phase-space volume is conserved (Liouville's theorem). If the
system's own state space contracts, the volume has to go somewhere, and the
only place available is the bath. Bath volume expanding means bath entropy
rising, and $\Delta Q = T\Delta S_{\text{bath}}$ converts that to heat.

\why
This is what makes a bounded agent necessarily a \emph{dissipative structure}
rather than a passive one, and it supplies the thermal budget that the next
part spends.

\statement
Three vocabularies for the same heat appear in the development, and they are
deliberately connected rather than left side by side.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[Combinatorial.] On a finite state space, a map that fails to reach every
state is not injective (\id{is\_erasure\_of\_not\_surjective}). Under globally
reversible system--bath dynamics, non-injectivity of the system update forces
$\Delta Q > 0$ (\id{finite\_phase\_space\_dissipates},
\id{landauer\_from\_reversibility}). A \emph{second law} statement follows in
the same file.
\item[Measure-theoretic.] The same statement with continuous entropy and
volume contraction in place of counting arguments. The two are proved to
coincide at counting measure on a finite register:
\id{landauer\_heat\_bound\_count\_eq}.
\item[Informational.] Wasted memory as erased entropy---see
Section~\ref{sec:still-from-landauer}.
\end{description}

\noindent
The bound is \emph{not} a universal ``$k_BT\ln 2$ per non-injective step''. It
is $T$ times the entropy actually erased by the particular update; erasing one
unbiased bit is the special case giving $k_BT\ln2$.

\scope
The step from ``the state space contracted'' to ``heat was dissipated''
requires the thermodynamic postulate $\Delta Q = T\Delta S_{\text{bath}}$.
That is carried as a class field, \id{StatisticalMechanics.heat\_eq}, not
proved---and \id{Examples}~\S1 exhibits a one-bit erasure system with an explicit bath discharging it, so the postulate is demonstrably satisfiable
rather than merely asserted. Identifying such a register with a biological
boundary is a further modelling step, and it is exactly what E23 carries.

\subsection{The Norton--Shenker distinction}

For example, a device can reset its visible bit while copying the old value
into a hidden register. The visible operation merges two labels, but the
complete outputs are still distinct. The heat accounting must include what
happens to that hidden record. This is why the choice of physical state space
matters to the principle.

\what
The standard objection to naive readings of Landauer's principle
\citep{norton2005,shenker2000}: \emph{logical} irreversibility---a many-to-one
map on abstract state labels---does not by itself entail heat. If the physical
implementation is injective, nothing has been erased physically, and no heat
need flow. A computation can look many-to-one at the level of the abstraction
while the underlying device is perfectly reversible.

\why
Without this distinction the whole thermodynamic argument is a pun on the word
``erasure''.

\statement
The development defines erasure as non-injectivity on the \emph{physical}
state space, never on abstract labels. The distinction survives explicitly in
the model: retaining an incoming perturbation is injective and free
(\id{keepRecord\_injective}), while refreshing the register that held it is not
(\id{absorbStep\_is\_erasure}). In informational form the same distinction
reappears as \id{nonpredictive\_eq\_zero\_of\_injective}: on a reversible
update, the entropy erased is zero, so the bound forces wasted memory to
vanish.

\section{Mutual information and the data processing inequality}

Imagine $X$ is a stored weather reading and $Y$ is whether it is raining.
If the reading always tells you the answer, it removes your uncertainty about
$Y$. If the reading is unrelated to rain, it removes none. Mutual information
measures this reduction on average; it concerns statistical dependence, not
whether a message has meaning for a person.

The \emph{joint law} $P_{XY}$ lists probabilities for pairs of outcomes.
The \emph{marginals} $P_X$ and $P_Y$ describe each variable separately, obtained
by adding over the other. Independence means the joint probabilities equal
the products of those separate probabilities, written $P_X\otimes P_Y$.
The Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence $D_{KL}(P\|Q)$ averages
$\log(P/Q)$ using probabilities from $P$. It is nonnegative, zero when the
distributions agree, and can be infinite if $Q$ rules out an outcome that
$P$ permits. It measures distributional discrepancy, but is not an ordinary
distance: reversing $P$ and $Q$ can change its value.

\what
Mutual information $I(X;Y)$ measures how much knowing $X$ tells you about $Y$.
Formally it is the KL divergence of the joint law from the product of its
marginals:
\[
 I(X;Y) \;=\; D_{KL}\!\left(P_{XY}\,\|\,P_X\otimes P_Y\right),
\]
which is zero exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

The \emph{data processing inequality} says information cannot be created by
processing. If $X\to Y\to Z$ forms a Markov chain---$Z$ depends on $X$ only
through $Y$---then $I(X;Z)\le I(X;Y)$.

\why
The passive branch splits what a system remembers into a part that predicts
the future and a part that does not. The chain here is
$X_t \to S_t \to S_{t+1}$: the system state $X_t$ at time $t$ was built from
the environmental signal $S_t$, and the environment evolves by its own
dynamics, unaffected by the system. Then
$I(X_t;S_{t+1}) \le I(X_t;S_t)$: what the memory says about the future is at
most what it says about the present. The difference,
\[
 \underbrace{I(X_t;S_t)}_{\text{total memory}} \;-\; \underbrace{I(X_t;S_{t+1})}_{\text{predictive memory}}
 \;=\; \text{\emph{nonpredictive} memory} \;\ge\; 0 ,
\]
is well-defined and non-negative.

\scope
Here the arrows describe conditional dependence, not a causal claim that the
memory generates the signal. A Markov kernel is a rule assigning probabilities
to the next state for each current state. The Markov structure encodes a \emph{no-back-action} assumption: the system
does not act on its environment. This is not decoration. The development
fences it with a counterexample---a Markov kernel on the pair that writes the
system's state into the signal and thereby \emph{raises} mutual information,
breaking the inequality. Acting systems are treated by the separate feedback
identity in Section~\ref{sec:agency}; the passive assumption applies to the
chosen variables and interval, rather than to every information-processing system.

\id{Phase3\_PredictiveThermodynamics.lean}; the inequality is
\id{predictiveInfo\_le\_mutualInfo}.

\section{The Still--Sivak--Bell--Crooks bound}

Suppose a memory records two independent unbiased bits of the present, but
only one helps predict the next signal. It carries two bits of memory, one
bit of prediction, and one nonpredictive bit. Under the physical assumptions
of this bound, the last bit contributes a lower bound $k_BT\ln2$ on dissipated
work. This is a constraint on an implementation's cost; it does not tell the
system which bit to keep or how to learn.

\what
\citet{still2012} proved that nonpredictive memory is thermodynamically
expensive:
\[
 \boxed{\;\Wdiss \;\ge\; k_B T\left[\,I(X_t;S_t) - I(X_t;S_{t+1})\,\right]\;}
\]
The work a system dissipates is at least the thermal cost of the information
it holds about the present that is useless for the future. Memory that fails
to predict is paid for in heat.

\why
Under a specified selection or optimization process, a cost for wasted memory
could favor its removal. The framework's proposed connection to predictive
structure is called \emph{structural resonance}. The bound supplies a constraint
that such a process would face; it does not supply the process. Retaining no
information also meets a bound on memory waste, so useful prediction needs
additional requirements.

\statement
The bound itself is a physical postulate---a class field,
\id{PredictiveDissipation.still\_bound}, not a theorem. Everything downstream
\emph{is} derived: the data processing inequality above; non-negativity
$\Wdiss\ge0$ (which follows rather than being assumed); the statement that a
given dissipation budget bounds wasted memory; and the statement that a
reversible quasistatic drive, dissipating nothing, leaves every retained bit
predictive. Quasistatic means changing the controls slowly enough to pass
through equilibrated states; slow operation alone does not guarantee zero
dissipation in an arbitrary driven system.

A related exact result is also carried: the Kawai--Parrondo--Van den Broeck
equality \citep{kawai2007},
\[
 \langle \Wdiss\rangle = k_B T\, D_{KL}(\text{forward}\,\|\,\text{reverse}),
\]
from which \id{nonpredictive\_le\_arrow} follows: the irreversibility of the
dynamics is at least the memory that fails to earn its keep.

The angle brackets in that equality mean an average over repeated realizations.
``Forward'' and ``reverse'' denote the distributions compared under a process
and its appropriately reversed protocol. Their KL divergence quantifies how
distinguishable the two directions are under the equality's assumptions.

\scope
\textbf{This is the single most over-read result in the framework, and the
manuscript is emphatic about it.} The bound does \emph{not}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item specify a learning rule;
\item maximize prediction;
\item require the internal representation to reproduce environmental geometry;
\item entail Bayesian inference or semantic content.
\end{itemize}
Bayesian inference updates probabilities in light of observations; semantic
content concerns what a representation is about. Neither is supplied just by
a numerical information-cost bound.
Section~\ref{sec:plasticity} reports a simulation making this concrete: a
coupling update that removes most of a squared-drift objective at the instant
it acts maintains coherence while moving \emph{away} from the structure it is
expected to learn---further from it than from almost every relabelling of the
nodes.

\lean
\id{Phase3\_PredictiveThermodynamics.lean}. The class is inhabited twice over
one correlated two-bit law (\id{Examples}~\S18)---a frozen signal
permitted to dissipate nothing, and a scrambled one forced to dissipate its
entire memory---so the theorems about it are not vacuous.

\subsection{Deriving Still's bound from Landauer's}
\label{sec:still-from-landauer}

A \emph{bridge assumption} identifies quantities from two otherwise separate
models. If wasted memory is no larger than erased entropy, a heat budget that
pays for erasure also pays for that waste. Without this comparison, two
inequalities mentioning information and heat need not concern the same system.

\what
Two thermodynamic charges have been introduced: Landauer charges a register
for \emph{destroying} information; Still charges a structure for
\emph{keeping} information it cannot use. They are the same heat viewed twice,
and the development connects them.

\statement
\id{PredictiveDissipation.ofLandauer} builds a predictive structure out of a
given register: its thermal scale is that register's temperature, its
dissipated work is that register's Landauer heat, and Still's bound is
\emph{derived} from \id{landauer\_bound} rather than postulated a second time.
The single hypothesis is the identification
\[
 \underbrace{I_{\mathrm{mem}} - I_{\mathrm{pred}}}_{\text{memory wasted}}
 \;\le\;
 \underbrace{S(\mathrm{id}) - S(t)}_{\text{entropy erased}} .
\]

\scope
That identification is a restriction, not bookkeeping. On an injective update
the right-hand side is zero, so the inequality forces the waste to vanish---the
Norton--Shenker distinction in informational form. The witness is the one-bit
eraser, where a register destroying $\log 2$ pays for a memory wasting
$\log 2$, with equality. Connecting the budget to Landauer heat therefore
requires nonpredictive information to be bounded by erased entropy; the formal
bridge derives the bound \emph{under} that identification and does not derive
the identification. That is E34.

\section{Agents act on their future inputs}
\label{sec:agency}

A weather recorder can observe rain without changing it. A controller connected
to a lamp can change what it subsequently observes: if its internal bit says
``on'' and its action turns the lamp on, the future lamp state carries
information about the controller. This correlation can arise even when the
controller and lamp were initially independent. It reflects the effect of
action on the future, which the passive Markov assumption excludes.

\what
The agency model specifies four probability rules, called \emph{channels}.
A \emph{policy} chooses an action from the current internal state; world
dynamics responds to that action; an observation channel supplies a reading;
and a memory update uses the reading and the sampled action. A policy here is
a specified rule, not necessarily a learned rule or an optimal one.
Writing $\sim$ for ``is sampled according to'', the channels are
\begin{align*}
 A_t&\sim\pi(\cdot\mid X_t),&
 S_{t+1}&\sim K(\cdot\mid S_t,A_t),\\
 O_{t+1}&\sim G(\cdot\mid S_{t+1}),&
 X_{t+1}&\sim U(\cdot\mid X_t,A_t,O_{t+1}).
\end{align*}
Here $K$ is a transition-probability rule, rather than the coupling strength
of the oscillator model. The same sampled action is supplied to the world
and the memory update. These equations describe one perception--action update;
they do not assert that the system returns to its starting state.

\why
When actions influence the signal, the difference
$I(X_t;S_t)-I(X_t;S_{t+1})$ can be negative. Calling it a nonnegative memory
waste would impose the wrong constraint. The information accounting must
allow the controller to create correlations with the future.

\statement
\emph{Conditional mutual information} $I(X;Y\mid Z)$ measures the dependence
between $X$ and $Y$ remaining when $Z$ is known, averaged over the possible
values of $Z$. For the finite states considered here,
\begin{align*}
 I(X_t;S_t)-I(X_t;S_{t+1})
 ={}& I(X_t;S_t\mid S_{t+1})\\
    &-I(X_t;S_{t+1}\mid S_t).
\end{align*}
The first conditional term asks what memory says about the present signal
beyond what the future signal tells us. The second asks what memory says about
the future beyond what the present signal tells us. Each term is nonnegative;
their difference can have either sign. The passive model makes the second
term zero and recovers its memory-waste formula exactly.

The comparison holds memory at its pre-action value $X_t$. The later internal
state $X_{t+1}$ belongs to the memory-update channel, and is not substituted
for $X_t$ halfway through the identity. Lean uses real subtraction for the
signed difference, retaining negative values.

\scope
Conditional dependence alone does not identify a causal effect. That reading
requires a causal model in which $S_t$ includes the environmental state needed
by the declared dynamics; a partial observation need not suffice. The four
channels specify how a candidate agent operates. They do not determine its
goal, explain how its policy was learned, or identify its internal variables
with neural content. An information identity also supplies no heat cost
until the physical transitions and reservoir are specified.

\section{Heat and entropy when an agent acts}
\label{sec:agency-heat}

To determine the cost of changing a lamp, a model must describe physical
transitions and their relation to a reservoir. The finite thermodynamic model
takes one elementary part of the update: the internal state $x$ stays fixed
while the world changes from $s$ to $s'$. The transition probabilities may
depend on $x$, so back action is permitted throughout.

\what
Let $p(x,s)$ be the initial joint probability and $K_x(s'\mid s)$ the
probability of the world transition. Adding over all possible starting world
states gives the final law:
\[
 p'(x,s')=\sum_s p(x,s)K_x(s'\mid s).
\]
A \emph{path law} assigns probabilities to whole transition records, here
the triples $(x,s,s')$. The model compares
\begin{align*}
 P_F(x,s,s')&=p(x,s)K_x(s'\mid s),\\
 P_R(x,s,s')&=p'(x,s')K_x(s\mid s').
\end{align*}
The forward law starts at the initial distribution. The reverse benchmark
starts at the final distribution and uses the same autonomous channel to
make the return transition. Both laws have total probability one. Autonomous
means that the transition rule is held fixed during this elementary update.

\statement
The KL divergence of these path laws is nonnegative. Expanding the logarithm
of their ratio separates the joint entropy change from the transition ratios:
\[
 \Sigma=D_{KL}(P_F\|P_R)
 =H(p')-H(p)+\sum_{x,s,s'}P_F(x,s,s')
       \log\frac{K_x(s'\mid s)}{K_x(s\mid s')}\ge0.
\]
This is an identity derived from the specified probabilities. To interpret
the last sum as reservoir entropy, a further physical assumption is needed:
\emph{local detailed balance},
\[
 q(x,s,s')=k_BT\log\frac{K_x(s'\mid s)}{K_x(s\mid s')}.
\]
It relates the relative likelihood of a transition and its reverse to the
heat $q$ entering a reservoir at temperature $T$. The mean heat
$\langle Q\rangle$ is $q$ averaged over the forward paths. Under this
assumption the balance becomes
\[
 \Sigma=H(p')-H(p)+\frac{\langle Q\rangle}{k_BT}\ge0.
\]
Here $H$ uses natural logarithms and $\Sigma$ is dimensionless. This capital
$\Sigma$ belongs to the physical path model; it is distinct from the
lowercase squared-drift functional $\sigma$ used in the plasticity study.

\why
A positive thermal scale and an upper heat budget $B$ yield the active node:
\[
 k_BT>0,\qquad
 k_BT[H(p)-H(p')]\le\langle Q\rangle\le B.
\]
Thus the joint entropy reduction is at most $B/(k_BT)$. The reduction itself
need not be positive: the system's joint entropy may increase. This constraint
uses the same process's distributions and actual heat, allowing action without
substituting a signed information difference into a passive work bound.

\scope
The theorem assumes finite state spaces, strictly positive initial masses and
transition probabilities, and this autonomous reversal rule. Zero-probability
transitions or time-dependent driving need corresponding support and reversal
conditions. This elementary theorem covers one substep with the controller
fixed. The composition below includes the memory-changing substep, with the
world fixed in turn. Additional storage or control operations require their
own dynamics and accounting.

Work needs its own first-law relation. With $q$ positive into the reservoir
and $w$ the work supplied to the joint system, that relation is
$w=E(x,s')-E(x,s)+q$. Lean derives the corresponding average relation from
this pathwise assumption. A mean heat bound alone does not identify work.

\subsection{The two substeps share one intermediate law}
\label{sec:agency-cycle}

Let $p_0(x,s)$ be the initial joint law. Actuation gives
$p_1(x,t)=\sum_s p_0(x,s)K_x(t\mid s)$. During sensing, the world $t$ is
fixed and memory changes from $x$ to $y$ through a channel $U_t(y\mid x)$.
This substep must start at $p_1(x,t)$, written in world--memory order as
$p_1^{\mathrm{swap}}(t,x)$. Its output, returned to memory--world order, is
\[
 p_2(y,t)=\sum_x p_1(x,t)U_t(y\mid x).
\]
Lean proves that this is the law produced by the composed perception--action
channels. The second initial law is constructed from the first final law;
an independently chosen distribution cannot replace it.

At one common thermal scale $\theta=k_BT>0$, add the two substep balances:
\[
 \Sigma_A+\Sigma_M
 =H(p_2)-H(p_0)+\frac{\langle Q_A\rangle+\langle Q_M\rangle}{\theta}\ge0.
\]
The intermediate entropy cancels because a coordinate exchange preserves
joint entropy. Neither $p_1$ nor $p_2$ has to be an equilibrium distribution.
If the total mean heat is at most $B$, the complete update therefore satisfies
$H(p_0)-H(p_2)\le B/\theta$.

Energy accounting uses one observable $E(x,s)$ for both substeps, evaluated
in the appropriate coordinate order. Under each substep's first law, with
heat positive into the bath and work positive into the system, the same
intermediate expected energy cancels:
\[
 \langle W_A\rangle+\langle W_M\rangle
 =\mathbb E_{p_2}E-\mathbb E_{p_0}E
   +\langle Q_A\rangle+\langle Q_M\rangle.
\]
Strict positivity and local detailed balance carry the same roles as in the
elementary theorem. Action and observation alias existing states; extra
physical registers and time-dependent control need further accounting.
The supplied upper heat budget does not identify its source in a register or
establish the coupling-convergence assumption of the active chain.

\subsection{A reciprocal two-bit example}

Consider an internal bit $x$ and a world bit $s$. In units $k_BT=1$, agreeing
bits have interaction energy zero and disagreeing bits have energy $\log3$.
Each elementary update redraws one bit, agreeing with the fixed other bit
with probability $3/4$. The transition rule and heat are specified so that
local detailed balance holds. This is a chosen theoretical model; no recording
is needed to verify its conditional mathematical consequences.

Start with two independent unbiased bits, giving probability $1/4$ to each
pair. Actuation uses action $a=x$ to update $s$. Afterwards each agreeing
pair has probability $3/8$ and each disagreeing pair $1/8$. Each bit remains
unbiased individually, while the pair has become correlated. The signed
passive information difference is negative because the future world now
carries information about the initially independent controller.

The actuation releases mean heat $(\log3)/4$, with entropy production
$\log2-(\log3)/2>0$. Sensing then uses observation $o=s$ to redraw $x$ by
the same thermal rule. Individual internal states can change while this joint
distribution is preserved; sensing has zero mean heat and entropy production.
The composed channels give the same final law as this two-substep calculation.

The general composition also applies when memory starts biased. Under the
same thermal channels, the intermediate law is then asymmetric and sensing
changes the joint law, while actuation still releases positive heat. This
example makes the coordinate swap observable: a second step initialized
without it is incompatible even though all its probabilities are positive.
The symmetric equilibrium law alone cannot detect that error.

Both substeps obey the first law with zero external work: their total mean
heat comes from the decrease in mean interaction energy. The action and
observation are aliases of the existing bits, with no extra physical storage
register in this model. Separate devices carrying those signals would require
their own accounting. Relaxing an initially nonequilibrium distribution is
not a continuing energy supply. The preference for agreement is specified by
the interaction; the model does not learn which relation to prefer.

\subsection{Choosing a policy for a finite task}
\label{sec:agency-control}

A goal can be supplied explicitly and policies compared against it. Consider
the task of making a lamp true after one complete update. The internal bit is
initially unbiased and independent of the lamp, which starts true with
probability $1/4$. A fixed world channel makes the lamp agree with the chosen
action with probability $3/4$. The memory channel then makes the internal bit
agree with the new lamp with the same probability. Every policy faces these
same channels and initial conditions.

There are four deterministic rules from a bit to a bit: always choose false,
copy the internal bit, invert it, or always choose true. The baseline is always
false. Performance is the final probability that the lamp is true, with a task
target of at least $1/2$. This objective is an input to the model.

The thermodynamic comparison uses the complete update. In units $k_BT=1$,
each transition's outward heat is its forward/backward log-ratio. Its work
is the change in the common interaction energy plus this heat. Agreement has
energy zero and disagreement has energy $\log3$. This is the declared
reservoir and work model for the compared policies.

\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
Policy & Lamp success & Total mean heat & Total mean work \\
\hline
Always false & $1/4$ & $(\log3)/4$ & $0$ \\
Copy & $1/2$ & $(\log3)/4$ & $0$ \\
Invert & $1/2$ & $3(\log3)/4$ & $(\log3)/2$ \\
Always true & $3/4$ & $3(\log3)/4$ & $(\log3)/2$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

These are exact expectations proved from the finite transition laws. The
initial mean interaction energy is $(\log3)/2$ and its final value is
$(\log3)/4$ under every policy. Their difference accounts for the heat
released at zero mean work; individual paths can still exchange work.

At a heat budget of $(\log3)/4$, copying is the unique feasible optimum.
It doubles success over the baseline and meets the task target. The baseline
has the same mean heat and work yet misses the target: feasibility alone
does not select successful behaviour. Always choosing true performs better
but exceeds this budget. Copying and inversion even end with the same joint
distribution, while inversion dissipates more. Computing costs from the
endpoint alone would miss that difference.

\subsection{A policy register that adapts}
\label{sec:policy-learning}

Comparing policies does not say how a physical controller changes its policy.
A small learning model makes that next step explicit. Add a separate register
bit: false stands for the baseline lamp policy, true for copying memory. Both
policies fit the task's heat budget. The question is how the probability of
using copy changes, and what changing the register costs.

The model starts with the policies' expected rewards already known. At thermal
scale one, assign the register energy $\log3$ when false and zero when true.
This energy is exactly $4\log3$ times the gap between copy's expected reward
and the selected policy's reward. The objective is therefore built into the
energy landscape. The model concerns adaptation toward that encoded
objective; it does not estimate an unknown reward from experience.

At each learning update the register follows this fixed channel:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
Current register & Next false & Next true \\
\hline
False & $5/8$ & $3/8$ \\
True & $1/8$ & $7/8$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The ratio of the forward and reverse switching probabilities is three.
Consequently, each transition's forward/backward log-ratio equals the energy
lost by the register. That loss is the outward heat in this reservoir model,
and the pathwise first law gives zero external work during relaxation.

Let $p_n$ be the probability that the register holds copy after $n$ updates.
Each update starts from the preceding update's actual output. With $p_0=1/4$,
\[
 p_{n+1}=\frac38+\frac12p_n,
 \qquad p_n=\frac34-\frac12\left(\frac12\right)^n.
\]
The expected reward of the policy it holds is then
\[
 J_n=(1-p_n)\frac14+p_n\frac12
 =\frac7{16}-\frac18\left(\frac12\right)^n.
\]
This expectation increases at every finite update. The distribution converges
to probability $3/4$ of copy, and that distribution is stationary under the
same channel. Its expected reward $7/16$ remains below copy's $1/2$ and below
the task target. Convergence need not mean optimal learning.

The resource accounting uses those same changing probabilities. Update $n$
releases mean heat $(\log3)(1/2)^n/4$, and the total through $N$ updates is
\[
 Q^L_{<N}=\frac{\log3}{2}\left[1-\left(\frac12\right)^N\right].
\]
This equals the decrease in the register's mean energy and stays below
$(\log3)/2$. The finite entropy balances telescope as well. The initial
preparation contains the energy that is released; the model supplies no
continuing power source.

The task has a different clock. $J_n$ evaluates how well the held policy
would perform on the lamp task's prepared initial law, independently of the
register. Both policies have task heat $(\log3)/4$ and zero mean task work,
so every mixture retains those expectations. The learning updates themselves
execute no lamp trials. Preparing either initial law and implementing the
register's readout in an actuator need their own physical accounting. A finite
total for register relaxation cannot pay for arbitrarily many task episodes.
Section~\ref{sec:observational-learning} takes up the separate question of a
register whose values are not supplied in advance.

Two checks delimit the improvement claim. A register holding copy can switch
to the baseline with probability $1/8$, lowering that trajectory's reward.
And the same channel, started with copy probability $7/8$, lowers expected
reward because it relaxes toward $3/4$. The proved improvement depends on
the chosen initial preparation as well as the objective and channel.

\subsection{Learning a task nobody told the register about}
\label{sec:observational-learning}

The model above starts from the answer: the energy landscape is built from
rewards that are already known. The obvious objection is that a learner is
precisely a thing that does not know them. So remove the supply and see what
survives.

Which of two actions pays is an environmental bit. The learner's register bit
is read out directly as the action it executes. The world answers each action
with a noisy outcome: success with probability $3/4$ if the action taken was
the rewarding one, $1/4$ otherwise. The update rule is win-stay,
lose-resample --- keep the register on success, redraw it uniformly on failure.

What makes this a different model, rather than the previous one relabelled, is
where the fences sit. They are in the types. The update has the shape
$\text{observation}\to\text{register}\to\text{register law}$, and the readout
the shape $\text{register}\to\text{action}$. Neither has a slot for the
environmental bit, so neither can consult it, and no expected reward can be
decoded into the action. The reward function is used to score the process and
appears in none of its channels.

Acting, observing and updating compose into a single channel on the joint law
of parameter and register, which is the same elementary bipartite update the
rest of this section uses. At $w=1$ it is

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
Current register & Next $0$ & Next $1$ \\
\hline
$0$ & $5/8$ & $3/8$ \\
$1$ & $1/8$ & $7/8$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent and its mirror image at $w=0$. Every mass of the actual joint law is
available in closed form from the uniform prior: each pair with $r=w$ carries
$3/8-\frac18(1/2)^n$ and each pair with $r\neq w$ carries
$\frac18+\frac18(1/2)^n$. The expected reward of the action \emph{actually
executed} is therefore
\[
 J_n=\frac34-\frac14\left(\frac12\right)^n,
\]
rising strictly from $1/2$ and never reaching certainty.

Two baselines say what did the work. Freeze the register and it stays at
$1/2$ forever. Keep the update rule but make the world uninformative --- success
with probability $1/2$ whatever is done --- and it also stays at $1/2$, and
spends nothing doing so. The improvement is bought by the observations, not by
the update rule. The information those observations carry is conditional on the
action: at a fixed register value the outcome distribution differs between the
two environments, while at the prior the outcome's marginal says nothing at all.

The accounting has a feature the previous model does not. Cumulative heat is
\[
 \sum_{n<N}\langle Q_n\rangle=\frac{\log3}{4}\left[1-\left(\frac12\right)^N\right]
 \leq\frac{\log3}{4},
\]
and because the register's two states are energetically degenerate, all of it is
work supplied from outside. That is not an artefact of choosing a flat energy.
A register energy that made these updates free would force the drive's heat to
be the same at both values of the environmental bit --- and a drive that behaves
identically in the two environments cannot be what tells the register which one
it is in. Learning from experience is paid for in work; relaxation toward an
encoded objective is not. The entropy the learner removes fits inside the same
$(\log3)/4$.

Two more checks delimit the claim. The register leaves the rewarding action
with probability $1/8$ at every update, so expected improvement is not
improvement along every path. And each action is executed exactly half the time
at every horizon: the learner acquires no preference between the actions, only a
correlation with the parameter. What is still outside the model is preparing the
prior, supplying the work, running task episodes beyond the declared updates,
building the readout, and any claim that these variables are cortical ones.

\subsection{Running another episode costs another episode}
\label{sec:continuing-agent}

A learner's register can release only a finite amount of energy while its
decoded policy improves. Running a task again also involves acting on the
world and preparing it for reuse. A continuing finite-agent model makes those
operations explicit on one evolving law.

The state has three bits: an unknown rewarding action, a register that
selects the action, and a task flag. The cycle has three stages. Acting moves
the flag according to the register's action. Learning reads that flag through
a noisy observation and writes the register. Reset returns the flag to a
standard probability distribution for the next episode. The coordinate not
being driven undergoes noisy drift. Each output is the next input; no stage
receives a freshly prepared copy of the task.

The register, observation and update cannot directly consult the unknown
parameter. The actuator's response depends on whether the chosen action is
the rewarding one, so the observation can convey useful information. Reward
scores what happens and enters none of the channels. Starting with three
uniform bits, the register agrees with the unknown rewarding action with
probability $65/128$ after the first complete cycle. Later cycles obey
\[
 a_{m+1}=\frac{65}{128}+\frac{a_m}{128}.
\]
Thus the acquired advantage remains above chance at every completed cycle
of the mathematical protocol. The flag returns to probability $1/4$ after
each reset; the register's changing correlation with the task carries over.

Now give the process an initial work allowance $B$. The remaining allowance
after $N$ stages is $B-W_{<N}$, where $W_{<N}$ is expected cumulative work.
A run fits that allowance only if it stays nonnegative at every earlier
stage too. For one common energy and temperature, the first law and entropy
balance add across the actual sequence:
\[
 \theta[H(p_0)-H(p_N)]\leq Q_{<N}
 =W_{<N}+\langle E\rangle_0-\langle E\rangle_N.
\]
The store and the system's energy loss therefore bound the joint entropy
the run can remove. If each stage costs at least a positive amount, a finite
store funds only finitely many stages.

The bit witness assigns zero energy to every joint state, so heat is paid
entirely by work. Heat is the transition log-ratio at thermal scale one for
each composite stage. The first cycle costs
$(15/32)\log3+(1/16)\log(5/3)$, including reset, and every cycle costs at
least $(3/16)\log3+(1/16)\log(5/3)$. An allowance $4\log3$ covers every
stage of the first cycle and cannot cover 22 complete cycles. This bound
does not locate the exact stage at which the allowance runs out.

The controls explain the roles of observation and preparation. Make the
observation independent of the flag and performance remains at chance,
with no learning-stage heat. Replace reset by idle drift and some learning
survives, but the flag fails to return to its standard law and performance
in the second cycle is lower. The reset has a cost and an effect on the
next action.

The observation is marginalized inside the learning channel, so its local

\subsection{A store you can carry, and one you can only report}
\label{sec:pathwise-store}

The allowance above is a number outside the process: it is compared against
an average of the work, so what it bounds is an average. That is weaker than
it sounds. A store can cover its expected draw at every stage and still be
overdrawn on a run that actually happens.

Make the store part of the state instead. The state now carries a reading
$\beta$, and each transition the process can execute draws $w^-$ from the
store and receives a declared supply $w^+$, with
\[
 \beta(\text{after})=\beta(\text{before})-w^-+w^+ .
\]
The condition to check is that the draw is covered where it is made:
$w^-\leq\beta(\text{before})+w^+$ on every transition of positive probability
out of a state the process reaches. If it holds at every stage, and the
reading starts nonnegative, then \emph{no state the process ever reaches}
carries a negative reading. Nothing here needs every transition to have
positive probability, so a deterministic gate --- the kind
\S\ref{sec:register-bath} uses --- is allowed, and the draw itself may be
random.

Averaging the same identity over the executed paths returns the earlier
bookkeeping: mean reading in, less work drawn, plus work supplied. So the
pathwise statement implies the expected one. The converse fails, and one
witness shows how. A store reading $1$ either idles or spends a unit, with
equal probability; the same from a reading of $0$. Over two stages it draws
$1$ in expectation against an allowance of $1$, so the expected allowance is
never exceeded --- and the path that spends twice, which has probability
$1/4$, ends at $-1$. The allowance was never a battery.

That leaves the question of what keeps a process running. Two conditions
bracket it. If the supply covers each draw as it is made, every horizon is
solvent and cumulative work is unbounded without difficulty. If instead every
executed transition draws at least $c>0$ more than it supplies, then a run
solvent at $N$ stages satisfies $Nc\leq\beta_0$: the process stops. Between
them sits the interesting case. A cycle that spends a unit and is then given
it back satisfies neither condition, is solvent at every horizon, and has
drawn more work by some horizon than any allowance you care to declare. Its
supply is doing the work: remove it and the reading no longer records what
happens to the store at all.

\scope
The generic supply is an input. The next subsection accounts for a finite
source behind it, and \S\ref{sec:sensor-memory} for the measurement's own
register. Preparing the initial resources and every cortical identification
remain distinct tasks.

\subsection{The source belongs in the budget}
\label{sec:finite-supply}

Recharging a buffer moves the resource question to whatever charges it. Put
that source in the state too, with usable energy $R$, and check that each
transfer into the buffer is energy lost by the source:
\[
 R(\text{after})=R(\text{before})-w^+ .
\]
Add this to the buffer's ledger. The transfer $w^+$ cancels, leaving
\[
 (\beta+R)(\text{after})=(\beta+R)(\text{before})-w^- .
\]
If both resources stay nonnegative, the total work drawn cannot exceed their
initial combined energy in expectation. If every executed stage before $N$
draws at least $c>0$ and the initial combined reading is at most $b$, then
$Nc\leq b$. A direct transfer of one unit followed by idling saturates the
bound. Only the stages of that finite run need have positive cost.
Moving the source inside the account does not create more energy.

Energy being available is different from its arriving when requested. The
finite witness has a source, a buffer and an output load, each storing zero
or one unit, and a selector bit that stores no energy. Initially the source
is full, the buffer and load empty and the selector uniform. If the selector
is on, the first gate swaps source and buffer; if it is off, nothing moves.
The second gate swaps buffer and load. Both gates are reversible and conserve
the total energy. Half the runs deliver one unit to the load, and half leave
it in the source. The mean work actually delivered is $1/2$.

Three checks make the resource claim precise. Reporting a unit delivered on
every run violates the buffer's ledger. Preparing the source empty gives zero
delivery with the same gates. And the preceding subsection's charger, which
keeps drawing without bound, cannot have any nonnegative finite source
coordinate whose loss pays for its supply. Its continuing input has to cross
that finite resource boundary.

\scope
This is one uncertain transfer; the selector is prepared once and is carried
through both gates. Subsequent stages idle. The work is energy deposited in
an ideal output load, and no heat bath is introduced. Preparing the source and
selector, controlling the gates, externally refuelling the source and
identifying this hardware with the learning agent's actuator remain separate
modelling tasks.

\subsection{What the measurement leaves behind}
\label{sec:sensor-memory}

In the agent of \S\ref{sec:continuing-agent} the observation is used and
thrown away inside a single channel. A real measurement leaves a mark: a
register that now holds something about the world and will hold it until
something clears it. Clearing it is an operation like any other, and it costs.

Give the state a memory coordinate and run four operations in order. The
readout drives the actuator. A measurement writes the memory from the world
the action moved. The register is updated from the memory --- only from the
memory, which is a fact about the update's type, not a promise about its
behaviour: two worlds leaving the same memory drive the register identically.
Then a clearing operation pushes the memory toward a declared standard law
$\nu$ while the world is prepared for the next episode.

The clearing operation moves three coordinates at once, and its transition
log-ratio splits exactly into their three own. So there is a well-defined
answer to what clearing the memory cost, rather than a leftover. When the
erasure lands on $\nu$ whatever it is given, that answer is
\[
 \langle Q_M\rangle=\theta\,[H(\mu)-H(\nu)]+\theta\,D_{KL}(\mu\|\nu),
\]
where $\mu$ is what the memory held. The first term is Landauer's: the entropy
you removed. The second is the price of using one fixed channel on a law it
was not built for. Gibbs' inequality says the second term is nonnegative, which
recovers the usual bound --- but the identity says more than the bound does.
Set $\mu=\nu$ and the cost is zero: a memory already standard is free to clear.
Make $\mu$ \emph{more} ordered than $\nu$ and the cost is negative: the
operation takes heat out of the reservoir. Clearing dissipates because of what
the memory holds, not because clearing is clearing.

The bit witness pins this down without ever computing the joint law. Every
channel mass is $1/4$ or $3/4$. Whatever the agent has learned, the flag it
leaves is a mixture of the actuator's two masses, so it lies in $[1/4,3/4]$;
the measurement mixes that into the memory and the drift moves it halfway to
uniform, so the memory's mass at each clearing lies in $[7/16,9/16]$. With
$\nu=(3/4,1/4)$ the cost is $[3/4-\mu(0)]\log3$, hence at least
$(3/16)\log3>0$ at every cycle. Learning does not make the measurement free.
In the first cycle the memory is exactly uniform, and the two terms are
$(3/4)\log3-\log2$ and $(1/2)\log(4/3)$, adding to $(1/4)\log3$. The first is
positive for the reason that $16<27$.

Two controls say what the cost is and is not about. Blind the measurement ---
let it record a coin instead of the world --- and the memory's marginal is
unchanged, so the heat is identical. You pay to clear a register that learned
nothing, exactly as much as one that learned something. Replace the clearing
by the symmetric drift and you pay nothing, and the measurement survives into
the next episode; the erasure returns the standard state whatever was written.

The four operations are stages of one protocol, so the cumulative entropy
balance of \S\ref{sec:continuing-agent} charges the whole run including the
clearing. Local detailed balance at the memory's reservoir is an input, as
everywhere here. Gate fabrication and cortical identification remain separate.
Section~\ref{sec:funded-memory} supplies the source these operations draw on;
\S\ref{sec:preparation} accounts for reaching the declared prior.

\subsection{Who pays for the clearing}
\label{sec:funded-memory}

The clearing costs. Nothing so far says where the energy comes from. The store
of \S\ref{sec:pathwise-store} is carried on the trajectory and the source of
\S\ref{sec:finite-supply} is a separate three-bit witness; neither runs on the
agent that does the clearing. Put them on one protocol: let the world the agent
acts on carry a charge as well as a task flag, and let the operation that
prepares the next episode spend a unit of it.

The horizon bound of \S\ref{sec:pathwise-store} does not reach this agent as it
stands, because it asks for a positive net draw at \emph{every} executed
transition and this agent draws at one operation in four. The fix is to let the
cost depend on the stage. A charge paid once in each period of $p$ operations
sums to $mc$ over $m$ periods, however it sits inside the period, so a run that
is solvent through $m$ cycles and starts no higher than $b$ satisfies
\[
 mc\leq b .
\]
A finite source funds finitely many clearings. In the witness the charge is
three units and each clearing spends one, so three cycles are funded, the bound
is attained, and the reading is below empty at the sixteenth operation. The
memory channels are the ones of \S\ref{sec:sensor-memory}, so every clearing
still costs between $(3/16)\log3$ and $(5/16)\log3$: what the source funds is
what that section prices.

Two things the model does not do are worth stating, because both are properties
of the model rather than of this witness. First, the erasure is a channel of the
memory alone --- that was the fence that made its cost well defined --- so it
cannot be conditioned on the charge. Past the third cycle the agent keeps
clearing, keeps costing at least $(3/16)\log3$, and draws nothing. The model
says the run is unfunded, not that it stops. Second, an agent whose channels all
have full support cannot be funded at all: if every transition is possible then
a reading that never rises is squeezed between every pair of states in both
directions, so it is constant and every net draw is zero. The witness's charge
moves deterministically for that reason, not for convenience.

charge, refuelling it from outside, fabricating the gates and any cortical
identification are separate. The preparation model in

\subsection{Who prepares the prior}
\label{sec:preparation}

An agent's prior specifies the law from which its operations start. To account
for reaching it, prepend a preparation channel to the same protocol. Here that
channel lands on one hardware law \emph{whatever it is given}, so the clearing
identity of \S\ref{sec:sensor-memory} also gives its mean heat. Its target must
give every hardware state positive probability.

For a uniform target the mean log-ratio heat is zero. The relative entropy of
any $\mu$ from the uniform law is $\log|S|-H(\mu)$; this cancels the entropy
change in the clearing identity. The result holds for any starting hardware law
and any thermal scale. Interpreting this expression as reservoir heat uses local
detailed balance. It does not price the construction or control of the channel.

The sensor-memory witness starts with a skewed hardware law with full support
and randomizes it to the agent's uniform prior. The starting and target laws
are proved different. Preparation is stage zero, every later law is the agent's
own, and the existing cumulative entropy bound covers the whole run. The choice
of the starting hardware law remains an input.

Two limits follow from what the channel can access. It writes the hardware and
leaves the unknown parameter's marginal unchanged, so it cannot turn a certain
parameter into the uncertainty this agent needs. A channel that reads neither
coordinate produces a product law: it cannot create a correlation between the
parameter and register. Both limits have concrete counterexamples in Lean.

For contrast, preparing the memory's standard state $(3/4,1/4)$ from uniform
has mean heat $(1/4)\log3$ at thermal scale one. For a target biased to
$\varepsilon$, the corresponding expression is
$(1/2-\varepsilon)\log[(1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon]$, with
$0<\varepsilon<1$. Lean proves this family has no finite upper bound. Its exact
endpoint has zero reverse probabilities and fails the positive-target premise.
This result concerns the specified channels; it does not establish the cost of
every implementation of exact preparation. In particular, preparing the sharp
charge of \S\ref{sec:funded-memory} remains a separate problem.

Local detailed balance at the preparing reservoir is an input, as everywhere
here. Funding the preparation from a source, fabricating the gates, preparing a

\subsection{One register's ledger}
\label{sec:register-ledger}

The active chain's fourth node needs an \emph{upper} bound on a controlled
substep's heat. Landauer's principle gives a \emph{lower} bound on an
erasure's heat, which is the opposite direction, and two numbers computed in
unrelated models do not bound one another at all. What closes the gap is a
model in which one register owns both quantities.

A ledger for a register records its own update, the operations it performs on
its environment, and one accounting identity: the heat the register dissipates
is the total those operations deliver to its reservoir,
\[
 Q_{\mathrm{reg}}=\sum_i\langle Q\rangle_i .
\]
Every operation in the ledger has that register as its controller and obeys
local detailed balance at that register's temperature, so the shares and the
total are quantities of one physical system rather than of several.

Call an operation \emph{compressive} when it does not increase the joint
register--environment entropy. The heat bound of \S\ref{sec:agency-heat},
applied to such an operation, gives $\langle Q\rangle_i\ge
k_BT[H(p)-H(p')]\ge0$: a compressive operation cannot draw heat out of the
reservoir. If every operation but one compresses, the remaining share is
therefore at most $Q_{\mathrm{reg}}$. That is the allocation the edge asks
for, derived from the identity and the second law instead of assumed.

The witness is the chain's own one-bit register, at temperature one and with
Landauer heat $\log2$. Its operations drive a lamp bit toward the register's
bit: the lamp agrees with the held register bit with probability $a$, starting
from a law of agreement probability $w$, with interaction energy
$\log[a/(1-a)]$ for disagreement. Such an operation delivers mean heat
$\log[a/(1-a)](a-w)$. At $a=2/3$, $w=4/15$ that is $(2/5)\log2$; at $a=4/5$,
$w=1/2$ it is $(3/5)\log2$. The two make exactly the register's $\log2$, and
the controlled one is the entropy-\emph{increasing} of the pair: its own
second law gives it no bound in either direction.

Neither input can be dropped. The same family at $a=8/9$, $w=1/9$ preserves
the joint entropy exactly, so it is compressive, and still delivers

\subsection{What a register's bath fixes, and what it does not}
\label{sec:register-bath}

\what
The ledger charges a register's operations against the heat that register
dissipates. Write down the bath that receives the heat, and ask which of the
ledger's premises the microscopic dynamics gives you.

Take one register bit and one bath bit. Each of the four maps of the register
--- identity, negation, and the two erasures --- is carried by a permutation
of the \emph{pair} that performs it when the bath is prepared in its ground
state: an injective map moves the register and leaves the bath alone, and an
erasure swaps the register's bit into the bath. Nothing is lost
microscopically; the register's information goes somewhere. Let the bath's
levels be $0$ and $2\log2$, let the register's be degenerate, take the
register uniform and the thermal scale one, and read heat off the paths the
gate actually runs as the bath's mean energy gain. It is $\log2$ for either
erasure and $0$ for either injective map, and those are the values Landauer's
heat equation demands of this bath, whose reachable states the model records.

For any sequence of operations on one law, the bath's own ledger is a first
law and nothing more:
\[
 \sum_{k<N}\langle\Delta E_B\rangle_k=\langle E_B\rangle_N-\langle E_B\rangle_0 ,
\]
with no positivity anywhere and no entropy inequality out of it. A reported
heat ledger differs from that gain by the transfer it fails to count, and the
difference is available as an identity rather than an estimate.

Three things then go wrong for a budget indexed by the register's map. First,
run the erasing gate a second time: the bath is not prepared any more, so the
gate hands the bit back, returns the $\log2$ and restores the law it started
from. The two shares are $\log2$ and $-\log2$ about a total of zero, so a
share is neither nonnegative nor bounded by the total, and an eraser is
reusable only if something prepares its bath again. Second, a different
reversible gate leaves the register exactly where it is and flips the bath:
the same register map as idling, at $2\log2$ instead of $0$. The work
accounts for the difference --- $\log2$ for one erasure, $2\log2$ for that
drive, zero for two erasures --- and the register's map does not determine
any of it. Third, a swap is its own inverse, so forward and reverse
transition probabilities agree on every path the gate runs, and the
log-ratio heat that local detailed balance would assign is zero at every
thermal scale, while the bath gains $\log2$. And a deterministic gate sends one
state to one state, so it is not a strictly positive channel either.

\scope
So positivity, local detailed balance and the accounting identity are physical
inputs of the resource model, not consequences of microscopic reversibility.
This model constructs no ledger and weakens no result that uses one; it says
what such a model has to be given. The numbers are those of the declared
energy gap and preparation. Preparing the bath, supplying the work,
fabricating the gate and identifying this bath with the reservoir the ledger's
operations exchange heat with are all still open, and nothing here concerns
cortex.

\subsection{How local actions install a coupling kernel}
\label{sec:microscopic-coupling}

\what
A scalar density is not a pairwise coupling. To build one, the model has to
say what the hardware is. Suppose it carries finitely many spatial response
modes. Each mode $i$ has a fixed profile $\phi_i(x)$ saying where on the
substrate it responds, and a price $u_i$ saying what it costs to hold open.
A configuration $z$ of the controller and its switches sets the occupancies
$c_i(z)$, and those same numbers fix two things at once:
\[
 K_z(x,y)=\sum_i c_i(z)\,\phi_i(x)\,\phi_i(y),
 \qquad
 U(z)=\sum_i u_i\,c_i(z).
\]
The first is a kernel on pairs of points; the second is the energy stored in
the hardware as configured. A transition $z\to z'$ changes the kernel only
through the modes whose occupancy it changes, and its external work is
$U(z')-U(z)+Q$.

\why
This is what the scalar model leaves out. The kernel the executed step
installs is the average of $K_z$ over the law that step actually leaves, so
its change is the mean of the pathwise changes rather than a function of an
entropy difference. The mean of the transition works is the installation
cost, and the first law splits it into the change in expected stored energy
and the channel's heat: closing a mode is paid for, not free.

The spatial limit is then a separate, spatial argument. Divide the substrate
into finitely many measurable cells and pick one sample point in each. The
reconstruction that uses the sampled weight throughout each \emph{cell pair}
has a cell-mass weighted energy exactly equal to its integral against the
product measure. As the sample error shrinks, a continuous kernel on a
compact substrate gives
\[
 \mathcal{E}_n\longrightarrow\iint K\,d\mu\,d\mu .
\]
The index $n$ counts refinements of space, not executed updates.

One response mode on $[0,1]$ with profile $(4/3)(1+x)$ and unit price,
switched by the same noisy two-bit channel, installs $(8/9)(1+x)(1+y)$ before
and $(4/3)(1+x)(1+y)$ after; the switch closing takes the kernel at the
origin from $0$ to $16/9$. The mean work is $(1+\log3)/4$, of which the unit
stored energy at probability one half is the installation and $(\log3)/4$ is
heat. Uniform cells give first energies $4/3$ and $25/12$ with limit $3$.
Reading a declared predictive system's own joint law on the same hardware
installs $(16/9)(1+x)(1+y)$ with limit $4$, so which law the hardware holds
changes the answer.

\section{What does not follow: the free-energy reading}
\label{sec:fep}

An upper bound is a ceiling, not a target. If an error is at most $10$ and
the ceiling falls to $3$, the error may still be $2$. Only a ceiling tending
to zero forces a nonnegative error to zero. This elementary distinction is the
issue here. In predictive modelling, \emph{variational free energy} is a
quantity used to assess an approximate probabilistic explanation of observations;
it is not automatically the same quantity as physical free energy, the
thermodynamic work budget under specified conditions.

\what
A tempting inference, and one associated with free-energy formulations
\citep{friston2010}: if a system minimizes its dissipation, and dissipation
bounds the divergence between its internal model and the world, then the
divergence must go to zero---the system converges on a true model of its
environment.

\statement
The inference is invalid, and the development says exactly where. The
assumption available is a bound of the form
\[
 D_{KL}(P\|Q) \;\le\; \Delta t\,\sigma ,
\]
carried as the class field \id{StructuralResonance.kl\_bound}. Descending
$\sigma$ does not force $D_{KL}\to0$ unless the right-hand side itself tends to
zero. A driven system settles into a non-equilibrium steady state with
$\sigma>0$, and the bound then permits \emph{any} divergence up to
$\Delta t\,\sigma$. The witness \id{boolDetuned} attains the assumed bound with
$D_{KL}=\sigma=\log 2$: a positive limiting divergence is permitted, so the
limit cannot be read off the bound.

Separately, Gibbs' inequality proves $D_{KL}\ge0$. It does not identify
relative entropy with physical entropy production. Conflating the two is the
other half of the same error.

\scope
The framework replaces the invalid inference with the correct, weaker bound
and declines the conclusion. It does not claim the free-energy conclusion is
false---only that it does not follow from these premises.

\section{The squared-drift functional and its gradient}
\label{sec:sigma}

Consider two sites with systematic phase velocities $1$ and $3$ in chosen
units. Their squared velocities sum to $10$. If an interaction redistributes
the velocities to $2$ and $2$, the sum falls to $8$, even though both sites
still move. Squaring penalizes uneven motion. \emph{Drift} here means the
systematic part of phase change; random jitter is treated separately. An
\emph{objective} is the quantity an update rule tries to lower. Plasticity
means changing the interaction strengths themselves.

The continuum notation below replaces a list of sites by locations $x$ and
$y$. The inner integral adds the influences on one site; the outer integral
totals their squared effect across sites. $d\mu$ specifies the spatial
weighting, so it plays the role of counting or averaging in a finite model.

\what
Fix the phases and ask how fast the system is being pushed around. Each site
$x$ has a drift---the instantaneous velocity its phase is being driven at:
\[
 v(x) \;=\; \omega(x) + \int K(x,y)\,\sin(\theta_y-\theta_x)\,d\mu(y) .
\]
The \emph{squared-drift functional} totals the squares of these:
\[
 \sigma(K) \;=\; \frac1D\int\Big[\omega(x)+\int K(x,y)\sin(\theta_y-\theta_x)\,d\mu(y)\Big]^{2}d\mu(x).
\]
Read as a function of the kernel $K$ with phases held fixed, this is a
\emph{quadratic functional of an affine image of $K$}: the inner integral is a
linear operator applied to $K$, then $\omega$ is added (affine), then the result
is squared and integrated (quadratic).

\why
The framework wants a quantity that a plasticity rule could descend, and wants
to know whether descending it organizes the coupling usefully.

\statement
Functions of this shape have gradients computable by Hilbert-space methods. Let
us unpack those words. A gradient points in the direction of steepest increase;
moving in its negative direction is gradient descent. An inner product
$\langle u,w\rangle$ generalizes the dot product of vectors and measures
their alignment. A Hilbert space is a space of vectors or functions with such
an inner product and no missing limits. An operator is a rule acting on these
objects; linear means it respects addition and scaling, and bounded means it
cannot amplify size without a fixed bound. The adjoint moves an operator
from one side of an inner product to the other, as transposing a matrix does.
Let
$A$ be the bounded linear operator sending a kernel to its drift field, and
$A^{*}$ its adjoint---the operator satisfying
$\langle Au,w\rangle=\langle u,A^{*}w\rangle$ for all $u,w$. Then
\[
 \nabla\sigma \;=\; \frac{2}{D}\,A^{*}\!\left(AK+\omega\right),
\]
exactly as for a finite-dimensional least-squares problem, and the gradient
flow $\dot K = -\nabla\sigma$ decreases $\sigma$ at fixed phases.

A second, more surprising result concerns \emph{symmetric} (reciprocal)
kernels. For symmetric $K$, the integrand $K(x,y)\sin(\theta_y-\theta_x)$ is
antisymmetric under swapping $x$ and $y$, so its double integral vanishes: the
\emph{mean} drift is the same for every phase field. Since the mean is pinned,
minimizing the sum of squares amounts to minimizing the variance, and a
phase-locked field achieves the minimum. Reciprocity is necessary---a one-way
two-site coupling strictly beats its own phase-locked state.

\scope
Three limitations, all stated in the source. First, $\sigma$ is
\emph{not} in general a thermodynamic entropy-production rate. The Lean
identifier is named \id{entropy\_production\_rate}, and the supplement notes
explicitly that the name does not establish the identification: an actual
entropy-production rate requires probability-current information absent from
this calculation. Second, descent is proved at \emph{fixed phases}; joint
noisy phase-and-coupling dynamics is a different problem, and
Section~\ref{sec:plasticity} shows the guarantee does not survive it. Third,
whether biological couplings follow this flow at all is an additional
modelling question.

\section{The model}
\label{sec:kuramoto-walkthrough}

\subsection{Start with one clock hand}

An \emph{oscillator} is something that repeats a cycle. Its phase says where
it is within that cycle: at the peak, halfway to the next peak, and so on.
Picture a clock hand going round a dial. The angle $\theta_i(t)$ labels the
position of oscillator $i$ at time $t$; adding $2\pi$ describes the same
position after one full turn. The phase model keeps this timing coordinate
while leaving out the changing height, or \emph{amplitude}, of an oscillation.
It therefore assumes that a meaningful repeating rhythm has already been
identified. A neural population or recording channel is not automatically a
single oscillator; that identification is part of modelling the data.

An isolated, noiseless hand follows
\[
 \dot\theta_i=\omega_i,\qquad
 \theta_i(t)=\theta_i(0)+\omega_i t.
\]
The first equation says its angular speed is constant; the second adds the
angle travelled to its starting angle. The \emph{natural frequency}
$\omega_i$ is the angular speed it would have without the other oscillators.
An ordinary frequency $f$ in cycles per second corresponds to angular frequency
$\omega=2\pi f$ in radians per second. Equal natural frequencies mean equal
free-running speeds, not necessarily equal starting angles.

\subsection{Let the hands influence one another}

For uniform attractive coupling, the deterministic model is
\[
 \dot\theta_i=\omega_i+
 \underbrace{\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}
 \sin(\theta_j-\theta_i)}_{\text{change in speed caused by the population}}.
\]
\emph{Deterministic} means an initial state and the rule determine the whole
motion. The phase difference $\theta_j-\theta_i$ is the relative angle of
hand $j$ as seen from hand $i$. For a small positive difference, $j$ is ahead,
the sine is positive, and the interaction speeds $i$ up. If $j$ is slightly
behind, the interaction slows $i$ down. Equal phases give zero correction.
The sine makes this rule repeat every full turn, as a rule for angles must.
Exactly opposite hands also give zero sine; the equation permits special
balanced configurations as well as alignment.

$K\ge0$ controls the strength of this attraction. Dividing by $N$ makes the
sum an average: increasing the population does not automatically multiply the
total coupling experienced by each oscillator. In a network, $K_{ij}$ replaces
$K/N$ and specifies the strength from site $j$ to site $i$. Positive entries
favor alignment; negative entries favor separation locally. The threshold
derived later concerns uniform attractive coupling.

A two-hand example makes the feedback explicit. Take identical frequencies,
$N=2$, and relative phase $\delta=\theta_2-\theta_1$. Subtracting their
equations gives $\dot\delta=-K\sin\delta$. A small positive separation
therefore decreases, and a small negative separation increases toward zero.
Their \emph{relative} timing converges even while both hands keep turning.

\subsection{Add unpredictable timing jitter}

\what
The framework uses the noisy, or \emph{stochastic}, version of this rule:
\begin{equation}
 d\theta_i=\Big[\omega_i+\sum_j K_{ij}(t)\sin(\theta_j-\theta_i)\Big]dt
 +\sqrt{2D}\,dW_i ,
 \qquad
 r_N=\Big|\frac1N\sum_i e^{i\theta_i}\Big| .
 \label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}
Here $W_i$ are independent Wiener processes (Brownian motions), so
$\sqrt{2D}\,dW_i$ is white noise of strength set by the \emph{phase diffusion}
$D>0$. The uniform mean-field convention is $K_{ij}=K/N$.

A Wiener process models accumulated random increments. Over a time step
$\Delta t$, its increment has mean zero and variance $\Delta t$; increments
on disjoint time intervals are independent. Multiplication by $\sqrt{2D}$
makes the random phase increment have variance $2D\Delta t$, or standard
deviation $\sqrt{2D\Delta t}$. Variance is mean squared departure from the
mean; standard deviation is its square root, in the original units. ``White
noise'' describes the idealization of uncorrelated driving increments, not
a literal differentiable random velocity.

Read the stochastic differential equation (SDE) as: phase change equals
systematic speed times elapsed time, plus a random increment. Larger $D$
means more phase spreading. Independent $W_i$ means each oscillator gets its
own jitter; a shared random drive would be a different model. With angles
treated as dimensionless, both $K$ and $D$ have inverse-time units, so $K/D$
is a dimensionless comparison of attraction with spreading.

\subsection{Measure how aligned the hands are: the order parameter}

A numerical summary of collective organization is called an \emph{order
parameter}. ``Order'' here refers to alignment, not to a command or to the
order in which events happen. Draw a unit arrow for each phase, add their
horizontal and vertical components, and divide by the number of arrows:
\[
 C_N=\frac1N\sum_i\cos\theta_i,\qquad
 S_N=\frac1N\sum_i\sin\theta_i,\qquad
 r_N=\sqrt{C_N^2+S_N^2}.
\]
The complex notation $z_N=C_N+\mathrm{i}S_N=r_Ne^{\mathrm{i}\psi_N}$ packages
this same average arrow. Its length $r_N$ measures alignment and its direction
$\psi_N$ is the collective phase. When the average arrow is zero, its
direction is undefined. The subscript $N$ reminds us this is a finite sample.

The \emph{order parameter} $r_N$ is the length of the average of the unit
vectors $e^{i\theta_i}$. If all phases coincide the vectors add up and
$r_N=1$; if they are spread uniformly they cancel and $r_N\approx0$. It is the
measure of phase coherence used in the framework's physical test. It contains
no information about what the activity represents. Nor does $r_N=0$ prove an
absence of all organization: two equal groups pointing in opposite directions
also cancel. Random finite samples usually cancel only approximately.

\emph{Phase locking} in general means phase differences stay constant; the
hands maintain fixed offsets. Perfect \emph{in-phase} synchronization is the
special case where all offsets vanish and $r_N=1$. Several formal results here
use ``phase-locked'' for this stronger aligned condition. With independent
noise, sustained statistical coherence need not mean exact locking of each
individual pair.

\subsection{Why an average arrow simplifies the interaction}

For uniform coupling there is an exact finite-population identity:
\[
 \frac1N\sum_j\sin(\theta_j-\theta_i)
 =S_N\cos\theta_i-C_N\sin\theta_i
 =r_N\sin(\psi_N-\theta_i).
\]
It follows by expanding the sine of a difference. Thus each oscillator feels
a pull toward the average direction, with strength $Kr_N$. When the arrows
almost cancel, this pull is small even if individual interactions exist. A
more aligned population produces a stronger collective pull, which can
reinforce alignment. Diffusion opposes this concentration.

This is the \emph{mean field}: the population's aggregate influence. Writing
the finite sum this way is exact. Replacing its fluctuating average with a
deterministic density for an infinite population is an additional limiting
step, explained in Section~\ref{sec:chaos}.

\why
Because it is the simplest dynamics in which a genuine phase transition
between incoherence and coherence occurs as a coupling parameter is raised---
which is precisely the structure the coupling-gated recovery hypothesis needs.

A phase transition is a qualitative change in the possible collective states
as a control parameter crosses a threshold. In this phrase ``phase'' means
a population regime; $\theta_i$ instead means position within an individual
cycle. The sharp stationary transition belongs to the infinite-population
description. Finite populations have fluctuating order on either side.

\scope
One modelling point is made repeatedly and is easy to miss. \emph{Frequency
heterogeneity and phase diffusion are separate parameters.} Classical Kuramoto
analyses often use a spread of natural frequencies $\omega_i$ as the
disordering influence; the framework's stationary theory assumes
\emph{identical} frequencies and gets its disorder from $D$. A frequency-spread
width cannot be substituted for $D$ when applying that theory, and the
simulations are run with diffusion rather than a fixed-frequency-spread surrogate
for exactly this reason.

\section{The Lyapunov potential and gradient descent}
\label{sec:lyapunov}

The next several sections study deterministic motion: set $D=0$, hold the
couplings fixed, and use symmetric interactions $A_{ij}=A_{ji}$. Symmetry here
means the influence is reciprocal. It allows these interactions to come from
one potential landscape. The noisy stationary theory resumes in Part VII.
Random kicks need not move downhill at every instant.

\what
A \emph{Lyapunov function} is a quantity that never increases along a
trajectory; finding one is the standard way to show a dynamical system settles
rather than wanders. For the Kuramoto system at zero natural frequency the
function is
\[
 V(\theta) \;=\; -\tfrac12\sum_{i,j} A_{ij}\cos(\theta_j-\theta_i),
\]
and the dynamics is exactly gradient descent on it: the velocity
$\dot\theta_i$ equals $-\partial V/\partial\theta_i$. Differentiating along a
trajectory gives the identity
\[
 \dot V \;=\; -\sum_i \dot\theta_i^{\,2} \;\le\; 0 ,
\]
so $V$ decreases at a rate equal to the total squared velocity, and stops
decreasing only when the motion stops.

\why
This converts a question about trajectories into a question about a landscape,
and the landscape can be characterized.

\statement
Two directions are proved, for \emph{positive} couplings:
\id{phase\_locked\_minimizes\_potential} (a phase-locked configuration attains
the global minimum) and \id{potential\_min\_implies\_phase\_locked} (any
minimizer is phase-locked). Together: minimizers of $V$ are exactly the
phase-locked states.

\scope
\textbf{Positivity is not a technical convenience.} A network of all-positive
couplings favors all phase arrows aligning, like magnetic moments aligning in
a ferromagnet. Its minimum is unique apart from rotating all phases together.
It does not supply many competing states of almost equal energy (a
\emph{near-degenerate landscape}) of the kind that a memory
argument from geometric frustration would require. The development is careful
about where positivity enters:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item Results that assume it: \id{phase\_locked\_minimizes\_potential},
\id{potential\_min\_iff\_phase\_locked}, the class field
\id{ThermodynamicCover.A\_pos} (hence every conclusion at the gluing node),
and \id{kuramoto\_tendsto\_global\_minimum}, which needs the strictly stronger
uniform bound $0<a\le A_{ij}$.
\item Results that do \emph{not}: existence and uniqueness of trajectories,
the Lyapunov identity itself, monotone decrease of the potential, its
convergence, and $\sum_i\dot\theta_i^2\to0$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
So \emph{that} the motion stops holds for frustrated systems too;
\emph{where} it stops does not. Because positivity is a field of one structure
rather than a hypothesis scattered through the development, it enters the chain
at one node and not before it.

\section{The rotating frame}
\label{sec:rotating}

Imagine filming runners moving around a circular track. A camera turning at
their shared speed makes their positions appear still if they maintain fixed
offsets. Their actual running has not stopped; the observer's coordinate system
has changed. A \emph{rotating frame} is that moving coordinate system.

If every oscillator has natural frequency $\Omega$, define its angle relative
to the turning camera by
\[
 \phi_i(t)=\theta_i(t)-\Omega t.
\]
Then $d\phi_i=d\theta_i-\Omega\,dt$. The common natural-frequency term
cancels, while $\phi_j-\phi_i=\theta_j-\theta_i$, so the coupling term is
unchanged. The additive noise is unchanged too. The resulting noisy equation is
\[
 d\phi_i=\sum_jK_{ij}\sin(\phi_j-\phi_i)\,dt+\sqrt{2D}\,dW_i.
\]
For the average arrow, $z_N$ is multiplied by $e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}$.
Its direction turns, but its length $r_N$ stays the same. Thus phase differences
and coherence can be studied without carrying the common rotation through
every equation.

Subtracting a common speed is distinct from \emph{centering} a snapshot on
its measured mean phase. The first removes $\Omega t$; the second subtracts
$\psi_N(t)$. A noisy finite population's mean direction can wander even in
the frequency-rotating frame. Density comparisons may also center each
snapshot to compare distribution shapes.

\what
For deterministic equations on real, unwrapped phases, nonzero natural
frequencies make the potential acquire a term
$-\sum_i\omega_i\theta_i$, which is linear in the phases and hence
\emph{unbounded below}: the potential has no minimum at all, and a
minimum-attaining argument is not available. The standard repair is to change
to a co-rotating coordinate frame, $\theta_i\mapsto\theta_i-\Omega t$, which
subtracts the common rotation. Unwrapped angles count complete turns rather
than resetting after each lap. A nonzero linear term can keep falling as that
count increases. ``Unbounded below'' means no finite number bounds its values
from below.

\statement
For \emph{identical} natural frequencies $\omega\equiv\Omega$, the reduction is
exact: a trajectory (the path of states through time) maps to one of the
zero-frequency system. The interaction potential depends only on differences,
so its value is unchanged by rotation, and the deterministic descent identity
applies in the reduced frame. Under the convergence hypotheses of
Section~\ref{sec:convergence}, the limit is aligned with $r^2=1$. Phase
differences, phase-locking and the order parameter magnitude are all proved
frame-invariant: changing coordinates preserves these relative observables.

\scope
Eliminating every natural frequency this way requires identical frequencies.
A spread of frequencies leaves residual
detunings $\omega_i-\Omega$ in the reduced system, to which the unboundedness
result still applies, and phase-locking then depends on the threshold---which
this file does not treat.

\lean
\id{Phase4\_RotatingFrame.lean}: \id{kuramoto\_potential\_unbounded\_below},
\id{is\_kuramoto\_trajectory\_rotate}, \id{dynamic\_potential\_descent},
\id{rotating\_frame\_chain}. Run end to end on a two-oscillator system in
\id{Examples}~\S7.

\section{Do the trajectories exist? Picard--Lindel\"of, globally}

An equation is a proposed rule, not yet a guarantee of a well-defined motion.
\emph{Existence} asks whether any path follows it from the prescribed start.
\emph{Uniqueness} asks whether two different paths could share that start.
``Local'' means for a short time interval; ``global'' means for every finite
time. A vector field is a rule assigning a velocity vector to each state;
it need not be a physical electromagnetic field.

\what
Before asking where a differential equation goes, one must know it has a
solution and only one. The Picard--Lindel\"of theorem gives both, locally, when
the vector field is Lipschitz---meaning it does not change faster than
linearly in the state.

\statement
The Kuramoto vector field is \emph{globally} Lipschitz, with constant
$2\sum_{ij}|A_{ij}|$: $\sin$ is $1$-Lipschitz and the index set is finite, so
there is no smallness caveat, and the natural frequencies drop out of the
constant entirely. Hence uniqueness on all of $\R$: two solutions agreeing at
one instant agree forever, in both time directions.

Existence globally in time takes one extra step, because Mathlib's
\id{IsPicardLindelof} caps a local solution's lifetime by the radius of the
ball on which the field's bounds hold divided by its largest velocity bound there,
and carries no global-in-time theorem. The field here is bounded by
$\sum_i|\omega_i|+\sum_{ij}|A_{ij}|$ on the \emph{whole} state space, so the
cap can be defeated by enlarging the ball \emph{after} choosing the window.
Solutions on every window $(t_0-T,\,t_0+T)$ are produced, uniqueness makes the
family coherent on overlaps, and they glue into a solution on all of $\R$.

\id{kuramotoField\_lipschitz}, \id{kuramotoField\_norm\_le},
\id{kuramoto\_exists\_on\_window}, \id{is\_kuramoto\_trajectory\_exists},
and the combined statement \id{kuramoto\_cauchy\_problem}: the trajectory
predicate is inhabited exactly once for each initial state.

\section{Where the trajectories go: Barbalat and \L{}ojasiewicz}
\label{sec:convergence}

This section describes the longest single chain of analysis in the
development, and it is worth following because two of its steps replace
textbook tools that Mathlib does not carry.

\subsection{The motion stops}

If a height only decreases and cannot fall below the valley floor, its value
approaches a limit. This is \emph{monotone convergence}. An integral over all
future time is an \emph{improper integral}, defined by taking the limit of
integrals over longer finite intervals. A finite total squared velocity is a
budget on motion, but by itself still allows isolated bursts.

\what
The potential decreases and is bounded below (at zero frequency), so by
monotone convergence it converges: $V(\theta(t))\to V_\infty$ along
\emph{every} trajectory. Integrating the Lyapunov identity gives the
quantitative form---the energy dissipated over $[0,T]$ is exactly the
potential dropped---so the improper integral
\[
 \int_0^\infty \sum_i\dot\theta_i^{\,2}\,dt
\]
converges, to the total drop.

\statement
Finiteness of that integral is \emph{not} the same as
$\sum_i\dot\theta_i^2\to0$: an integrable function can spike forever on
ever-narrower intervals. The classical bridge is \textbf{Barbalat's lemma}
(roughly: a uniformly continuous function with a convergent integral tends to
zero). Mathlib does not have it, so it is proved here, in the form: \emph{a
non-negative Lipschitz function whose integrals $\int_0^T g$ are bounded above
tends to zero}. The proof idea is geometric---a spike of height $\varepsilon$
drags a whole window of width $\varepsilon/2K$ up with it, and only finitely
many such windows fit under a finite integral.

Applying it needs the dissipation rate to be Lipschitz in time, which is
checked without differentiating it: velocities are bounded, the field is
Lipschitz in the configuration, and the configuration is Lipschitz in time
because its derivative \emph{is} the bounded field. Conclusion
(\id{velocity\_sq\_tendsto\_zero}): along every trajectory of a zero-frequency
system, $\sum_i\dot\theta_i^2\to0$.

\scope
This locates no limit. It says the motion stops, not where.

\subsection{Where it stops}

An \emph{equilibrium} of a deterministic equation is a state with zero
velocity. An \emph{invariant region} is one a trajectory cannot leave after
entering. Compactness is a mathematical form of having no escape to infinity
or missing boundary points; in ordinary finite-dimensional coordinates,
closed bounded regions are compact. These concepts help locate eventual motion,
but they do not mean every equilibrium is a global minimum.

\what
Locating the limit looks like a job for the \emph{LaSalle invariance
principle}, which Mathlib does not carry, and which would want a compact
invariant set that the state space $V\to\R$ does not supply. Neither turns out
to be needed. LaSalle is what one needs when a decreasing functional is
\emph{all} one has; here the flow is a gradient flow whose minimizers are
already characterized (Section~\ref{sec:lyapunov}), and the right tool is a
\emph{\L{}ojasiewicz} (or Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz) estimate: an inequality
bounding how far the functional is above its minimum by how fast it is
currently decreasing.

\statement
The estimate proved is: on the region where every pairwise phase difference
lies within a quarter turn,
\[
 2a^2 W \;\le\; \Big(\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\Big)\sum_i\dot\theta_i^{\,2},
\]
where $W$ is the excess of the potential over its minimum and $a>0$ is a lower
bound on the couplings. The proof uses the two sites carrying the largest and
smallest phase, without assumptions about the curvature of the whole landscape.

Combined with $\dot W=-\sum_i\dot\theta_i^2$ this is the differential
inequality $\dot W\le -cW$ with $c=2a^2/\sum_{ij}A_{ij}$, which integrates
elementarily (``$W(t)e^{ct}$ has non-positive derivative'') to exponential
decay $W(t)\le W(0)e^{-ct}$. The rate transfers to the velocity via
$|\sin d|\le\sqrt{2(1-\cos d)}$, giving $|\dot\theta_i(t)|\le Be^{-ct/2}$,
which is integrable on $(0,\infty)$; since
$\theta_i(t)=\theta_i(0)+\int_0^t\dot\theta_i$, the trajectory itself
converges, and $W\to0$ forces the limit to be phase-locked---a \emph{global}
minimizer with $r^2=1$.

\subsection{The compactness objection, met without an invariant region}

Here the direct route is to control the initial energy. If it is too low for
any pair to reach a quarter-turn separation, energy descent keeps the pair
within that range. Continuity prevents it from jumping across the forbidden
angle. This is what the argument calls \emph{branch-pinning}: keeping a phase
difference in one chosen interval of unwrapped angles.

The quarter-turn region has to be shown forward-invariant, or the estimate
applies only at the initial instant. It is, and the argument is neat: because
all terms of the excess are non-negative, a single pair satisfies
$\tfrac12 a(1-\cos(\theta_j-\theta_i))\le W$, so an initial excess below $a/2$
---which monotone decrease preserves---keeps every pairwise cosine strictly
positive for all time. A phase difference escaping $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ would have
to cross $\pi/2$ in absolute value by the intermediate value theorem, and there
the cosine vanishes. This is branch-pinning by continuity on a connected
half-line, and it replaces a quotient by the phase-shift symmetry.

\scope
The two hypotheses are on the \emph{initial data} and neither can be removed.
A splay state spreads phases evenly around the circle; a twisted state makes
phase advance systematically around a spatial loop. Such balanced
configurations can be equilibria too, so ``every trajectory
reaches the phase-locked state'' is simply false. What is proved is the
arc-condition version. It is witnessed on three sites
(\id{Examples}~\S17), where no closed-form solution exists---the
trajectory is produced by the existence theorem and its limit by the
convergence theorem, the whole numerical content of checking the hypotheses
being $\cos\tfrac12>\tfrac34$.

\section{The open gap: propagation of chaos}
\label{sec:chaos}

A finite simulation keeps a list of $N$ angles. A continuum description keeps
instead a density $\rho(\theta,t)$: integrating it over an arc gives the
fraction or probability of phases in that arc. A histogram with ever more
samples suggests such a density, but interactions can correlate the samples.
To justify a closed equation for one representative oscillator, one must
control those correlations as the population grows.

``Chaos'' here means statistical independence in the limiting construction,
not unpredictability from sensitive deterministic dynamics. Factorization
means the joint probability becomes a product of individual probabilities.
The one-particle marginal is the distribution of one oscillator after the
others have been averaged out. A closed equation determines its evolution
without needing a separate equation for every pair, triple and larger group.

\what
Everything in Part VII is about a \emph{mean-field} description---a single
density over phases, as though each oscillator felt an averaged influence from
an independent population. Justifying that from the finite-$N$ system
\eqref{eq:sde} is the problem of \emph{propagation of chaos}
\citep{sznitman1991}: showing that if oscillators start independent they stay
asymptotically independent as $N\to\infty$, so the joint law factorizes and the
one-particle marginal obeys a closed equation.

\why
Without it, the stationary theory of Part VII and the finite system of
Part VI are two different objects with a suggestive resemblance.

\statement
\textbf{No theorem in the development closes this gap}, and the manuscript says
so plainly. Exchange symmetry alone does not justify factorization,
particularly for an ensemble with an unpinned collective phase.

\scope
Two finite-$N$ controls probe the gap without closing it, and both are
reported as negative or partial:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Subcritical.} As $N$ grows, mean order falls toward the
finite-size floor and absolute circular pair correlation decreases---consistent
with independence. But the matched-sample sliced-Wasserstein distance between
the joint law and the product of its marginals barely moves, exposing that
statistic's finite-sample floor rather than measuring convergence.
\item \textbf{Supercritical.} Mean order stabilizes, but lab-frame pair
correlation stays substantial rather than following the $1/N$ reference. The
reason is structural, not a failure of the simulation: above threshold the
ensemble has an \emph{unpinned collective orientation}, a shared latent
variable that every realization draws once. Unconditional independence and
independence \emph{conditional on that orientation} are different claims, and
only the first is refuted. After centering on each realization's collective
orientation, the $N=1000$ density matches the von Mises target closely.
\end{itemize}
This is a negative result for the \emph{unconditional} protocol and settles
nothing about the conditional mean-field limit.

For intuition, imagine photographs of the same cluster of arrows, each photo
rotated through a randomly chosen angle. Across photos, knowing one arrow's
direction tells you about the others, although the cluster shape is unchanged.
Conditioning means specifying that shared orientation before comparing the
arrows. The sliced-Wasserstein diagnostic above measures differences between
distributions by projecting them onto lines and comparing the projected samples;
its sampling floor is the discrepancy finite samples produce even when the
underlying distributions agree.

\section{The von Mises stationary density}
\label{sec:vonmises}

\subsection{What becomes stationary?}

Imagine watching many clock hands whose speeds are continually perturbed.
Individual hands keep moving, but the fraction in each part of the dial can
remain unchanged. A \emph{stationary density} describes that unchanging
probability pattern. Stationary therefore does not mean every noisy hand
stops. The statement is made in the rotating frame, at fixed $K$ and $D$,
and for a chosen collective orientation $\psi$.

The density integrates to one around the circle. Its collective arrow is
\[
 z=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\rho(\theta)\,d\theta
   =r e^{\mathrm{i}\psi}.
\]
This is the continuous counterpart of averaging $N$ arrows: $r$ has the same
meaning as $r_N$, but refers to a distribution rather than one finite list.

\subsection{What is an ansatz?}

An \emph{ansatz} is a proposed form for a solution, with quantities still to
determine. For example, proposing a straight line $y=mx+b$ leaves its slope
$m$ and height $b$ to be found. One then checks whether that form satisfies the
problem. Calling a form an ansatz does not prove it, and does not mean it is
an arbitrary guess: an equation or symmetry can motivate the choice.

Here the stationary ansatz proposes a circular probability density proportional
to $e^{a\cos(\theta-\psi)}$. The cosine is largest at $\psi$, so positive
$a$ weights nearby angles more heavily. At $a=0$ every angle gets equal
weight; increasing $a$ concentrates the distribution around $\psi$. The
quantity $a$ is called its \emph{concentration}. It is different from $r$:
$a$ specifies the density's shape and is unbounded above, while $r$ is the
length of its average arrow and lies between zero and one.

\what
The \emph{von Mises} distribution is the circular analogue of the Gaussian:
\[
 \rho(\theta)=\frac{e^{a\cos(\theta-\psi)}}{2\pi I_0(a)} .
\]
It is peaked at $\psi$ with \emph{concentration} $a\ge0$; at $a=0$ it is
uniform, and as $a$ grows it tightens. The normalizing constant involves
$I_0$, a \emph{modified Bessel function of the first kind}---the functions that
arise in circular integrals. No prior knowledge of Bessel functions is needed
here: the two used in self-consistency are defined by
\[
 I_0(a)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{a\cos u}\,du,\qquad
 I_1(a)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos u\,e^{a\cos u}\,du.
\]
$2\pi I_0(a)$ is the total unnormalized weight, so dividing by it makes
$\rho$ integrate to one. The ratio $I_1/I_0$ is the average horizontal
component of a phase arrow after centering on $\psi$. The vertical components
cancel by symmetry. A Gaussian is the familiar bell-shaped distribution on
a line; von Mises plays a similar role on a circle.

\subsection{Why this particular density fits the model}

The mean-field equation for a density is the \emph{Fokker--Planck equation}.
It tracks probability moving around the dial, rather than each oscillator's
random path. Writing $b(\theta)=Kr\sin(\psi-\theta)$ for systematic drift,
\[
 \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
 =-\frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta},\qquad
 J=b\rho-D\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\theta}.
\]
$J$ is the \emph{probability current}: net probability crossing an angle per
unit time. Its first term transports probability with the drift; the second
spreads it from denser to less dense regions. If more current enters a small
arc than leaves, its probability increases. This is the content of the first
equation, a conservation law.

For the zero-current stationary solution, choose the mean direction as zero
and set $J=0$. With $b=-Kr\sin\theta$, this gives
\[
 D\rho'=-Kr\sin\theta\,\rho,
 \qquad
 \frac{\rho'}{\rho}=-\frac{Kr}{D}\sin\theta.
\]
The left side is the derivative of $\log\rho$. Since the derivative of
$\cos\theta$ is $-\sin\theta$, integration yields
$\log\rho=(Kr/D)\cos\theta+\text{constant}$. Exponentiating and normalizing
gives the von Mises form with $a=Kr/D$. This elementary calculation explains
the ansatz under the mean-field and zero-current assumptions. A stationary
density in a different driven model can carry nonzero current, so the word
``stationary'' alone does not justify setting $J=0$.

\subsection{Make the population reproduce its own average}

\why
It is the stationary density of the noisy mean-field Kuramoto dynamics: the
shape the phase distribution settles into when a coherent mean field competes
with diffusion. Every distributional prediction the framework makes flows from
it.

\statement
For identical frequencies in the rotating frame, the assumed stationary
density is the one above with
\begin{equation}
 a=\frac{Kr}{D},\qquad r=R(a):=\frac{I_1(a)}{I_0(a)} .
 \label{eq:selfcons}
\end{equation}
The second equation is a \emph{self-consistency} condition and deserves
unpacking. The concentration $a$ is proportional to the coherence $r$, because
a more coherent population exerts a stronger mean field. But the coherence is
itself determined by the density: the average of $e^{i\theta}$ under a von
Mises law at concentration $a$ is exactly $R(a)$. So $r$ must reproduce
itself, and the fixed points of $r\mapsto R(Kr/D)$ are the possible
stationary states. \citet{sakaguchi1988} is the source for the threshold this
yields.

The calculation can be read as a loop: propose $r$, use it to obtain the
pull $Kr$, use attraction versus diffusion to obtain a density, then average
that density's arrows. A \emph{self-consistent} proposal returns the same $r$
you started with. A fixed point of this calculation is a solution of an
algebraic equation. Repeating this calculation numerically is not the same
as evolving the physical oscillators in time.

Following these stationary solutions across choices of $K$ traces a
\emph{stationary branch}: a family of possible settled densities, each at its
own fixed coupling. Section~\ref{sec:fold} explains how to read that graph.

\scope
\textbf{The formal stationary theorems take this density as their starting
point.} The calculation above is an explanatory derivation, not a Lean proof
connecting the SDE to its long-time distribution. The repository script
\id{simulations/fokker\_planck\_von\_mises.py} checks the stationary algebra
symbolically and numerically. It proves neither the passage from the finite
system to a density nor the density's dynamical selection. Stability analysis
asks how small perturbations evolve; spectral methods study the growth or
decay of their different modes. These are two distinct missing links:
Section~\ref{sec:chaos} asks whether the finite system approaches a mean-field
description; the long-time question asks which density that description selects.

\lean
\id{Phase8\_SelfConsistency.lean}. Because Mathlib has no modified Bessel
functions, \id{besselRatio} is written as a ratio of interval integrals.
\id{vonMisesDensity} is proved a probability density on $[-\pi,\pi]$;
\id{circularOrderParameter\_vonMises} proves its order parameter equals $R(a)$
(real, because $\E_a[\sin\theta]=0$ by symmetry); and
\id{fixedPoint\_iff\_selfReproducing} states the equivalence---$r$ solves
$r=R(Kr/D)$ exactly when the density it induces has order parameter $r$.

\section{Why the threshold is \texorpdfstring{$\Kc=2D$}{Kc = 2D}}
\label{sec:threshold}

Near a uniform density, a small concentration gives approximately $R(a)=a/2$.
In the self-consistency loop, a small proposed $r$ therefore returns roughly
$(K/2D)r$. If $K<2D$, that returned value is smaller; if $K>2D$, it is larger.
The critical value $K_c=2D$ is where the initial slope of this response equals
one. The following inequalities turn that local intuition into a statement
about all the stationary solutions. This slope argument alone is not a proof
of how finite noisy trajectories evolve.

\what
The claim is that a positive self-consistent stationary $r$ is impossible at
or below $K=2D$ and, above it, takes exactly one value. Zero also remains a
solution above threshold. The number $2$ is not fitted; it falls out of
a single inequality about the von Mises family.

\statement
Integration by parts is the product rule for derivatives used in reverse
inside an integral. It exchanges a derivative on one factor for a derivative
on the other, plus endpoint terms. Here the key identity is
\[
 \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos\theta\,e^{a\cos\theta}\,d\theta
 \;=\; a\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin^2\!\theta\,e^{a\cos\theta}\,d\theta ,
\]
exact because $\sin(\pm\pi)=0$ kills the boundary term. Dividing by the
normalizer, this factorizes the Bessel ratio:
\[
R(a) \;=\; a\,E(a), \qquad E(a) := \E_a[\sin^2\theta].
\]
The subscript $a$ means average with the von Mises probabilities at that
concentration. Thus $E(a)$ is the average squared vertical component of a
centered phase arrow. A moment is an average of a power of a variable; the
cosine moments below are averages or unnormalized integrals of powers of cosine.
Now everything turns on $E$. Since $\sin^2\le1$ trivially, $R(a)\le a$, which
would put a threshold at $K=D$. The sharper fact is
\[
 E(a) \;\le\; \tfrac12, \qquad\text{strictly, for } a>0,
\]
which gives $R(a)<a/2$ and hence a threshold at $K=2D$.
\textbf{That factor of $1/2$ is exactly what separates a threshold at $D$ from
one at $2D$}, and the development keeps the crude version around to make the
point visible.

Proving $E(a)\le1/2$ amounts to showing the second cosine moment
$I_2(a)\ge0$, done by \emph{folding} the interval $[-\pi,\pi]$ onto
$[0,\pi/4]$ through the two reflections $\theta\mapsto\pi-\theta$ and
$\theta\mapsto\pi/2-\theta$, after which the integrand becomes a product of two
manifestly non-negative factors. Strictness for $a>0$ comes from positivity of
the folded integrand on the interior of that interval.

With $R(a)=aE(a)$ in hand, the two halves of the threshold follow:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[Subcritical ($K\le2D$).] The only $r\ge0$ solving $r=R(Kr/D)$ is $r=0$.
Because $R(a)<a/2$, the map $r\mapsto R(Kr/D)$ lies strictly below
$r\mapsto (K/2D)r \le r$ for $r>0$, so it cannot cross. The threshold case
$K=2D$ falls on the \emph{incoherent} side, so the two regimes exhaust
$K\ge0$ with no gap.
\item[Supercritical ($K>2D$).] There is an $r$ with $0<r\le1$ solving it, and
no series expansion is needed. Writing the condition through $E$: since
$D/K<1/2=E(0)$ and $E$ is continuous, the map overshoots for small $r>0$;
$R(a)\le1$ (being a mean of $\cos\theta$) makes it undershoot at $r=1$; and the
intermediate value theorem supplies the crossing.
\end{description}

\noindent
Three further results complete the picture:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Uniqueness.} At most one positive solution exists---see
Section~\ref{sec:fold}.
\item \textbf{Continuity at threshold.} For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is
$\delta>0$ such that on $(2D,2D+\delta)$ every coherent solution has
$r<\varepsilon$. The branch emerges \emph{continuously} from zero: no
discontinuous jump. (The proof needs no monotonicity of $E$---on a compact
interval bounded away from the origin the extreme value theorem gives a
maximum strictly below $1/2$, and beyond that the crude bound $E(a)\le1/a$
suffices.)
\item \textbf{Strict growth.} $r$ increases strictly with $K$ above threshold.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Together: the non-negative solution set is exactly $\{0\}$ at or below
threshold and $\{0,r\}$ above it. A \emph{bifurcation} is a qualitative change
in the solution structure as a parameter varies. The onset is often drawn as
a pitchfork using a signed amplitude. Here $r$ is a nonnegative length, and
different mean directions $\psi$ give a whole family of rotated coherent
densities with that same length. Uniqueness concerns the positive magnitude.

\scope
Everything here is a statement about a stationary \emph{density}. Of the
coherent branch the development proves existence, uniqueness, continuous
emergence and strict growth---but \textbf{not that it is dynamically
selected}. No theorem in these files mentions a trajectory, so none could.
The order parameter reached is that of a density, not of a trajectory, and no
theorem relates the two---that relation is the mean-field limit of
Section~\ref{sec:chaos}.

There is also a modelling caution about the spatial version. Replacing the
finite sum by an integral against a kernel $K(x,y)$ requires the substrate
measure to be \emph{normalized to total mass one}, and this is load-bearing
rather than cosmetic. The predicate compares the kernel's double average
against $2D$; on a substrate of total mass $m$ that average carries a factor
$m^2$, so without normalization the comparison tracks the substrate's
\emph{size} rather than its coupling and can be satisfied by any kernel at all.
\id{Examples}~\S9 exhibits a system a factor of two below threshold whose
unnormalized double integral nonetheless clears $2D$. Reduction of a
heterogeneous spatial kernel to a scalar $K$ is a further modelling step, and
the threshold does not automatically transfer to every spatial or signed
network.

\section{The fold-and-cross proof of uniqueness}
\label{sec:fold}

\subsection{First, what is a stationary branch?}

Fix $D$. For each chosen $K$, solve the stationary equation and mark its
solutions on a graph with $K$ horizontally and $r$ vertically. Following one
continuous family of solutions as $K$ changes traces a \emph{branch}. The
horizontal line $r=0$ is the incoherent branch. Above $K_c$, a positive
coherent branch emerges and rises. ``Stationary'' describes each solution
at its own fixed coupling; ``branch'' collects these separate solutions.
The horizontal axis is coupling, not elapsed time.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.27\linewidth}p{0.66\linewidth}@{}}
\hline
Object & Question it answers \\
\hline
Stationary solution & What density can remain unchanged at this fixed $K$? \\
Stationary branch & How do those solutions vary across choices of $K$? \\
Stability & Does a small disturbance shrink or grow under the dynamics? \\
Trajectory & What does this initial population actually do through time? \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Finding a branch does not prove stability or say which trajectory approaches
it. A ball exactly balanced on a hilltop illustrates the distinction: the
position is an equilibrium, but a small displacement takes it away.

\subsection{Why there cannot be two positive solutions at the same coupling}

The positive-branch equation can be written $E(a)=D/K$. If $E$ strictly
decreases as $a$ rises, a horizontal line of height $D/K$ can meet it at most
once. That is the geometric meaning of the uniqueness proof.

This is the one proof worth describing in outline, because its structure is
unusual and the supplement devotes a section to it.

\what
Uniqueness of the coherent branch reduces to the \emph{injectivity} of $E$ on
$(0,\infty)$, and what is proved is the stronger statement that $E$ is
\emph{strictly decreasing} on $[0,\infty)$.

\statement
One route is to differentiate. An exponential family weights outcomes by an
exponential of a parameter times a statistic; changing the parameter is called
\emph{tilting}. Covariance measures whether two quantities tend to be above
their means together: $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\E[XY]-\E[X]\E[Y]$.
For this family, differentiation gives
\[
 E'(a) \;=\; -\,\mathrm{Cov}_a\!\left(\cos^2\theta,\ \cos\theta\right),
\]
and one would show that covariance positive. That route was not taken, and it
was the harder one: it costs differentiation under the integral sign, and the
covariance is not sign-definite pointwise---symmetrizing with an independent
copy gives $\tfrac12\E[(X-X')^2(X+X')]$ with $X=\cos\theta$, an integrand
changing sign with $X+X'$.

In the proof below the integrals, both over $[-\pi,\pi]$, are
\[
 Z(a)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{a\cos\theta}\,d\theta,\qquad
 C_2(a)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(2\theta)e^{a\cos\theta}\,d\theta.
\]
$\cosh x=(e^x+e^{-x})/2$ is the hyperbolic cosine. Folding means pairing
reflected angles so their contributions can be added on a smaller interval.
The target is the two-point inequality
$C_2(a)Z(b)<C_2(b)Z(a)$ for $a<b$, and two moves settle it.

\emph{Fold first.} The same two reflections used for $I_2\ge0$ carry both
integrals onto $[0,\pi/2]$, replacing $e^{a\cos\theta}$ by
$W(a,\theta)=2\cosh(a\cos\theta)$ and confining $\cos\theta$ to $[0,1]$. There,
$\cos2x-\cos2y$ and $W(b,x)W(a,y)-W(a,x)W(b,y)$ have the \emph{same sign},
both being governed by $\cos x$ against $\cos y$. This is the same
$X\mapsto -X$ symmetrization the covariance form needed, performed once at the
start rather than inside a double integral. Its entire analytic content is one
identity: $\cosh(ap)\cosh(bq)\le\cosh(bp)\cosh(aq)$ for $0\le a\le b$,
$0\le q\le p$, which follows from
$2\cosh X\cosh Y=\cosh(X{+}Y)+\cosh(X{-}Y)$ and $\cosh$ increasing in
$|\cdot|$.

\emph{Then cross.} Rather than integrating that inequality over a square---which
would need a product measure and Fubini's theorem, which permits exchanging
the order of two integrals under suitable conditions---let $\alpha$ be the mean of $\cos2x$
under $W(a,\cdot)$, and let $x_0\in[0,\pi/2]$ be a point where
$\cos2x_0=\alpha$, supplied by the intermediate value theorem. Evaluating at
$y=x_0$ and integrating in $x$ alone gives the conclusion, because the term
carrying $W(b,x_0)$ vanishes by the choice of $\alpha$. Strictness comes from
positivity of the integrand on a subinterval missing $x_0$.

\emph{Nothing in the proof differentiates anything.} No product measure and no
Fubini appear.

The same crossing argument run with $\cos\theta$ in place of $\sin^2\theta$
needs no fold at all---$R(a)=\E_a[\cos\theta]$ is the mean of the very variable
the family is tilted by---and yields \id{besselRatio\_strictMono}: the Bessel
ratio $R$ is strictly increasing on all of $\R$. Mathlib carries this in no
form.

\statement
The consequences are the uniqueness results
(\id{coherent\_fixed\_point\_unique},
\id{supercritical\_fixed\_point\_existsUnique},
\id{supercritical\_solution\_set}) and strict growth of the branch
(\id{coherent\_branch\_strictMono}), which uses both halves: $E$ decreasing
puts the concentrations of two couplings in the order $a_1<a_2$ through
$E(a_i)=D/K_i$, and $R$ increasing carries that order to $r_1<r_2$.

\scope
Because nothing is differentiated, $E$ and $R$ are obtained as strictly
monotone rather than as smooth. The rate at which the branch leaves zero
therefore needs a different file---the next section.

\section{The exponent \texorpdfstring{$\beta=1/2$}{beta = 1/2}}
\label{sec:exponent}

An exponent describes how a quantity changes when its input is rescaled.
For $r\propto\sqrt{K-K_c}$, making a small coupling excess four times larger
makes $r$ about twice as large. This is the meaning of exponent $1/2$.
It describes the branch close to threshold, not the entire curve up to full
alignment. A critical exponent is an exponent at such a transition.

\what
Near a continuous phase transition, the order parameter typically behaves like
a power of the distance to threshold:
$r\sim(K-\Kc)^{\beta}$. The exponent $\beta$ is a \emph{universal} feature of
the transition---it does not depend on microscopic details, only on the class
the transition belongs to. For mean-field theories $\beta=1/2$, giving a
square-root shape.

\why
This supplies the framework's proposed near-onset shape. A square-root graph
has \emph{infinite initial slope}: its slope becomes arbitrarily large as the
positive coupling excess approaches zero. Its height still starts continuously
at zero. This is a statement about a stationary graph, not an instantaneous
physical jump. Converting it to a recovery-time prediction requires the
additional tracking assumptions in the next section.

\statement
The calculation uses a Taylor expansion: near a point, approximate a smooth
function by a polynomial built from its derivatives there, keeping track of
the remaining error. Differentiating under an integral means exchanging the
order of differentiation and averaging. Dominated convergence justifies such
limit exchanges when one common integrable bound controls the quantities.
These are the tools behind the following steps.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Differentiate under the integral.} Define the cosine moments
$\mathrm{vmMoment}\,n\,a=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos^n\!\theta\,e^{a\cos\theta}$.
Differentiating in $a$ raises the cosine power by one. The interchange of
derivative and integral is justified by dominated convergence, dominated by
the constant $e^{|a|+1}$ on a unit ball around the parameter---this is where
compactness of $[-\pi,\pi]$ and boundedness of $\cos$ do the work. Iterating
gives two continuous derivatives.
\item \textbf{Rewrite $E$ through the moments.}
$E(a)=1-\mathrm{vmMoment}\,2\,a/\mathrm{vmMoment}\,0\,a$, a quotient with
positive denominator, so $E$ inherits two derivatives.
\item \textbf{Evaluate at zero.} The moments at $a=0$ are elementary integrals
of powers of $\cos$: $2\pi,\ 0,\ \pi,\ 0,\ 3\pi/4$. They give
\[
 E(0)=\tfrac12,\qquad E'(0)=0,\qquad E''(0)=-\tfrac18 ,
\]
the first derivative vanishing by the symmetry that kills the odd moments.
\item \textbf{Taylor.} Hence
\[
 E(a) \;=\; \frac12-\frac{a^2}{16}+o(a^2).
\]
The remainder is $o(a^2)$, not $O(a^4)$; the stronger form would need a fourth
derivative and nothing requires it.
\item \textbf{Substitute into the branch equation.} On the coherent branch
$E(Kr/D)=D/K$. Writing $a=Kr/D$ and rearranging gives
\[
 K-2D \;=\; K a^2\!\left(\tfrac18-2q(a)\right)
\]
with $q$ the normalized remainder. Letting $K\to2D$ along \emph{any} family of
positive coherent solutions with $r\to0$ sends $q\to0$ and yields
\begin{equation}
 \frac{r^2}{K-\Kc}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac1D,
 \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
 r\sim\sqrt{\frac{K-\Kc}{D}} .
 \label{eq:onset}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The conclusion applies to any family of positive solutions approaching the
threshold in the stated way. Lean expresses that generality using a
\emph{filter}, a device specifying what counts as ``sufficiently close'' in a
limit. This mathematical filter is unrelated to filtering a recording.
``Eventually'' means after entering that sufficiently close regime. The file
also constructs an actual family, \id{coherentBranch}, and proves it approaches
zero and satisfies the limit. That witness prevents the general statement from
being useful only for a family that does not exist.

\scope
Asymptotics of the solutions of a \emph{stationary} equation. It establishes
no stability, no dynamical selection and no time course. Reading it as a
square-root foot on a recovery curve requires two further assumptions, both
stated in the manuscript as assumptions: adiabatic separation, and a threshold
crossing at non-zero speed.

\section{From exponent to prediction}

Two graphs are involved. The stationary graph puts coupling $K$ on the
horizontal axis. A recovery trace puts time $t$ there. To connect them, one
must know how coupling changes with time and whether the phase distribution
can adjust quickly enough to stay near each stationary solution.

\emph{Relaxation time} is the timescale on which a disturbed distribution
approaches its settled form. \emph{Adiabatic tracking} means the controls
change little during that adjustment, so the actual distribution remains
close to the corresponding stationary branch. Here ``adiabatic'' means slow
tracking; in thermodynamics the same word can instead mean no heat exchange.

\statement
If the coupling $K(t)$ rises through $\Kc$ at time $t_0$ with non-zero speed
$\dot K(t_0)>0$, and the phase distribution \emph{tracks} the coherent
stationary branch, then substituting $K(t)-\Kc\approx\dot K(t_0)(t-t_0)$ into
\eqref{eq:onset} gives
\begin{equation}
 r(t)\;\sim\;\sqrt{\frac{\dot K(t_0)}{D}}\;(t-t_0)^{1/2},
 \qquad t>t_0 .
 \label{eq:recovery}
\end{equation}
The ideal picture is a flat subthreshold interval followed by a continuous
square-root onset.

\scope
Four qualifications, each of which the manuscript raises before a referee
could.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{No saturation follows.} Where the curve goes after onset requires
a specified subsequent coupling trajectory. Monotone coupling alone does not
determine a logistic or any unique sigmoid.
\item \textbf{Latency is not parameter-free.} The onset time is fixed by
$K(t_0)=\Kc$ only if $K(t)$ is \emph{independently} known. When coupling is
inferred from the same coherence trace, the prediction of latency is
circular.
\item \textbf{Critical slowing down.} Near a continuous transition, relaxation
times diverge, so the adiabatic (branch-tracking) assumption is worst exactly
where the prediction is sharpest.
\item \textbf{Finite-size floor.} A finite population carries non-zero
fluctuating order below threshold, of order $1/\sqrt N$. An observable onset
therefore requires a \emph{predeclared} threshold above that floor, and a
finite-rate trajectory need not show an infinite-slope cusp at all.
\end{enumerate}

The stationary theory describes fixed-parameter densities of an idealized
infinite population. Applying it to a changing coupling requires adiabatic
tracking in addition. A cortical population is finite, and its speed of
adjustment must be measured. The simulations probe these differences.

\section{Euler--Maruyama integration}

A simulation approximates continuous motion by short updates. The \emph{time
step} $\Delta t$ is the duration of one update. The Euler method uses the
current systematic velocity to predict the change over that step;
Euler--Maruyama adds the appropriate random increment. The notation
$\xi_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ means draw a Gaussian random number of mean zero
and standard deviation one, independently for each site and each step.

\what
The numerical scheme for stochastic differential equations. The deterministic
Euler step $\theta \mapsto \theta + f(\theta)\,\Delta t$ acquires a noise term
scaling as $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ rather than $\Delta t$---because Brownian
increments over a step of length $\Delta t$ have standard deviation
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$. Applied to \eqref{eq:sde}:
\[
 \theta_i \;\mapsto\; \theta_i + \Big[\omega_i+\sum_j K_{ij}\sin(\theta_j-\theta_i)\Big]\Delta t
 + \sqrt{2D\,\Delta t}\;\xi_i ,\qquad \xi_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1).
\]

For illustration only, set $\Delta t=0.01$, $D=0.5$, and the current
systematic velocity to $2$ in chosen time units. The deterministic increment
is $0.02$ radians; the noise increment has standard deviation $0.1$ radians.
A draw $\xi_i=-1$ would give a total increment $-0.08$ radians. A single step
can oppose the drift. Persistent attraction is a statistical effect over
many steps and oscillators.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item Choose the population size, frequencies, coupling rule, diffusion,
starting phases and duration. Decide whether coupling stays fixed or changes.
\item Compute every site's drift from the current phases, then update all
sites with independent random increments. Recompute the order parameter.
\item Save observations at a declared interval. Early adjustment is the
\emph{transient}; a claimed stationary average needs a specified later window.
\item Repeat with different random-number \emph{seeds}. A seed makes a random
sequence reproducible; each repeated run is a \emph{replica}. Their ensemble
mean averages over runs, whereas a time mean averages within one run.
\item Reduce the time step while keeping the physical duration fixed to check
integration bias. Increase $N$ separately to examine population-size effects.
A parameter \emph{sweep} repeats the experiment over declared parameter values.
\end{enumerate}

Independent uniform arrows have $\E[r_N^2]=1/N$: cross terms cancel in the
expected squared sum, leaving $N$ unit self-terms divided by $N^2$. Their
root-mean-square order is therefore $1/\sqrt N$. This \emph{finite-size floor}
is a fluctuation scale, not a hard lower bound. Correlated near-critical
populations can fluctuate above this independent reference.

\scope
Every simulation reported is a finite run at sampled parameters, with explicit
seeds and saved compact summaries. Equal-duration half-step controls probe
numerical sensitivity near transitions; they do not establish convergence of
the integrator, and no run discharges a hypothesis of the formal composition.

\section{Ramp speed sets the apparent onset exponent}
\label{sec:ramp}

\subsection{What finite ramp speed means}

A \emph{ramp} is a prescribed change in a control parameter over time.
Here it is the coupling strength, not an oscillation's frequency or amplitude.
A linear upward ramp has
\[
 K(t)=K_{\mathrm{start}}+vt,\qquad v=\frac{dK}{dt}>0.
\]
Finite ramp speed means making that change at an actual nonzero rate, giving
the system only finite time to adjust. It is distinct from finite population
size $N$ and from finite numerical time step $\Delta t$.

For a hypothetical dimensional example, increasing $K$ from $1$ to
$3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ over $10$ seconds gives $v=0.2\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}$.
If $D=1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, the static threshold is
$K_c=2\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, crossed after $5$ seconds. That crossing does
not promise that a measured coherence criterion is reached at $5$ seconds.
These numbers illustrate units; they are not a calibration of human recovery.

Suppose a distribution needs a relaxation time $\tau_{\mathrm{relax}}$ to
adjust. During that time the target coupling moves by roughly
$v\tau_{\mathrm{relax}}$. Tracking is plausible only if this is small on
the coupling scale of interest. Near a continuous transition the relaxation
time grows very large---\emph{critical slowing down}---so even a slow ramp
can outrun the response close to threshold.

\subsection{Crossing the threshold and observing escape are different events}

\what
Drive coupling through the threshold and watch when coherence rises beyond a
declared fluctuation criterion. Call the threshold-crossing time $t_0$, and
the first qualifying escape time $t_{\mathrm{escape}}$. For a linear ramp,
the coupling delay is
\[
 \Delta K=K(t_{\mathrm{escape}})-K_c
         =v(t_{\mathrm{escape}}-t_0).
\]
Coupling delay and time delay are different quantities. A faster ramp can
increase the former without increasing the latter. The simulations study
delayed passage through a noisy bifurcation. The Kibble--Zurek idea likewise
concerns the failure of slow tracking near a transition; it does not by itself
determine every escape criterion or fitted exponent in this finite model.

A \emph{leg} is one ramp rate in the sweep. Right-censored means the run ended
before the event was observed; it does not mean escape never occurs.
The fitted $A$ sets the vertical scale, $K_0$ is an estimated horizontal
offset, and $\beta_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is an \emph{effective} exponent over the
chosen window. It need not equal the limiting stationary exponent $\beta$.
A log--log fit takes logarithms of both axes: a power law becomes a line whose
slope is its exponent. The \emph{foot} of a curve means its earliest rising
portion.

\statement
With $N=2000$, $D=1$, $\rampReplicas$ seeded replicas and
$v\in\{10^{-1},10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}\}$, escape defined as the first of three
consecutive samples at $r\ge0.2$ after threshold:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item The fastest leg is right-censored---only $\rampFastEscaped/\rampReplicas$
replicas escape before $K=2.5$.
\item The three slower ramps give mean coupling delays $\rampDelaySlowOne$,
$\rampDelaySlowTwo$ and $\rampDelaySlowThree$, whose log--log fit is
$\Delta K\propto v^{\rampDelayExponent}$---near the predicted exponent $1/2$
at this resolution.
\item At fixed $v=10^{-2}$ the delay grows with population size
($\rampNDelayOne$, $\rampNDelayTwo$, $\rampNDelayThree$ at $N=500$, $2000$,
$8000$). However, dividing the delays by $\sqrt{\log N}$ does not give a
common constant. At the smallest size, fluctuations can already meet the
absolute escape criterion near threshold. The size trend does not establish
the proposed scaling law.
\item \textbf{The apparent onset exponent}, fitted as
$r=A(K-K_0)^{\beta_{\rm eff}}$ over $0.1\le r\le0.4$, runs
$\rampOnsetOne,\ \rampOnsetTwo,\ \rampOnsetThree,\ \rampOnsetFour$ from
fastest to slowest ramp. Applying the same fit to the exact stationary branch
on the same coupling grids gives
$\rampOnsetReferenceOne,\ \rampOnsetReferenceTwo,\
\rampOnsetReferenceThree,\ \rampOnsetReferenceFour$.
Those reference estimates are below $1/2$ because the fitting window extends
beyond the asymptotically small onset region. The two slowest trajectories fall
on either side of their references. At the fastest ramp, the ensemble mean
never reaches the declared window; the returned value is a local slope of a
delayed trajectory, not a measured critical exponent.
\end{itemize}

\par\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Why this matters.}\ 
This is a methodological result with reach beyond the framework. \textbf{The
exponent recovered by fitting a driven transition is a joint function of the
mechanism and the drive rate.} An onset exponent read off a recovery trace
therefore says little about the underlying transition unless the ramp rate is
independently known and the finite-size floor is declared---and that holds for
\emph{any} analysis reading a critical exponent off a driven transition.

The first thing it constrains is the framework's own prediction: the stationary
square-root law cannot be imposed on an arbitrary recovery trace, and its
absence is not by itself evidence against a coupling-gated mechanism. A linear
apparent onset counts against the adiabatic branch interpretation only in a
regime where that approximation is independently justified.

\scope
The $(a,r)$ collapse is more robust than the temporal shape: independently
estimated $(a,r)$ traces stay close to $I_1(a)/I_0(a)$ even where the
trajectory lags the branch, with post-critical RMS deviations
$\rampCollapseOne,\ \rampCollapseTwo,\ \rampCollapseThree,\ \rampCollapseFour$,
none reaching twice the slowest run's floor
($2\mathrm{Dev}_0=\rampCollapseThreshold$). The specified deviation-based
critical ramp speed is therefore \emph{not bracketed} by this sweep. Mapping
these dimensionless rates to human recovery times needs an independently
calibrated phase diffusion and coupling trajectory.

RMS means root mean square: square deviations, average, and take the square
root. A \emph{collapse} here means points from different runs lie near one
common relation after choosing the stated axes; it is not a loss of coherence.
``Not bracketed'' means the sampled rates do not include cases on both sides
of the specified deviation threshold, so the sweep cannot locate its crossing.

\section{Spatial coupling and geometric frustration}

A spatial network specifies who interacts with whom as a function of position.
A \emph{lattice} is a regular grid; periodic boundaries join opposite edges
to remove an outer boundary. An exponential kernel makes influence fall by a
fixed factor over each fixed increase in distance; its decay length sets that
distance scale. Row normalization rescales the inputs to each site to keep
their total fixed as interaction range changes.

In a signed network, positive and negative entries give opposing influences.
\emph{Dale's law} is modelled by requiring a source site's outgoing weights
to share a sign; excitatory and inhibitory sources have positive and negative
signs here. Row balance means each target receives cancelling positive and
negative weight totals, not that the phase-dependent forces always cancel.
An Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi network samples each permitted edge independently with a
fixed probability. A self-edge is a connection from a site to itself.

\what
Two things could break coherence in a spatially extended sheet: coupling that
decays too fast with distance to hold the sheet together, and \emph{frustration}
---signed (excitatory and inhibitory) couplings that cannot all be satisfied at
once, in the way that three mutually antagonistic spins on a triangle cannot.

\statement
\emph{Spatial decay.} On a periodic $128\times128$ sheet spanning 2.0~mm, an
exponential kernel row-normalized to fixed total coupling at every decay
length: with steady $r>0.2$ as the operational criterion, the smallest
interpolated decay length above which every larger sampled length stayed
coherent was $\spatialCriticalDecay$~mm. At the empirical controls $0.1$,
$0.2$ and $0.3$~mm, steady order was $\spatialOrderOne$, $\spatialOrderTwo$ and
$\spatialOrderThree$, with winding-defect density at most
$\spatialDefectMaximum$. So this sweep shows no fragmentation in the empirical
band---which sits inside a plateau where steady order spans only
$\spatialPlateauSpread$ out to the longest range sampled. The boundary itself
is $\spatialCriticalDecayCells$ lattice spacings, below that grid's own
resolution; halving the spacing---a $\spatialRefinedSide\times
\spatialRefinedSide$ sheet over the same 2.0~mm---leaves it at
$\spatialRefinedCriticalDecay$~mm, $\spatialRefinedBoundaryRatio$ times its
coarse value and $\spatialRefinedCriticalDecayCells$ spacings on the finer
sheet. So the operational crossing barely moves under this one refinement---
which is agreement between two interpolated thresholds, not convergence of the
sheet near them: at $\spatialSharedCoherentDecay$~mm, the shortest length from
which both sheets stay coherent, steady order is $\spatialSharedCoarseOrder$ on
the coarse sheet and $\spatialSharedRefinedOrder$ on the finer one.

\emph{Frustration.} A row-balanced Dale-law network (no self-edges,
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi edge probability $0.2$, $80/20$ excitatory/inhibitory
presynaptic fractions, each row's positive and negative weights summing to
$\pm g$) shows that row balance \emph{alone} does not guarantee a floor-level
incoherent baseline: signed coupling can amplify finite-size fluctuations, and
$N\langle r^2\rangle$ stays above the independent-phase value of unity. In a
separately specified weak-synapse regime, adding a uniform per-pair modulatory
term $\epsilon$ (contributing mean-field strength $\Keff=N\epsilon$) produces
an operational coherence crossing at
$\Keff/D\in[\frustrationCouplingMin,\frustrationCouplingMax]$ across seeds.
The $\epsilon$ grid is geometric with ratio $\frustrationGridRatio$, refined
linearly to steps of $\frustrationRefinementStep$ inside the step those values
interpolate across; the refined bracket
$[\frustrationBracketMin,\frustrationBracketMax]$ still contains $\Kc/D=2$.

\scope
Each is one finite parameter regime. Fixing total coupling while changing
range tests \emph{fragmentation}, not whether cortex supplies that total
coupling. The crossing range describes finite-run variability, not a
confidence interval or an infinite-size critical point. And the ratio
$\Keff/D$ is dimensionless---carrying it over to cortex needs the rate
calibration $\gamma$ of Section~\ref{sec:gamma}, which neither sweep supplies. No clinical
mechanism is inferred from any of this.

\section{Squared-drift plasticity does not learn structure}
\label{sec:plasticity}

The question is whether changing connection strengths to reduce motion also
makes the network represent a target pattern. These are separate scores.
Imagine grouping oscillators by natural frequency: a template could reward
strong connections within each group. An update reducing differences in drift
may instead strengthen connections \emph{between} groups to pull unlike
frequencies together. A lower objective can therefore accompany worse
agreement with that template.

Each experimental \emph{arm} is one update rule applied to comparable starting
conditions. A frozen arm keeps the couplings fixed. A random arm moves them in
random directions. A permuted arm relabels which sites receive the proposed
changes. A hollow matrix has zero diagonal; symmetric weights act equally in
both directions. The Frobenius norm is a matrix's size, calculated by summing
the squares of all entries and taking the square root. Holding the sum of
weights fixed does not hold this norm fixed: concentrating weight on fewer
edges increases the sum of squares.

A latent signal is a shared underlying influence in a model; covariance
describes how signals fluctuate together. The template is such a specified
covariance pattern, used to score the result. A percentile against relabelled
templates reports how the chosen template ranks among those alternatives.
It is not the fraction of biological information the model has learned.

\what
It is tempting to read the predictive-dissipation bound as saying that a system
lowering its dissipation thereby comes to mirror the structure of its input.
The bound licenses no such reading: it bounds \emph{non-predictive} memory, and
a memory carrying no information at all satisfies it with room to spare, so
lowering dissipation presses toward \emph{less} representation rather than
toward better-aligned representation. This control exhibits a system in which
the pressure is not merely toward less but away.

\statement
Phases evolve with noise and clustered frequencies (three nearly equal clusters
at $0.5$, $1$, $1.5$ per unit time, with non-zero grand-mean drive), while
symmetric couplings update every 50 fast steps by descending the squared-drift
objective of Section~\ref{sec:sigma}. Couplings stay non-negative, hollow, and
normalized to fixed total resource. The symmetric-edge derivative of
$\tfrac12\sum_i v_i^2$ is $(v_i-v_j)\sin(\theta_j-\theta_i)$, so both endpoints
of an edge contribute. Each seed runs four arms on one phase-noise stream: the
gradient step, a symmetric random step at the Frobenius norm of that arm's own
gradient, that arm's own gradient step delivered to randomly relabelled nodes,
and a frozen kernel. Each arm computes its direction from its own phases and
couplings, so the controls follow the gradient arm's recipe rather than
receiving its step.

The first thing to measure is how much of the objective the update removes.
Diagnostics are sampled at the update cadence, at the instant each update has
just acted, so every objective figure below is a mean of that post-update
readout rather than a time mean over the intervals between updates. Symmetric
coupling cancels from the mean drift, which fixes $\sum_i v_i$ and so puts a
floor of $N\langle\omega\rangle^2/D=\plasticitySigmaFloor$ under the objective:
the update can equalize drift across nodes but never remove it. Across three
seeds:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item The frozen arm sits at $\plasticityFrozenDissipationMin$ to
$\plasticityFrozenDissipationMax$; the gradient arm reaches
$\plasticityDissipationMin$ to $\plasticityDissipationMax$, removing
$\plasticityDescentMin$ to $\plasticityDescentMax$ of the headroom between the
two. \textbf{So the update really does lower the objective where it acts.} The random
arm removes $\plasticityRandomDescentMin$ to $\plasticityRandomDescentMax$,
so this belongs to the gradient direction and not to displacing a kernel at
fixed resource. A step size set the same way is not a matched deformation, though:
isotropic steps cancel across updates, leaving that arm at a kernel norm growth
of $\plasticityRandomNormGrowthMin$ to $\plasticityRandomNormGrowthMax$ where
the gradient arm reaches $\plasticityNormGrowthMin$ to
$\plasticityNormGrowthMax$. The permuted arm closes that gap: a relabelling
moves an arm's own gradient entries onto edges that gradient did not choose, so
the arm deforms the kernel to $\plasticityPermutedNormGrowthMin$ to

$\plasticityPermutedOrderMin$ to $\plasticityPermutedOrderMax$ while
\emph{raising} the objective by $\plasticityPermutedDescentMin$ to
$\plasticityPermutedDescentMax$ of the headroom.
\item Second-half mean order lies between $\plasticityOrderMin$ and
$\plasticityOrderMax$. \textbf{So coherence is maintained throughout.}
\item The ratio of mean within-cluster to mean between-cluster coupling---a
measure unchanged by rescaling the kernel---falls from
$\plasticityRatioInitialMin$--$\plasticityRatioInitialMax$ to
$\plasticityRatioMin$--$\plasticityRatioMax$, while the random arm leaves it at

unity. Against $2000$
node relabellings the final kernel is further from the true template than from a
fraction $\plasticityPercentileMin$ to $\plasticityPercentileMax$ of them,
where the permuted arm reaches $\plasticityPermutedPercentileMin$ to

\item Scored against $2000$ partitions of the same group sizes drawn blind to
frequency, at most a fraction $\plasticityBlindPercentileMax$ give a lower
within-over-between ratio than the frequency partition does, against
$\plasticityFrozenBlindPercentileMin$ to $\plasticityFrozenBlindPercentileMax$

environment carries, rather than against block structure of any kind.}
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Most of the available objective is removed at the sampled instants, coherence
is maintained, and within-cluster coupling falls to a fraction of
between-cluster coupling. \textbf{Coherence is not a proxy for representational
alignment in this regime, and descending a squared-drift functional does not
produce representational learning.}

There is a further question underneath the first bullet, and it is the one an
argument from dissipation to representation actually needs: does the objective
\emph{stay} low between updates? Averaged over complete update intervals rather
than at the update cadence, it does not. A run at this rate and cadence sits at
$\plasticityStudyProductionRatioMin$ and $\plasticityStudyProductionRatioMax$ of
its frozen arm over the two halves of its second half---what the update takes
off at the instant it acts, the interval that follows returns. A bounded study, its grid and criteria declared
before the runs, sweeps $\plasticityStudyCaseCount$ combinations of learning
rate and update interval looking for one that holds a $5\%$ reduction while
keeping coherence and losing alignment. None does; the best manages
$\plasticityStudyImprovementMin\%$ to $\plasticityStudyImprovementMax\%$. So
what this regime exhibits is a coupling update that loses environmental
structure while staying coherent, and not one that sustains a lower objective.
A bounded grid failing to find such a regime is not a proof that none exists.

Nor is the direction of the failure accidental, and this is the part worth
carrying away. Because symmetric coupling conserves the mean drift, minimizing
$\sum_i v_i^2$ can only \emph{equalize} drift across nodes---and equalizing
drift rewards coupling between clusters of \emph{unlike} frequency, which is
the complement of a template rewarding coupling within clusters of \emph{like}
frequency. Objective and template are opposed by construction. That is not a
weakness of the demonstration but its content: nothing about a system's
dissipation fixes which structure its slow variables acquire, and which
functional they follow is what decides it.

\scope
The template is a covariance of shared latent cluster signals, used as a
diagnostic---it is not an input to the update, nor a measured covariance of
the constant frequencies. Two readouts are recorded but do not carry the
result. Frobenius distance to the template grows, but the kernel norm grows by
a factor $\plasticityNormGrowthMin$ to $\plasticityNormGrowthMax$ over the same

of that growth is mass concentrating onto fewer edges rather than misalignment;
this is why the scale-free ratio is the statistic quoted above. The

started: at this sparsity edge-level variance swamps a block-mean contrast. The

which the update is known to converge. Resource constraints and evolving phases remove the
fixed-phase monotonicity guarantee of Section~\ref{sec:sigma}, so this is not a
counterexample to that theorem but a demonstration of its scope. A plateau in
the objective establishes neither stationarity of the adaptive process nor a
thermodynamic entropy-production rate---the latter would require
probability-current information the calculation does not contain. And the
non-zero grand-mean frequency drives the collective coordinate, since symmetric
interactions cancel in its mean drift.

\section{Building a coupling matrix from a field}

\emph{Discrete} means represented by separate sites; \emph{continuum} means
represented at every point of a space. Think of estimating the area under a
curve by rectangles. Subdivide the interval, sample the height in each piece,
multiply by its width, and add. Refining the mesh makes the pieces smaller.
Convergence means the approximation approaches the integral as the largest
piece shrinks, provided the pieces cover the intended region correctly.

A triangulation organizes vertices, edges and higher-dimensional pieces into
a mesh; a face is one of those constituent pieces. Measurable regions are
regions to which the chosen measure assigns a size. Pairwise disjoint regions
do not overlap. Anchoring a sample to its region prevents sampling far away
from the area it is meant to represent. A modulus of continuity bounds how
much a field's value can vary across a small piece.

The physical input here is a stress--energy tensor, an object recording energy,
momentum and stress components relative to directions. A tensor is a rule for
combining directional inputs; a $(0,2)$ tensor takes two vector inputs and
returns a number, linearly in each. Turning it into one nonnegative magnitude
at each location requires a choice, just as one can summarize a matrix by its
trace (sum of diagonal entries) or its norm (size).

\what
The framework wants the coupling matrix $A_{ij}$ to be \emph{derived} from a
continuum physical field rather than posited. The construction: triangulate the
substrate; assign to each edge $(u,v)$ a region of the manifold; and define the
coupling weight as the integral, over that region, of a scalar magnitude read
off the stress--energy tensor. Symmetry and non-negativity of $A$ are then
theorems rather than assumptions.

\statement
The bridge back to the continuum is \emph{mesh refinement}: as the regions
shrink, the discrete energy $\tfrac12\sum_{u,v}\mu(R_{uv})f(x_u)$ should
converge to $\int_S f$.

Stated over the bare class of triangulations, that is \textbf{false}---nothing
forces the regions to cover the manifold, empty regions have diameter zero, and
the everywhere-empty triangulation refutes it. Stated correctly it is a
theorem, quantified over sequences of triangulations whose regions are
measurable, pairwise disjoint across distinct edges, covering, and anchored to
the embedded vertices, with mesh size tending to zero. The fixed-mesh error is
bounded by $\varepsilon\,\mu(S)$ with $\varepsilon$ a modulus of continuity of
the integrand, and the symmetric double sum is proved equal to the midpoint
rule over unordered edges.

Carrying the regularity conditions as a structural field, rather than checking
them case by case, buys three further statements:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \id{weight\_self}: a vertex has no edge to itself, so the coupling matrix
has zero diagonal.
\item \id{face\_of\_weight\_ne\_zero}: a non-zero weight forces $\{u,v\}$ to be
an actual face of the complex---the discretization cannot invent couplings
between vertices the triangulation does not join.
\item \id{total\_weight\_eq\_setIntegral}: $\tfrac12\sum_u\sum_v w(u,v)=\int_S|T|$
---total coupling strength is exactly the scalar stress--energy of the region
covered. Nothing counted twice (disjointness), nothing dropped (covering).
\end{itemize}
That last identity is the precise sense in which the coupling matrix is derived
from the continuum rather than posited.

\scope
Three limits. (i) The theorem gives \emph{convergence}, not the $O(1/N^2)$ rate
the numerics report. (ii) One field is deliberately left free and marked as a
modelling choice: \id{scalar\_magnitude}, the scalar read off the stress--energy
tensor, constrained only to be non-negative. A $(0,2)$ tensor has no canonical
scalar---the trace, an observer's energy density and the tensor norm are
different functions, and the structure carries neither a metric nor a frame---so
imposing one would pin a choice rather than derive it. That this is a genuine
physical input is stated as a \emph{theorem}: two instances over the same
triangulation, tensor and regions, differing only in that field, satisfy the
partition identity while every edge weight differs
(\id{gridThermo\_magnitude\_matters}). (iii) The witness is one-dimensional
with a constant stress--energy tensor, so it establishes satisfiability, not a
physically interesting discretization.

\section{The negative result: coarse-graining produces no kernel}
\label{sec:nokernel}

Knowing the total weight of all connections is different from knowing the
weight of each connection. Similarly, a single limiting energy does not
determine a function of two locations. \emph{Coarse-graining} means replacing
fine detail with summaries on larger regions. It can preserve a total while
discarding which pairs contributed to it. The product space $M\times M$
consists of pairs of locations; that is where a two-site kernel belongs.

\what
It would be natural to read the mesh-refinement theorem as constructing the
continuum kernel $K(x,y)$ that the rest of the framework uses. It does not,
and the development proves this rather than noting it.

\statement
Three obstructions, all recorded as results:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \id{mesh\_refinement\_convergence} converges a \emph{scalar} coupling
energy. It produces no kernel and no field dynamics.
\item The discrete side has no product structure to pass to a limit:
\id{edge\_region} is a subset of $M$, not of $M\times M$, so there is nothing
kernel-shaped there to converge.
\item The obvious repair---a kernel supported on the vertices---does carry the
weights, and yet contributes \emph{exactly zero} to the continuum coupling
energy on an atomless substrate.
\end{enumerate}
Point (3) needs a word on \emph{atomless} measures. A measure is atomless when
no single point carries positive mass---Lebesgue measure on an interval is the
example. A kernel supported on finitely many points is then integrated over a
set of measure zero in each argument, so its double integral against
$\mu\otimes\mu$ vanishes identically, for every phase field and every choice of
weights.

\scope
The manuscript is scrupulous about the direction this cuts. It is
the continuum/discrete comparison of Part XIV applied to the framework's own
coarse-graining step---the same argument that would separate a finitely-sited
architecture from a continuum one also blocks the framework's own route from
mesh to kernel.

All three obstructions are to \emph{this} architecture. The construction in
\S\ref{sec:microscopic-coupling} evades none of them and answers each
separately: its kernel comes from declared hardware rather than from a scalar
limit, its discrete data are cell \emph{pairs} and so are product-shaped, and
its cells carry positive mass, so nothing is sited on a null set. What that
construction still does not supply is the hardware. The continuum kernel
remains a \emph{modelling input}, and its identification with the brain's
endogenous electromagnetic field is a further empirical commitment. E45 or
E45Active (the thermodynamic bound $\to$ a continuum energy limit) and E56
(continuum $\to$ field) are where those two steps live.

\section{What a sheaf is}
\label{sec:sheaf}

\subsection{Assemble two overlapping maps}

Imagine a system with sites $A,B,C$. One local description covers $A,B$ and
assigns masses $0.2,0.3$. A second covers $B,C$ and assigns masses $0.3,0.5$.
They agree at their shared site $B$, so they assemble into the global profile
$(0.2,0.3,0.5)$. If the second instead assigns $0.4$ to $B$, no single profile
can restrict to both. Agreement is required only on the overlap.

\emph{Local} refers to a region; \emph{global} to the whole chosen space.
A section is an allowed description over a region. Restriction keeps the part
of that description belonging to a smaller region. A cover is a collection
of regions whose union is the whole space. ``Open'' specifies the regions the
topology permits as neighbourhoods; in an ordinary plane an open region
includes a small disk around each of its points. On a discrete space every
subset is open, so the three-site example needs no geometric subtlety.

For spatial measures, restriction keeps the existing mass on the smaller
region; it does not renormalize it to one. Thus the local masses above need
not individually sum to one, even when the global profile does. Gluing arbitrary
normalized probability distributions is not automatic: the permitted local
objects and restriction maps must actually have the stated gluing property.

\subsection{The vocabulary for that assembly rule}

\what
A \emph{presheaf} assigns data to each open region of a space, together with
\emph{restriction maps} that cut data down to smaller regions. If
$\mathcal{F}(U)$ is the set of ``local descriptions over $U$'' and
$V\subseteq U$, there is a map $\mathcal{F}(U)\to\mathcal{F}(V)$ discarding
what happens outside $V$. The formal construction uses spatial measures:
weights assigned to locations in each patch. Interpreting those weights, or
other suitable local data, as descriptions of represented content is a further
modelling step.

A presheaf is a \emph{sheaf} when local data that agree on overlaps assemble
uniquely into global data. Formally: given an open cover $\{U_i\}$ of $X$ and
sections $P_i\in\mathcal{F}(U_i)$ such that $P_i$ and $P_j$ have the
\emph{same restriction} to every intersection $U_i\cap U_j$, there is exactly
one global section $P_X\in\mathcal{F}(X)$ restricting to each $P_i$. This is
the \emph{gluing axiom}, and it is the whole reason sheaves appear here.

The auxiliary notion is a \emph{germ}: what a local description says near one
point, ignoring differences farther away. Two descriptions have the same germ
if they agree on some smaller neighbourhood of that point. The \emph{stalk}
collects all possible germs there. Formally it is a \emph{colimit}: one object
assembled from a system of local descriptions by identifying the descriptions
that become equivalent on smaller neighbourhoods.

\why
Because synchronization alone does not give unity, and the framework needs a
mechanism that assembles local states into \emph{one} state. Sheaf gluing is
that mechanism, and it delivers uniqueness for free.

\statement

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Theorem (Sheaf-theoretic unity).} Given the specified sheaf $\mathcal{F}$
of spatial measures over a substrate $X$, if local states
$P_U\in\mathcal{F}(U)$ agree on all intersections $U\cap V$, they glue
uniquely into a global section $P_X\in\mathcal{F}(X)$.
\end{quote}

\noindent
Both existence and uniqueness are derived from the local data. The
construction uses local data only---no global state is supplied as a premise---
and the statement that every local section is the restriction of the glued one
is a \emph{theorem} about the object produced, not a hypothesis.

\section{Compatibility is not phase agreement}
\label{sec:compat}

This is the conceptual hinge of the whole framework, so it is worth stating
slowly.

\what
Take two cortical regions whose receptive territories overlap. Each carries a
probability distribution over what occupies the shared part. Their oscillators
can be locked to the \emph{same phase} at every instant while one region's
distribution places an object left of fixation and the other places it right.
The phases agree; the descriptions do not; and \emph{no single distribution
over the shared territory restricts to both}.

\why
A theory that identifies unity with synchrony cannot mark that difference, and
so cannot separate a brain whose regions oscillate together from one whose
regions are about the same world. If the unity of experience is a matter of
local contents being about one world rather than merely co-timed, then
compatibility, not coherence, is the property to look for---with coherence
entering as the physical condition under which compatibility can be
\emph{maintained}, not as a substitute for it.

\statement
The development separates two inputs to the gluing theorem, and only one of
them is discharged by dynamics.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[Thermodynamic equilibrium (discharged).] The phase field sits at the
minimum of the coupling potential. This \emph{is} supplied by dynamics on a
class of initial data---the convergence theorem of
Section~\ref{sec:convergence} builds the required structure, and the witness is
a three-patch cover whose phase field is the limit of a trajectory started
\emph{unsynchronized}.
\item[Overlap agreement (not discharged).] Patches at a common phase agree
where they overlap. \textbf{No dynamics in the development derives this.} It is
a class field of the cover the theorem is handed, so it is assumed alongside
the eight edges rather than carried by one of them.
\end{description}

\noindent
Overlap agreement has a concrete pointwise reading: on a discrete substrate,
two patch sections have the same restriction to their overlap exactly when they
assign the same mass to every site they share
(\id{restrict\_eq\_iff\_densityOn\_eqOn}). And it genuinely excludes data the
rest of the structure admits---the development carries a \textbf{counterexample}
rather than merely noting the possibility:

\begin{quote}
A two-patch cover of a three-site substrate, all patches at \emph{one common
phase}, whose local density profiles differ at the shared site. It satisfies
every other requirement of the gluing theorem and fails compatibility alone
(\id{overlap\_agreement\_fails}).
\end{quote}

\noindent
That object is what a prose table of assumptions cannot carry. It is also the
target the compatibility observable of Part XVIII would have to separate from its
compatible counterpart.

\scope
The theorem does \emph{not} require patches to agree away from their
overlaps---the three-patch witness carries three pairwise-different profiles.
And the whole construction is a formalized gluing \emph{mechanism} under an
explicit compatibility postulate, not a derivation of compatibility from
synchronization. Neither field continuity nor synchronization alone guarantees
a globally compatible representation.

\subsection{Agreement to within a tolerance}

A biological description will not agree exactly. So ask what survives if the
patches agree only to within some $\varepsilon$: at every shared site, the two
profiles differ by at most $\varepsilon$ in mass.

\what
A \emph{partition of unity} subordinate to the cover is a set of weights
$w_i(x)\geq0$, each vanishing off patch $i$, with $\sum_i w_i(x)=1$ at every
site. It averages the family into a single profile,
\[
(\mathrm{select}\;w\,s)(x)\;=\;\sum_i w_i(x)\,s_i(x),
\]
which is a weighted vote among the patches that can see the site $x$.

\statement
Three facts, in the uniform distance (the largest mass difference at any site).
The selected profile is within $\varepsilon$ of each patch on that patch. Two
partitions subordinate to the same cover select profiles at most $\varepsilon$
apart --- and that constant does not grow with how many patches cover a site,
because the weights sum to one rather than to the number of patches voting. At
$\varepsilon=0$ the selection is the glued state, so the exact case is
recovered and nothing is given up there.

The three-site substrate makes the second fact concrete. Take the three
two-site patches, each blind to one site; the common profile is $(2,1,3)$, and
let patch $0$ report $\varepsilon$ more mass at each of the two sites it sees.
Assign every site to the lowest-indexed patch containing it and the selection
is $(2,\,1+\varepsilon,\,3+\varepsilon)$; assign every site to the highest-indexed
patch containing it and the selection is $(2,1,3)$. Those two selections are
exactly $\varepsilon$ apart, so the bound is attained and cannot be improved.

\scope
The tolerance costs existence. For every positive
$\varepsilon$ that family has no global profile at all: patches $0$ and $1$
both see the right-hand site and report $3+\varepsilon$ and $3$ there. So
selection is a \emph{choice} among states rather than an extension of the data,
and a different choice of weights gives a different unified state. Uniqueness
is replaced by a selection rule with a diameter, which is a weaker thing to
have.

Two further cautions. The $\varepsilon$ must hold on \emph{every} pair, not on
a spanning set of overlaps: put a $2\varepsilon$ discrepancy at the middle
site, which one patch cannot see, and the pairs $(0,1)$ and $(1,2)$ agree
exactly while the two selections are $2\varepsilon$ apart. And a readout that
is $L$-Lipschitz in the profile moves by at most $L\varepsilon$ between
selections --- total mass, with $L=3$ here, actually moves by $2\varepsilon$.
Reading the selected state as a conscious content at resolution $\delta$ would
require exhibiting the decoder and arguing $L\varepsilon\leq\delta$. Nothing in
this construction produces the approximate agreement, picks the cover or picks
the weights.

\section{Where uniqueness comes from}

Return to the three-site example. Once both compatible local profiles are
given, every global entry is fixed. That is uniqueness \emph{for those inputs}.
It does not choose the sites, decide which measurements define the profiles,
or select this cover over another candidate cover. A theorem can settle the
assembly question without settling which biological system to assemble.

\what
Accounts of conscious unity need something that picks \emph{one} experience
from among the candidates a system could support. Integrated information theory
postulates an \emph{exclusion} principle for this purpose \citep{tononi2016}.

\statement
Here uniqueness arrives twice as a \emph{consequence} rather than a postulate:
given a cover whose local sections agree, the sheaf condition gives exactly one
global section; and given a contracting self-prediction map, Banach's theorem
(Part XIII) gives exactly one fixed point.

\scope
\textbf{Uniqueness is relative to a cover and a map.} Choosing the regions,
overlaps and encoding region remains part of identifying a candidate subject.
E78 and E89 connect supplied objects; they do not select those objects from
neural data. The theorems settle extension and fixed-point uniqueness within a
construction, leaving selection between constructions to a biological model.

\section{Twisted gluing, and why it fails to add content}

Two maps can use different compass zeros while describing the same directions.
If one map is rotated by an angle, their overlap disagrees by a constant
offset. Adjusting each map's zero may remove all such differences. ``Twisted''
gluing asks when this kind of coordinate adjustment makes local data agree.

For three patches with absolute phase choices $\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3$, the
offsets around their loop add to
\[
 (\phi_1-\phi_2)+(\phi_2-\phi_3)+(\phi_3-\phi_1)=0.
\]
Every term cancels. Calling these offsets a \emph{coboundary} says they come
from differences of choices on individual patches, so changing those choices
removes them. An arbitrary collection of pairwise offsets need not have that
property.

\what
A natural weakening of exact overlap agreement: allow local states to differ by
a \emph{phase} on each overlap, and ask when the family still glues after one
rotation per patch. In sheaf-theoretic language this is asking whether the
family of offsets is a \emph{coboundary}---whether the offset on
$U_i\cap U_j$ can be written as $\phi_i-\phi_j$ for some per-patch phases.

\statement
Both directions are proved: coboundary offsets glue after one rotation per
patch, and conversely, when the phase action is free on overlaps, a family that
glues up to phase has coboundary offsets.

A phase action is a rule for rotating the local data. It is free if only
the zero rotation leaves the relevant overlap data unchanged. A quotient groups
objects treated as equivalent. Cochains are lists of values assigned to
patches or overlaps; \v{C}ech cohomology organizes which such overlap data
cannot be removed by making local choices.

\textbf{And that is precisely why the construction adds nothing here.} Any
Kuramoto configuration---including a twisted or splay state---supplies absolute
patch phases $\phi_i$, so its offsets $\phi_i-\phi_j$ are \emph{automatically}
a coboundary, and the twisted family always glues. Phase frustration
generates no obstruction by this route.

\scope
The formal quotient is deliberately \emph{not} called \v{C}ech cohomology,
because its total cochains still assign irrelevant values to patch pairs with
empty overlap. Naming it after the real thing would overstate what is built.

\section{Phase singularities and winding numbers}

An angle says where a hand points; an \emph{unwrapped} angle also counts how
many turns it has made. Choosing that real-number version continuously is
called a \emph{lift}. Walking round a loop while the phase makes one full turn
brings the pointing direction back to its start, but increases the unwrapped
angle by $2\pi$. The winding number records that net turn count, with its
sign recording direction.

For a signal pictured as an arrow, amplitude is arrow length and phase is its
direction. A zero-length arrow has no direction. This is why a point of zero
amplitude can be a phase singularity: the amplitude is defined but the phase
is not. A spiral is one possible pattern around it; not every amplitude zero
has nonzero winding. The analytic-signal construction used to extract such
arrows from recordings is explained in Part XVI.

\what
A narrower and genuinely positive answer comes from the data. The
\emph{analytic signal} $z=Ae^{i\phi}$ assigns an amplitude and a phase to a
recording at each point. Around a point where the amplitude vanishes---a
\emph{phase singularity}, the centre of a spiral or rotating wave---the
circle-valued phase need not admit a single real-valued \emph{lift}. Local
lifts exist on small patches, and on overlaps they differ by integer multiples
of $2\pi$. Summing those integer transitions around a closed loop of electrodes
gives the \emph{winding number}, an integer that counts how many times the
phase wraps.

This is measured, not stipulated: Townsend and colleagues (2015) identify phase
singularities in multielectrode primate LFP recordings by an integer winding
number around electrode loops, with the associated complex waves modulating
spiking; Xu and colleagues (2023) find task-dependent spiral centres and
rotation directions in human fMRI phase maps.

\statement
The formalization treats the finite electrode loop: \id{loopWinding} sums its
integer transitions, \id{loopWinding\_eq\_zero\_of\_hasGlobalLift} proves that
transitions arising from a single global lift telescope to zero---so non-zero
winding \emph{obstructs} a global lift---and a winding-one witness on three
electrodes shows the obstruction is realized.

The following notation spells out where phases can be measured. The
\emph{punctured domain} is the surface with singularities removed. The
\emph{nerve} of a cover records which patches overlap: a node for each patch,
an edge for each pairwise overlap, and higher pieces for shared multiple
overlaps. Its loops describe closed routes through patches.
The cover-level specification is explicit: cortical surface $M$, analytic

\section{Banach's fixed-point theorem}

Start with the update rule $f(x)=(x+2)/2$. From $0$, repeated application gives
$1,1.5,1.75,\ldots$, approaching $2$. Starting somewhere else also approaches
$2$, because each update halves the distance to it. The number $2$ is a
\emph{fixed point}: $f(2)=2$. Iteration means applying the same rule repeatedly.
It need not represent physical time unless a model explicitly gives it that
meaning.

A \emph{Cauchy sequence} has terms that become arbitrarily close to one
another sufficiently far along. Completeness says their limiting point belongs
to the space. If we removed $2$ from the real line, the example sequence would
approach a missing point; that space would not be complete. A distance must
be nonnegative, symmetric, zero exactly for equal states, and obey the triangle
inequality: going via a third point cannot shorten the direct distance.

\what
A \emph{metric space} is a set with a notion of distance. It is
\emph{complete} when every Cauchy sequence converges---no limits are missing.
A map $f$ is a \emph{contraction} with rate $\lambda<1$ if it shrinks all
distances by at least that factor:
$d(f(x),f(y))\le\lambda\,d(x,y)$.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Banach's fixed-point theorem} \citep{banach1922}. A contraction on a
non-empty complete metric space has exactly one fixed point, and iterating the
map from any starting point converges to it.
\end{quote}

\noindent
The proof is three lines: iterates form a Cauchy sequence because distances
shrink geometrically; completeness supplies a limit; continuity makes the limit
a fixed point; and two fixed points would have to be at distance
$\le\lambda\,d$ from each other, forcing $d=0$.

\why
The framework proposes that a minimal self involves an accurate internal
representation of the state whose contents form the episode. Its map encodes
the global state locally and reconstructs it through a readout. If the map
contracts, Banach gives exactly one fixed point. Identifying that point with
the state glued on the chosen cover is an additional hypothesis; interpreting
the reconstruction as a minimal self is a philosophical commitment. The map
does not describe a temporal prediction without a further dynamical model.

\statement

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Theorem (Conditional auto-resonance).} On a nonempty complete metric space of
global sections, a self-prediction map with Lipschitz rate
\[
 \lambda \;=\; e^{-(K-\Kc)\tau/2} \;<\; 1
\]
has a unique fixed point.
\end{quote}

\noindent
Linearizing means approximating the dynamics of a small disturbance by its
first-order terms. In the near-threshold amplitude approximation
$\dot r=\tfrac12(K-2D)r-cr^3$, $c>0$ sets the saturation strength: the cubic
term slows growth at larger amplitudes. The rate in the exponent, $(K-\Kc)/2$,
is the growth rate of the incoherent
state's instability, so supercriticality $K>\Kc$ makes the factor less than one.
Relaxation about the \emph{coherent} branch is twice as fast---linearizing
$\dot r = \tfrac12(K-2D)r - cr^3$ about its stable fixed point gives
$(K-\Kc)$---so $\lambda$ is the larger of the two factors and the Lipschitz
obligation stated with it is the weaker. Both are below one on exactly the
half-plane $K>\Kc$.
Here $\tau>0$ is a specified interval, and a Lipschitz bound says output
distances are at most $\lambda$ times input distances. Below threshold this
particular formula gives $\lambda\ge1$, so it does not supply the strict
factor below one required by the contraction theorem. Other maps can still
be contractions at other rates; what fails is this rate's contraction
condition, not the existence of every possible contraction.

\scope
\textbf{Coherence supplies $K>\Kc$; it does not supply the Lipschitz law.} The
bound is an obligation on each instance, not a consequence of field dynamics,
and no field dynamics in the development produces the read-out map. E89 carries
both the Lipschitz law and the identification of the glued section with the
fixed point---Banach supplies uniqueness, not that identification.

\subsection{Approximate reconstruction}

A state need not reconstruct itself exactly to be close to the fixed point.
Suppose its reconstruction error is at most $\varepsilon$, measured with the
same metric as the contraction. Since $F(s_*)=s_*$,
\[
d(s,s_*)\leq d(s,F(s))+d(F(s),F(s_*))
\leq\varepsilon+\lambda d(s,s_*).
\]
Moving the last term to the left gives
\[
d(s,s_*)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\lambda}.
\]
The exact fixed point remains unique; several approximately reconstructed
states may lie near it. For the three-site witness, the silent profile
$(0,0,0)$ reconstructs as $(0,1,0)$, while the fixed profile is $(0,2,0)$.
The reconstruction error is one and the distance to the fixed point is two.
With $\lambda=1/2$, the bound is attained exactly.

The denominator measures how strongly successive predictions contract.
At the stipulated rate $\lambda=e^{-(K-2D)\tau/2}$, the proved bounds are
\[
\frac{2\varepsilon}{(K-2D)\tau}
\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\lambda}
\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{(K-2D)\tau}+\varepsilon
\qquad(K>2D,\ \tau>0,\ \varepsilon\geq0).
\]
For fixed positive error tolerance and interval, the radius therefore grows
without bound as coupling approaches the threshold from above. The estimate
loses precision; it does not say that an actual state moves arbitrarily far
away. At zero reconstruction error, its distance from the fixed point is zero
at every supercritical coupling.

If the state is also displaced by at most $\delta$, its new reconstruction
error is at most $\varepsilon+(1+\lambda)\delta$, giving distance at most
$[\varepsilon+(1+\lambda)\delta]/(1-\lambda)$. The errors add and the inverse
contraction gap amplifies the bound. This calculation holds the map fixed and
assumes the displacement bound. It supplies no mechanism for achieving
agreement between local descriptions.

Strict contraction matters. On the same metric, identity encoding and readout
through a whole-space avatar give $F(s)=s$ for every state. This map is
nonexpansive, with factor one, and both silent and baseline profiles are fixed
points. The result locates the failure of the uniqueness guarantee at the
loss of strict contraction. Choosing a biological state space, metric and
readout remains a modelling task.

\section{The metric on global sections, and why the obvious ones fail}

Consider two mass profiles $(0.2,0.3,0.5)$ and $(0.2,0.4,0.4)$. Their largest
sitewise difference is $0.1$. This \emph{uniform distance} measures the
largest local error in reconstructing a profile. A $0/1$ distance instead calls
any unequal profiles distance one, whether they differ slightly or completely.
That discards the gradations an informative contraction needs.

Total variation compares how much two measures can disagree on a whole event
or region, rather than one site. L\'evy--Prokhorov distance also allows a small
spatial enlargement of regions before comparing their probabilities. These
are alternative ways to measure discrepancy, with different contraction and
completeness properties; naming one does not establish the needed theorem.

\what
To apply Banach one needs an actual complete metric on the space of global
sections. Two natural candidates fail, and the development records both
failures.

\statement
\emph{The discrete ($0/1$) metric fails informatively.} Under it, every
contraction is constant (\id{contracting\_implies\_const})---because distances
take only the values $0$ and $1$, and shrinking $1$ by any factor $<1$ forces
it to $0$. So that metric supports no informative fixed point.

\emph{The L\'evy--Prokhorov metric---named in the file header---is
\emph{not} what is constructed}, and the supplement says why. On a discrete
substrate the $\varepsilon$-thickening of a set is the set itself for
$\varepsilon<1$, so the averaging map
$\mu\mapsto\tfrac12\mu+\tfrac12\mu_0$ is not a L\'evy--Prokhorov contraction
there; and Mathlib carries no completeness result for that metric anyway.

\emph{What is constructed} is the uniform distance between densities: the
largest difference between the masses two sections' measures put on any single
site. It is proved complete by exhibiting the limit section of a Cauchy
sequence of densities, and distinct sections at distance $1/2$ are exhibited so
the space is not degenerate. On a finite substrate this is equivalent to total
variation of the glued measures within a factor of the number of sites---but
\emph{not equal} to it, and no claim of equivalence with L\'evy--Prokhorov is
made.

\subsection{The germ--measure dictionary}

The abstract local descriptions must be translated into numbers before their
distance can be calculated. On a discrete substrate, a point is itself its
smallest neighbourhood. Its germ therefore determines a single site's mass.
Reading those masses at all sites turns a section into a mass profile.
Conversely, the permitted mass profile defines a measure and hence a section.
The result \id{massEquivOn} proves this correspondence works in both directions
without identifying distinct sections or missing profiles. That bijection is
the dictionary making the uniform-distance construction possible.

\section{The avatar: making the self-model run through a region}

An \emph{encoding} writes information about a global state into a chosen
local region. A \emph{readout} reconstructs a global state from that local
record. The framework calls the region the avatar; the word does not imply a
picture, a person-shaped representation or a digital character.
The composite map means ``write, then read'':
\[
 s\ \longmapsto\ \mathrm{write}(s)\ \longmapsto\
 \mathrm{readout}(\mathrm{write}(s)).
\]
At a fixed point, the reconstruction equals the original state. A predicate
is a yes/no condition on an object. The \emph{restriction-resonance} predicate
requires the write to be the state's own restriction to the encoding region.
A constant encoding writes the same record whatever the state; this is the
mathematical meaning of the blinding control.

\what
The self-prediction map could have been carried as free data sitting beside the
structure. The development declines to, for a reason worth understanding.

\statement
If \id{predict} were a free field, then a \emph{blinded} boundary---one whose
self-encoding region is replaced by a constant---would retain the same
predictive model and the same fixed point, since nothing would tie the map to
the region. So the structure instead carries the region's \emph{write}
(\id{auto\_resonance}) and its \emph{read-out} (\id{readout}), and
\id{predict} is their \emph{composite} rather than a field. The self-model runs
through the encoding region---the ``avatar''---or it does not exist.

Four theorems fix the status of the resonance condition, which is a
\emph{predicate} (\id{IsRestrictionResonance}: the write is the presheaf
restriction of the state to the avatar region) rather than a field:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \id{eq\_of\_avatar\_eq}: if the avatar regions of a family of resonant
boundaries \emph{cover} the substrate, two global states with the same reading
at every avatar are equal. The readings are local; the global state is
reconstructed from them by the same sheaf condition Part XI uses.
\item \id{self\_mem\_range\_readout}: a fixed point lies in the range of the
read-out, so the Self is reconstructed from a single section over the avatar
region. This proves reconstruction from a local section; it does not by itself
establish the encoding's dimension or storage capacity.
\item \id{self\_eq\_readout\_restrict}: under resonance the Self satisfies
$s=\mathrm{readout}(s|_{\text{avatar}})$---the field is what its own localized
self-encoding reconstructs. This is exactly the statement a structure carrying
\id{predict} as free data could not make.
\item \id{not\_self\_of\_constResonance}: the negative half. The blinded
boundary's map is constant, its only fixed point is named, and the original
Self is \emph{not} one. Note the asymmetry: the opposite statement---that
blinding leaves the Self where it was---does not even typecheck, since a
contraction hypothesis about one boundary's map says nothing about another's.
\end{itemize}

(i) The read-out is a \emph{modelling choice}: no field dynamics produces it,
and the Lipschitz bound at the mean-field rate is an instance obligation.
(ii) The witness substrate's coupling value is a choice of units, not a
measurement. (iii) Nothing derives the resonance condition---that a physical
avatar performs the restriction is an assumption on an instance, in the same
class as the overlap-agreement obligation of Part XI.

\section{Resource-matched coupling reallocation}

Imagine a wiring budget of $R$ units to distribute among allowed pairs.
For fixed phases, the score of pair $(i,j)$ is $\cos(\theta_j-\theta_i)$:
one for alignment, minus one for opposition. With nonnegative weights, the
weighted sum cannot exceed the budget times the best allowed pair's score.
If an unavailable pair has a higher score, allowing a wire there can improve
the optimum at the same budget. This is the comparison made below.

\emph{Support} specifies where a coupling may be nonzero. Resource matching
means both alternatives receive the same total weight. Optimization means
finding the best score among permitted choices; suboptimal means some
permitted comparison achieves a strictly higher score.

\what
\emph{Rigidity} here means a constraint on \emph{which pairs can be wired at
all}---weights tunable, wiring fixed---not a constraint on weight values. The
predicate \id{RealizableIn}\,$S\,R\,A$ says $A$ is a valid coupling meeting
resource budget $R$ and vanishing on every pair the architecture has no wire
for.

\statement
If phase correlation over the \emph{wired} pairs is bounded by $M$, then no
realizable coupling achieves total correlation above $R\cdot M$, and that bound
is attained. Consequently, if some pair the wiring \emph{misses} has
correlation exceeding $M$, every realizable coupling is strictly
suboptimal---and the quantitative gap is at least
$R\,(\cos(\theta_l-\theta_k)-M)>0$ at equal budget. Note that this
\emph{derives} the suboptimality hypothesis that the reallocation lemma would
otherwise have to assume: it is a consequence of the wiring pattern, not an
extra premise.

\scope
The result is resource-matched and not question-begging, but it turns on
\emph{fixed wiring support}, not on continuity. \textbf{An architecture wired
to the best pair is not beaten.} It is a statement about constrained
optimization, not about biology.

\section{The continuum/discrete separation}

Ordinary area gives a single point zero area, and any finite list of points
still has zero area. A finite patch can have positive area. An \emph{atomless}
measure has this no-point-mass property in the spaces considered here; length
and area are examples. Integrating a function nonzero only at finitely many
points therefore gives zero. A different measure, such as counting measure,
would see those points. The distinction is in what is being measured.

\statement
\id{fieldCorrelation} integrates a kernel against $\mu\otimes\mu$. A
finitely-sited architecture is a finite set of points; on an \emph{atomless}
substrate such a kernel contributes exactly zero to the coupling energy, for
every phase field and every choice of weights, while a patch-supported kernel
gives $c\,\mu(U)^2$. So a continuum observable cannot register a finitely-sited
architecture at all.

\scope

resource-matched}---a measure-zero substrate carries zero resource in the
$\mu\otimes\mu$ sense as well. What it establishes is a claim about the
\emph{model's observable}, not about computational power. And as
Section~\ref{sec:nokernel} noted, it cuts against the framework's own
coarse-graining step as much as against anything else.

either.} The manuscript's identification is, as stated, multiply realizable:

electromagnetic coupling, so the substrate claim is carried entirely by E56 and

\part*{Part XV. Formal method: why the development declares no axioms}

\section{What formalization is claimed to add}

A formal proof spells out an argument in a language whose rules software can
check. A \emph{type} specifies what kind of object is allowed: a number, a
function, a probability measure, or evidence for a proposition. Type-checking
checks that every expression supplies the kind of object the next step needs.
A proof \emph{term} is the explicit object encoding a derivation. The checker
can verify that a conclusion follows from assumptions; it cannot measure
whether a brain satisfies them or decide whether the interpretation is apt.

For example, a proof about a density does not itself supply a time-dependent
path. To use it in a theorem requiring a trajectory, one must construct that
trajectory and prove the relationship, or state that relationship as an
assumption. This is how a missing physical bridge becomes visible in code.

\what
A prose table of assumptions would list the same eight edges. What the Lean
development adds is that it makes the list \emph{non-negotiable}.

\statement
The characteristic failure in an argument of this shape is not a false step but
a \emph{silent change of subject}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item a theorem about a stationary density read as a claim about a trajectory;
\item an inequality about a register read as a claim about learning;
\item agreement of phases read as compatibility of representations.
\end{itemize}
Type-checking forbids each of those substitutions. An assumption needed to make
a step go through appears as a hypothesis \emph{in the term}, rather than as a
word in a sentence. The last substitution is not merely forbidden but
\emph{refuted by a constructed object}---the incompatible-at-equal-phase cover
of Section~\ref{sec:compat}---and that object is what a prose table cannot
carry.

\scope
Formalization does not make the assumptions true. It makes them impossible to
lose.

\section{Why a global axiom is the wrong shape for a physical postulate}
\label{sec:soundness}

An \emph{axiom} is accepted without proof. A \emph{class} or structure is a
record containing named quantities and conditions; its entries are called
\emph{fields}, a programming term distinct from a physical field. An
\emph{instance} is one concrete record satisfying those conditions.
Putting a heat law in the record says ``this result concerns systems obeying
this law''. Declaring it separately for every record can instead assert it
even for a record with heat values chosen to violate it.

A variable is bound when a statement specifies what it ranges over.
An implicit argument is still an input to a theorem even when Lean fills it
in automatically. \emph{Inconsistent} means the assumptions yield a
contradiction. In classical logic a contradiction permits any conclusion;
a successful build alone is therefore not evidence that the physical
assumptions were expressed consistently.

\what
Formalization is only as trustworthy as its axioms, and an \emph{inconsistent}
axiom silently makes every theorem downstream of it vacuous while leaving the
build green. The failure mode is specific and easy to reproduce.

\statement
The failure has one shape: \textbf{an axiom that constrains a symbol it does
not itself bind}. Such a declaration is not a postulate about one system; it is
a constraint on \emph{every} model of the class the symbol belongs to,
including models built to violate it---and a class with such a model is thereby
inconsistent. Three concrete cases, each of which proves \id{False} on its own:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item A symmetry-breaking statement taking the potential-energy functional as
an \emph{implicit} argument occurring only in its hypothesis. Instantiating
that functional with the constant map discharges the hypothesis by \id{rfl} and
yields the conclusion for an arbitrary field. With $V(v)=v^2$, $v_0=0$ and
$\phi\equiv1$ this proves $1=0$.
\item A Landauer heat statement equating a free class field with a
bath-entropy difference \emph{for every instance}. An instance with zero heat
dissipation and a bath gaining one bit gives $0=\log2$.
\item A KL bound asserting $\sigma\ge D_{KL}(P\|Q)/\Delta t$ for \emph{all}
$P,Q$, while $\sigma$ is a single real fixed by a class field. Since $D_{KL}$
is unbounded above, a point mass against a uniform distribution refutes it.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Two further declarations of the same shape are recorded because their
refutation is different in kind: as global assertions pinning free fields, a
phase-periodicity condition and a synchronization-to-section equation are
refutable as soon as the probability presheaf admits two distinct global
sections. That refutation needs no arithmetic at all, only that the sheaf is
non-trivial.

\medskip\noindent
The rule that prevents all of this is uniform:

\begin{quote}
\emph{A physical postulate that mentions a class field must be a \textbf{field
of that class}, never a standalone axiom quantified over all instances.}
\end{quote}

\noindent
A postulate is then an \emph{obligation each model discharges}, not a global
claim about every model. Under that rule the development declares no axioms at
all: \id{\#print axioms} on any result reports only Lean's own foundational
three---\id{propext}, \id{Classical.choice} and \id{Quot.sound}.

Those foundations support treating logically equivalent propositions as equal,
choosing elements whose existence is known, and identifying equivalent
representatives in quotient constructions, respectively. ``No axioms'' in
this repository means no additional declared axioms beyond those foundations.
The default Lean build audits every library declaration and rejects any other
axiom, including the placeholder \id{sorryAx} introduced by an unfinished
proof. A separate local check also catches unfinished anonymous examples.

\section{Witnesses, and what inhabitation does and does not prove}

``Every object satisfying conditions $A$ has property $B$'' is true if no
object satisfies $A$. This is \emph{vacuous truth}. A witness supplies an
actual object satisfying $A$, showing the result has at least one case to
which it applies. Inhabited means at least one such object exists.
A toy witness tests mathematical consistency, not biological plausibility.
A discrete topology allows every set of sites as an open region; its Borel
$\sigma$-algebra is the collection of measurable sets generated by those
regions, which here includes every subset. A $\sigma$-algebra is a collection
closed under complements and countable unions, providing the events to which
a measure assigns sizes or probabilities.

\what
A conditional theorem about an \emph{uninhabited} class is vacuously true and
worthless. So every class carrying a physical postulate gets an explicit witness under \id{Examples/}: a one-bit erasure system with a genuine bath
reproducing $\Delta Q=k_BT\ln2$; a perfectly resonant system discharging the KL
bound; a scalar field on a one-point spacetime discharging the action
principles; and---the most demanding case---a thermodynamic cover over a
three-site substrate with the discrete topology and its Borel $\sigma$-algebra,
covered by two overlapping open patches meeting at a single shared site.

\scope
\textbf{What the witnesses establish is that the classes are inhabitable, so
the theorems about them are not vacuous. What they do not establish is that a
cortex is such an instance.} In the cover above the phase field is already
constant---every patch locked at $\theta=0$---which is precisely what makes
the equilibrium obligation dischargeable. Getting a physical system into that
configuration is the content of the informal dynamical argument, not of the
witness.

that assumption explicit as a density law: its energy depends on the same
step's joint entropy reduction. Its spatial convergence follows from mesh
regularity, and the budget bounds its limit only through the supplied gain
and profile. That construction supplies no kernel. The microscopic witness
supplies one, from occupancies the same step changes, and pays the
installation work; its convergence is again spatial, and its hardware is
declared. The passive edge is discharged on that same hardware from a declared
predictive system's own joint law, and two such systems differing by their
whole dissipated work give the identical edge.

After either branch reaches n5, \id{chain\_from\_coarseGrains} supplies the
common proof through E56--E89. The field, cover and fixed section are preserved
through that passage. Introducing feedback changes the thermodynamic premise;
it does not remove the supplied compatibility, contraction or fixed-state
identification.

\scope
The joint witnesses prove the hypotheses \emph{simultaneously consistent}.
They do not prove that one mechanism realizes them, and they do not
establish the empirical truth of any premise. E12 remains equivalent to its own
conclusion, which is why n1 and n2 are described as independent roots rather
than as a derivation.

The active chain theorem concerns an autonomous substep with the controller
fixed, finite states and strictly positive probabilities. The complete finite
update theorem in Section~\ref{sec:agency-cycle} matches the intermediate law
and accounts for both substeps at a common thermal scale. Its heat bound does
not supply E34Active's register-to-actuator allocation, and no heat bound
supplies the hardware or the partitions that E45Active's spatial convergence
rests on. A claim about learning
requires specified update dynamics and a criterion for improvement, as in
the policy-register model of Section~\ref{sec:policy-learning}. That model's
convergence is over learning updates; n5 concerns refinement of a spatial
mesh. Convergence over learning time alone does not establish that limit.

\subsection{A chosen physical model and its empirical interpretation}

A physical model specifies variables with proposed physical meanings and rules
for their evolution. For the two-bit model, these are the two states, their
interaction energy, the thermal transition probabilities and the reservoir.
Choosing those ingredients does not require first measuring a brain. One can
prove a conditional entropy balance, construct a counterexample or derive a
convergence theorem entirely from explicitly chosen mathematical assumptions.

An empirical interpretation is a further claim: that particular neural
quantities obey those assumptions closely enough for the result to explain
an observation. That claim needs evidence. Lean can check whether a specified
dynamics implies a stated property; it cannot establish which dynamics cortex
implements. Progress in the formal model and validation of its biological
interpretation are therefore distinct tasks.

\section{Doing statistics on angles}

An electrode channel records a voltage difference over time. The analysis
extracts an angle from each selected oscillatory signal and then asks how those
angles are distributed. A \emph{sample} is one measured value; a distribution
is the probability pattern those values are used to estimate. An
\emph{estimator} is the rule turning samples into an estimate of a quantity.
The \emph{estimand} is the underlying quantity it aims to measure. Changing
which sites or times are pooled can change the estimand even if the formula
stays the same.

\what
Phases live on a circle, where ordinary statistics break. The mean of
$1^\circ$ and $359^\circ$ is $180^\circ$ if you average the numbers and
$0^\circ$ if you average the directions; only the second is right. Circular
statistics works with the unit vectors $e^{i\theta_j}$ instead:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[Resultant length $r$.] $r=\left|\frac1N\sum_j e^{i\theta_j}\right|$. Zero
for a uniform spread, one for perfect alignment. This is the order parameter.
\item[Circular mean $\psi$.] The direction of that same resultant vector.
\item[Concentration $a$.] The von Mises parameter, governing how tightly the
distribution is peaked.
\end{description}

\why
Because the manuscript's protocol turns on estimating $a$ and $r$
\emph{independently}, and that is the constraint most easily violated in
practice: linear statistics on wrapped phases estimate neither quantity.

\section{Getting the phases: the analytic signal}

For a pure cosine $x(t)=\cos(\omega t)$, adding the quarter-cycle partner
$\sin(\omega t)$ gives the arrow
$z(t)=\cos(\omega t)+\mathrm{i}\sin(\omega t)$. Its direction is phase and
its length is amplitude. The Hilbert transform constructs a corresponding
quadrature partner for a more general signal. A \emph{narrowband} signal
concentrates its oscillatory activity in a limited frequency band, making a
single phase easier to interpret. A mixture of unrelated rhythms can still
yield a numerical angle, but not necessarily one meaningful oscillator phase.

\emph{Detrending} removes a fitted slow baseline. A bandpass filter retains
a chosen frequency range and suppresses others. Filter order controls its
frequency response; a Butterworth filter is designed for a smooth, flat
response in its passing band. Second-order sections implement it as a series
of small filtering operations for numerical reliability. Filtering forward
and backward removes net phase delay, but uses future as well as past samples
and can distort short transients. Padding supplies extra samples beyond the
interval of interest to reduce distortions at its edges.

EEG means electroencephalography, electrical recordings at the scalp. EOG
records eye-related electrical activity and EMG muscle activity. Hertz (Hz)
means cycles per second; milliseconds (ms) are thousandths of a second.

\what
The \emph{Hilbert transform} turns a real oscillatory signal $x(t)$ into a
complex \emph{analytic signal} $z(t)=x(t)+i\,\mathcal{H}[x](t)=A(t)e^{i\phi(t)}$,
from which an instantaneous amplitude and an instantaneous phase can be read.
It is meaningful only for reasonably narrowband signals, which is why the
pipeline bandpass-filters first.

\statement
The implemented pipeline: detrend each channel; bandpass with a 4th-order
Butterworth filter in the 4--40~Hz band using second-order sections
(\id{sosfiltfilt}, which is zero-phase); take the Hilbert transform for
$\theta_j(t)$; use only the 62 scalp EEG channels, discarding EOG and EMG.
Three seconds of padding on each side is downloaded and trimmed after filtering
to suppress edge artifacts.

\section{The parameter-free prediction, and the trap in testing it}
\label{sec:collapse}

Suppose a curve claims one measurement predicts another. A meaningful check
must calculate both quantities without forcing either to obey that curve.
Here $r$ comes from averaging phase arrows, while $a$ is estimated from the
shape of their histogram. ``Independent'' means distinct estimating equations;
it does not mean the estimates, made from the same data, are statistically
independent.

\emph{Maximum likelihood} chooses model parameters that make the observed
sample most probable. A \emph{sufficient statistic} retains all the sample
information that this fitting calculation uses about a parameter. A score
equation sets the derivative of the log likelihood to zero to find a candidate
optimum. In the centered von Mises fit that equation already enforces the
very concentration--coherence relation being tested.

\what
The stationary model predicts that concentration and coherence lie on one
curve:
\[
 r \;=\; \frac{I_1(a)}{I_0(a)} ,
\]
with \emph{no fitted shape parameter}---the same curve for every subject and
every state.

\statement
\textbf{The estimator must not be a maximum-likelihood one, and this is the
sharpest methodological point in the empirical section.} The von Mises family
is a one-parameter exponential family whose sufficient statistic is
$\sum_j\cos\theta_j$---that is, $r$ itself. So \emph{any} ML estimate of the
concentration solves its own score equation
$I_1(\hat a)/I_0(\hat a)=r$ and returns $\hat a=A^{-1}(r)$ identically. The
pair $(\hat a,r)$ would then lie on the predicted curve \emph{for any input
whatsoever}, including phases drawn from a distribution that is not von Mises,
and the collapse would be an algebraic identity rather than a test.

The implemented estimator therefore uses the log-density \emph{shape}: phases
are counted in intervals called bins. A pseudocount adds a small positive
count so an empty bin does not require taking $\log0$. Regression fits a line;
ordinary least squares chooses its slope and intercept to minimize the sum of
squared vertical errors. Unweighted means each bin contributes equally.
Specifically, phases
are centred on their circular mean, binned into 40 bins on $[-\pi,\pi]$, and
the log of the binned density (with a $0.5$ pseudocount) is regressed on
$\cos\theta$ by \emph{unweighted} ordinary least squares. Under the von Mises
model $\log\rho(\theta)=a\cos\theta-\log(2\pi I_0(a))$, so the slope is $a$.
Other weighting rules are different estimators and are not assumed equivalent
to maximum likelihood.

The final residual $r-R(\hat a)$ compares measured coherence with the curve's
prediction using the separately estimated shape; the hat denotes an estimate.
The artificial controls use other shapes. A wrapped Cauchy wraps a Cauchy
distribution, which has heavier tails than a Gaussian, from the line onto the
circle. A top-hat arc has equal density on a chosen arc and zero elsewhere;
a mixture draws from one of two distributions with specified proportions.
These controls ask whether the procedure can reject other density shapes.

\emph{Discriminating power was verified directly.} At the analysis's sample
size, a wrapped Cauchy ($\rho=0.6$), a top-hat arc and a $50/50$ von
Mises--uniform mixture give residuals $r-I_1(\hat a)/I_0(\hat a)$ of $+0.088$,
$-0.117$ and $+0.075$, against a worst case of $0.0024$ on genuine von Mises
samples---a separation of $31.7\times$. A uniform distribution passes,
correctly, being von Mises at $a=0$. The pseudocount biases the estimator by
$\le0.007$ for $a\le3$, reaching $-0.375$ only at $a=5$ where the troughs empty
out.

\scope
Even a perfect result here identifies neither ephaptic coupling nor this
framework: the relation is common to the whole von Mises family, so agreement
with it does not distinguish this framework from alternative synchronization
models. Worse, within the same ansatz $a/r=K/D$ for $r>0$, so the limiting
threshold ratio \emph{follows from} the density relation instead of testing
it---and ratios near the finite-size floor are especially unstable.

\section{What the EEG analysis found}

Propofol is an anesthetic drug; these are recordings during drug-induced
sedation, not ordinary awakening. OpenNeuro is the repository hosting the
dataset. A \emph{montage} specifies which voltage differences become channels.
A common-reference montage subtracts one shared reference from each electrode;
a bipolar montage takes differences between electrode pairs. Shared reference
signals can therefore enter many channels at once.

SEM, the standard error of a mean, estimates uncertainty in that mean under
the stated sampling assumptions. It differs from the spread of individual
observations. RMSE is root-mean-square error and reports a typical residual
size. Theta, alpha, beta and gamma name conventional EEG frequency bands;
these names concern different ranges of rhythm speed. ``Broadband'' combines
the whole selected range. A bootstrap repeatedly resamples observations to
estimate sampling uncertainty; percentile intervals use quantiles (cutoffs at
specified fractions) of those estimates. Nearby time bins can be dependent,
so treating them as independent in resampling can misstate uncertainty.

\statement
On public propofol-sedation EEG (OpenNeuro ds005620, \citealp{bajwa2025}),
21 subjects at 65 channels and 5~kHz, analysed in non-overlapping 100~ms bins:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Cross-subject (8 subjects), bipolar montage.} Mean residual
$-0.0002\pm0.0002$ (SEM), RMSE $0.0005$, max $|r-I_1/I_0(a)|=0.0013$. Group
$a=0.373$ (range $0.302$--$0.542$), $r=0.173$.
\item \textbf{Common-reference montage.} Mean residual $-0.0057\pm0.0028$,
RMSE $0.0093$, max $0.0246$---an order of magnitude worse, and
\emph{systematically negative} in every block and every subject.
\item \textbf{Narrowband.} Theta, alpha, beta, gamma and broadband all
collapse (mean residuals $-0.0018$, $+0.0001$, $+0.0001$, $+0.0000$,
$+0.0000$). The collapse is not band-specific.
\item \textbf{Window sensitivity.} Stable over 20--100~ms pooling windows; not
at 5--10~ms.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The montage gap is the one systematic feature. A common-mode component that
simply raised $r$ would push the residual the other way, so that is not the
mechanism; the sign is instead consistent with the top-hat synthetic control,
suggesting a shared reference imposes a sharp common-mode peak on channels that
remain broadly distributed around it, steepening the fitted $a$ more than it
raises the pooled resultant length. This is reported as a candidate mechanism
whose direction fits, not a demonstrated one.

\scope
Four limits, and the third is the one that matters most.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The estimand is not what the protocol asks for.} 62 channels
pooled over a 100~ms window estimates a \emph{spatiotemporal} distribution, not
an instantaneous spatial one---a departure forced by channel count, and one
that any scalp implementation will face. Pooling is required because the
independent log-density estimator needs at least 100 phase samples.
\item \textbf{Referencing is a material analysis choice}, not a preprocessing
detail. Bipolar and common-reference give different answers.
\item \textbf{The concentration range is too narrow to be informative about the
curve.} Across the eight subjects $a$ spans only $0.302$--$0.542$. Over so
short an arc the Bessel ratio is nearly straight: it departs from the naive
linear form $a/2$ by at most $0.0094$ and from $\tanh(a/2)$ by at most
$0.0030$, while per-bin residual scatter is $0.02$--$0.03$. \textbf{The data
are compatible with the relation but do not discriminate $I_1/I_0$ from a
straight line.} Doing so needs recordings reaching $a\gtrsim1.5$, where the two
separate by more than $0.17$.
\item \textbf{Bootstrap intervals are exploratory.} Resampling time bins (2000
resamples, percentile method) does not preserve dependence between neighbouring
bins.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
And the purpose was never to test the awakening hypothesis, for which these are
the wrong data: they concern propofol sedation, not recovery from sleep. The
exercise demonstrates what the protocol produces and where it is fragile.

\section{The awakening experiment}

A \emph{protocol} fixes how measurements and decisions will be made before
the relevant outcomes are examined. A proxy is an indirect measurement of a
quantity; calibration establishes its relationship to that quantity in a
specified setting. A behavioral endpoint is a separately defined outcome,
such as performance on a task. Predeclaring it prevents redefining recovery
after seeing the coherence curve.

Diffusion MRI is magnetic resonance imaging sensitive to water movement;
it is not a direct measure of the model's phase diffusion $D$. Astrocytes are
supporting brain cells whose signals might supply other indirect measurements
of extracellular conditions. These proxies require their own validation.
Unblinding means revealing outcomes or group labels hidden during analysis
design. Observation noise is measurement error, distinct from random motion
in the underlying model.

A sigmoid is an S-shaped rising curve; an exponential recovery has a fixed
characteristic rate of approach. They are alternative shapes to compare
under the same filtering and measurement process. \emph{Hysteresis} means the
response depends on the path by which a control value was reached, such as
different coherence on increasing and decreasing coupling. A stationary fold
is a branch turning back in the control parameter; it can permit multiple
stationary states. Dynamical lag can produce a loop even without such a fold.

\statement
The manuscript specifies six requirements, and the design---not any single
measurement---is where the content of the hypothesis lives.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Coupling and diffusion in the same units.} $K$ and $D$ estimated
from the same cortical regime, in inverse-time units, separating phase
diffusion from fixed natural-frequency differences and from observation noise. Without
this, ``$K>2D$'' is not an evaluable statement about cortex. Watching a
sequence of equilibrated phase distributions will not do it: the stationary
density is von Mises with concentration $Kr/D$, so it fixes the \emph{ratio}
and nothing else, and a coupling increase, a diffusion decrease and a common
drive of matched strength are then identical window for window. The estimate
has to reach the phase increments, which still carry the rate, and telling an
endogenous coupling from a common drive needs a measured drive or a controlled
perturbation on top of that.
\item \textbf{Geometry calibrated to coupling.} A diffusion-MRI observable or
astrocytic signal indicates the extracellular volume fraction $\alpha$; its mapping
to effective coupling must be established separately, with its own uncertainty.
The conductivity argument below supplies the expected sign of that mapping and
nothing more.
\item \textbf{Adequate spatial resolution.} At least 100 spatial samples per
estimate, $r$ by resultant length and $a$ by unweighted log-density regression.
With fewer sites, pooling over time changes the estimand---and must be stated
as such.
\item \textbf{An independent recovery endpoint.} Behavioural recovery measured
separately from the phase trace, so latency is not defined by the signal whose
latency is at issue.
\item \textbf{A predeclared onset criterion}, fixed above the finite-size floor
before unblinding, chosen knowing that a finite ramp smears the cusp.
\item \textbf{Model comparison under matched observation.} Against exponential,
flexible sigmoidal and other slow-recovery models under identical temporal
filtering and observation noise. Latency alone cannot distinguish the
mechanism, because many processes create delays.
\end{enumerate}

\statement
The design fails informatively in three ways: failure of the stationary
phase-density relation over a sufficiently broad concentration range; absence
of the proposed coupling--geometry association; or a recovery trace better
explained by an alternative after finite-rate and observation effects are
accounted for.

\scope
\textbf{Two apparent failures are not failures.} A linear apparent onset is
evidence against the adiabatic approximation only where that approximation is
independently justified---see Section~\ref{sec:ramp}---and does not falsify the
field hypothesis under arbitrary ramp speeds. And the absence of a fold in the
stationary equation excludes stationary coexistence of two positive branches in
this model, but does not exclude finite-rate hysteresis or adjudicate theories
of ignition.

\section{Why the units do not match}
\label{sec:gamma}

\emph{Dimensional analysis} checks whether quantities have compatible physical
units. Distance divided by time is speed; distance alone cannot be compared
with a speed threshold. Likewise, a phase shift and a phase-change rate are
different quantities. A Fermi estimate is a rough estimate assembled from
plausible factors to establish an approximate scale. Correct arithmetic cannot
repair incompatible units.

\what
The manuscript calls this ``the sharpest open limitation of the framework'',
and it is a pure dimensional-analysis problem---the kind that is embarrassing
precisely because it cannot be argued away.

\statement
A natural Fermi estimate of field-mediated coupling multiplies a population
size $N$, a spike-time shift per unit field $s$, a field amplitude $E$, and an
oscillation frequency $f$. Track the units:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item $s$ is a time per unit field, so $sE$ is a \emph{time};
\item multiplying by a frequency $f$ gives a \emph{pure number}---the
fractional phase advance a cell receives per cycle from the population field;
\item multiplying by a population size $N$ leaves it a pure number.
\end{itemize}
But a coupling in the sense of the stochastic model \eqref{eq:sde} is an
\emph{inverse time}, comparable with $D$. So the estimate cannot be compared
with $D$ at all. The conversion
\[
 \Keff=\gamma\,N s E f
\]
puts the entire gap into a rate calibration $\gamma$.

\section{Why \texorpdfstring{$\gamma$}{gamma} is not recoverable}

A unit conversion changes how the same quantity is expressed: seconds into
milliseconds, for example. Calibration determines an unknown physical
relationship. A single timing shift does not tell us whether it persists,
decays, or accumulates with later shifts. The rate $\gamma$ encodes that
missing relationship, so choosing a time unit cannot measure it.

\statement
The substantive point is that $\gamma$ is a \emph{hypothesis about
persistence}, not a unit conversion to be fixed by convention. It states how a
per-cycle phase advance accumulates into a drift rate, which depends on how
long a field-driven timing shift survives. Three candidates are available and
they are not interchangeable:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item $\gamma=f$: each cycle's advance is retained in full and independently;
\item $\gamma=1/\tau$ for a phase relaxation time $\tau$: it decays as the
medium relaxes;
\item $\gamma=D$: it accumulates at the rate phase itself diffuses.
\end{itemize}
These need not agree, in cortex or anywhere else, and a field measurement
chooses between them only if the persistence of induced timing shifts is
measured alongside it.

\textbf{The third must be declined for an additional reason, and the reason is
instructive.} Under $\gamma=D$ the threshold test $\Keff/D>2$ reduces to
$NsEf>2$---a comparison of the dimensionless field estimate against the number
$2$, \emph{with the diffusion parameter cancelled out of a test whose entire
content was the ratio of coupling to diffusion}. The economy of that
substitution is what makes it unsafe: it converts an unmeasured rate into an
apparent confirmation while removing the quantity the confirmation was about.
The same objection applies to any $\gamma$ recovered by requiring the model to
cross its own threshold, or read off the Bessel collapse used to test the
stationary density.

\scope
Every saved field-strength value in the repository is therefore
\emph{conditional} on a calibration convention. Under the numerical convention
$\gamma=1$ in the simulation's inverse-time unit, the Fermi arithmetic gives
required field ranges that fall \emph{below} the
$\fermiFieldMin$--$\fermiFieldMax$~mV/mm comparison band, and they scale
inversely with $\gamma$. The simulation does not measure the calibration, so it
cannot independently validate physical-unit consistency or a cortical field
threshold. Identification requires coupling and diffusion estimated in one
cortical regime by a route \emph{independent} of the field arithmetic---which is
item~1 of the protocol above.

\section{The empirical case for the field, and its critics}

\emph{Endogenous} means generated within the system. Transmembrane current
crosses a cell membrane; extracellular current flows through the spaces
outside cells. \emph{Ephaptic coupling} is an influence of the surrounding
electric field on a cell's membrane voltage and timing, without requiring a
direct synaptic connection. Entrainment means an influence brings a rhythm's
timing into a sustained relationship with it. Modulation changes ongoing
activity without necessarily initiating it.

The cerebral cortex is the outer neural sheet of the brain. An action
potential, or spike, is a brief electrical pulse of a neuron. Synapses are
specialized junctions through which cells influence one another. Gap junctions
are channels directly connecting cells; a syncytium here means a connected
cellular network functioning collectively. Representational drift means the
neural activity associated with a measured content changes over time.
Superposition means adding contributions to a field.

Conductivity measures how readily current flows for a given electric field;
its unit is siemens per metre (S/m). A field in volts per metre (V/m) is a
voltage change per distance, and $1\,\mathrm{mV/mm}=1\,\mathrm{V/m}$.
Resistive conduction means current approximately follows the applied field
without a substantial frequency-dependent phase offset in the stated range.
An effective-medium model replaces microscopic detail by an averaged material
description. Volume fraction is the proportion of tissue volume occupied by
a component; diffusion tortuosity measures how tissue obstacles hinder water
or solute diffusion compared with free medium. Sleep inertia is the temporary
impairment after waking.

\statement
The identification of the continuum kernel with cortex's endogenous
electromagnetic field is supported as \emph{modulatory and integrative}, not as
primary. Transmembrane currents generate extracellular fields that shift
neighbouring membrane potentials---ephaptic coupling. Endogenous fields of
$1$--$5$~mV/mm entrain spike timing
\citep{frohlich2010,anastassiou2015}; stable electric fields carry information
that survives representational drift in the underlying spiking population
\citep{pinotsis2022,pinotsis2023ephaptic,pinotsis2023cytoelectric}; the field
is spatially continuous and superposes contributions from large populations,
which is the integrative role CEMI field theory assigns it
\citep{mcfadden2002,mcfadden2020,pockett2012}, physically supported by
astrocytic syncytia joined by gap junctions \citep{verkhratsky2018}. Sleep-
associated changes in extracellular space and clearance \citep{xie2013}
motivate the possible slow control of coupling.

\scope
The formal predicate that E56 carries is explicit and modest: a jointly
continuous kernel, a normalized substrate, a coupling constant equal to that
kernel's double average, no single site contributing more than half of it, and
a noise $D$ that is the field's own. \textbf{No object in the development
denotes cortex}, so whether cortex satisfies these is not a Lean question.
Toy witnesses satisfy the conditions; none is a model calibrated to cortex.

The longest-standing objection is that the fields are too weak to matter,
pressed from \emph{within} the field-theoretic camp by
\citet{pockett2012,pockett2002}. \citet{voroslakos2018} found that applied
current is shunted through scalp, delivering fields at or below $1$~V/m in
cortex. That bounds \emph{applied} rather than endogenous fields, but it
constrains how much causal work a field of that magnitude can do---and is the
reason the framework does not claim the field drives spiking. Changes in phase
organization under propofol \citep{bastos2021} likewise do not measure a
crossing of the model's coupling threshold.

Extracellular geometry reaches the coupling through the field amplitude and not
through a further mechanism. The extracellular space occupies a volume fraction
$\alpha\approx0.2$ of cortical tissue at a diffusion tortuosity $\lambda\approx1.6$
\citep{sykova2008}, and natural sleep raises $\alpha$ from roughly $0.14$ to $0.23$
\citep{xie2013}. Cortical tissue conducts approximately resistively below
$10$~kHz, at $0.3$--$0.6$~S/m, an order of magnitude set by the conductivity of
saline diluted into that volume fraction \citep{barbour2017}. At a fixed
transmembrane current density, then, a larger $\alpha$ means a smaller
extracellular field: the space contracts on awakening and $K$ should rise. That
is an effective-medium argument, so it fixes a sign and not a magnitude, and it
says nothing about how a heterogeneous field aggregates into a single $K$.
Neither the monotonic relation
between extracellular geometry and coupling, nor its role in human sleep
inertia, follows from the cited physiology; the model requires both to be
measured.

The identification would be challenged if independently calibrated
field-mediated coupling were insufficient for the proposed regime, or if a
selective perturbation of that interaction left the proposed neural and
behavioural endpoints unchanged. Such a perturbation needs controls for direct
effects on transmembrane currents and other coupling pathways; selectivity is
not assumed experimentally available.

The composition terminates in a mathematical object, and the manuscript takes a
position on what that object is. This part states the position, its motivations,

\section{The commitment}

A \emph{formal correlate} is a mathematical feature proposed to correspond
to an aspect of experience. The correspondence requires an interpretation;
a theorem about the feature alone cannot establish it. A conscious episode
is a period considered as one experienced situation. \emph{Unity} concerns
the experience being one whole, and a \emph{minimal self} the perceiver's
place or perspective within that situation, without requiring a life story
or an explicit verbal judgment about oneself.

\statement
Two clauses, neither a theorem:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[Unity.] The unique global section glued from compatible local states is
the formal correlate of the unity of a conscious episode---the fact that a
subject undergoes \emph{one} experience rather than a collection of
co-occurring local ones.
\item[Selfhood.] Accurate internal reconstruction of that same section is
the proposed formal correlate of a minimal self. Assigning it this meaning
requires evidence that the encoding represents the system's own condition.
\end{description}

\section{Motivations and the choices they require}

A representation carries information interpreted as being about something,
its \emph{content}. A decoder is a specified rule inferring that content
from measured activity; it does not follow automatically from coherence.
Reflexivity means the representation concerns the representing system itself.
An idealization deliberately makes a feature exact, such as reconstruction
without error, to define a tractable model. Testing it requires deciding how
much approximation is acceptable in real measurements.

Compatibility gives a precise way for distinct local descriptions to contribute
to one global state. Only shared domains must agree. A content model must
therefore distinguish inconsistent descriptions from consistent descriptions
of an uncertain situation or a conflict between judgments. Spatial mass
profiles provide the formal example; extending it to decoded perceptual
contents requires a suitable model of global extension.

The manuscript illustrates this with reaching for a cup. A description of
the cup's appearance and one of the hand's relation to it can agree on cup
location after conversion to common coordinates, while contributing different
details. This is a modelling example, not an implemented neural decoder or an
instance of the spatial-measure theorem. A content model must establish its
own extension property.

The reflexive map adds an internal representation of that same state. Under
restriction-resonance, it restricts the global state to an encoding region and
reads the encoding back out. A fixed point is accurately reconstructed at the
level of its modelled variables. Exact reconstruction is an idealization, and
the manuscript leaves an approximation appropriate to changing neural states
as a research question.

The self interpretation concerns the perceiver's place in the represented
situation. Reconstructing a scene alone would not demonstrate a perspective.
A candidate encoding of hand or viewpoint should have a measurable role in
how the system represents its own relation to the cup. Interventions on that
encoding should produce changes predicted by its proposed readout. This
functional criterion helps assign meaning to the model's variables; the
fixed-point theorem does not establish it or identify it with experience.

Both constructions require independent choices. Local descriptions must carry
content attributable to their domains, and the encoding and readout must have
a demonstrated relation to the global state across a specified range of
states. Contraction and a fixed point alone would also hold for a constant
readout returning a predetermined state. Their psychological significance
depends on the proposed encoding relation.

The full identification proposes that unity and reflexivity occur together in
conscious episodes. Gluing itself supplies no necessity for reflexivity.
Unified experience without the specified self-encoding would challenge that
requirement while leaving the compatibility clause assessable separately.

\section{The costs}

The \emph{hard problem} is the question why a physical process should have
any felt quality at all. \emph{Multiple realizability} means the same abstract
conditions could be implemented by different physical systems. Both terms
concern the interpretation's reach, rather than an algebraic step in a proof.

\scope
The manuscript states these rather than leaving them to a referee.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{It does not address the hard problem.} The commitment is a
proposal about \emph{which} physical state is the conscious one, not about why
states of that kind feel like anything. It is therefore open to the objection
that a system could satisfy every condition---compatible local states, a
supercritical field, an accurate self-model---while nothing is experienced.
\emph{No reply to that objection is offered or claimed.}
\item \textbf{It is multiply realizable as stated.} Nothing in the sheaf or
fixed-point conditions is specific to cortex or to electromagnetic coupling, so
the substrate claim rests entirely on the two empirical edges E56 and E67. No
conclusion about artificial systems follows from the formal results.
\item \textbf{It is independent of the physical tests.} The predictions of
Parts VIII, IX and XVI concern coupling, coherence and recovery. They can fail
while the identification stands, or succeed while it is wrong.
\end{itemize}

\section{What would count against it, and the observable that is missing}

An observable is a quantity that a specified measurement can estimate.
\emph{Sufficiency} means meeting the conditions guarantees the outcome;
\emph{necessity} means the outcome cannot occur without them. A dissociation
test seeks cases where proposed linked properties come apart, such as coherent
timing with incompatible decoded contents. Separately measuring each property
is essential to such a test.

\statement
A full test must evaluate coupling, compatibility and the proposed
self-encoding separately, alongside an independent experience endpoint. A
state satisfying all the conditions with evidence against a unified episode
would challenge sufficiency. Unified experience with incompatible local
descriptions would challenge the unity clause; unified experience without the
specified self-encoding would challenge the reflexive requirement. Absence of
report alone cannot establish absence of experience.

The compatibility observable is specified but not implemented, and the neural
self-encoding has yet to be identified. The supplement details what a
compatibility measurement needs; the manuscript explains how that measurement
would enter a dissociation test.

\subsection{Three components}

A \emph{null model} specifies what a measurement would look like if the
tested effect were absent. Here the null is compatibility with noisy decoders,
so even agreeing underlying contents can give a positive estimated distance.
Validation means checking the method on cases with known answers, including
both matching and mismatching local descriptions.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[A restriction map.] Two regions with overlapping receptive territories
each carry a distribution over what occupies the shared part, decoded from that
region's activity alone. Compatibility is a relation between the two
\emph{restrictions}, so the map taking a region's decoded distribution to a
distribution over the overlap must be specified before any statistic is
computed. For the spatial measures in the formalization, restriction retains
the masses at shared sites. For a decoded distribution over content variables,
marginalization instead sums over unwanted variables. The content model must
specify which operation its shared domain requires.
\item[A statistic with a null.] A distance between the two restricted
distributions is not yet a measurement, because decoding error alone makes that
distance positive. The statistic needs the distribution of the distance
\emph{under compatibility}---what two decoders of the same underlying content
produce given their own noise---so that incompatibility is a departure from a
stated null rather than a non-zero number. Overlapping territories supply
dependent samples, and the uncertainty must be computed accordingly.
\item[Validation where the answer is built rather than inferred.] The estimator
must be exercised on data in which compatible and incompatible overlaps are
constructed by design. The formalization supplies the configuration to target:
local states at a single common phase whose densities disagree where the
patches meet (Section~\ref{sec:compat}). An estimator that cannot separate that
configuration from its compatible counterpart at realistic decoding quality is
not yet a measurement of the property the framework needs.
\end{description}

\subsection{Three ways it fails silently}

A prior is a probability distribution supplied before the current evidence;
a posterior is the distribution after incorporating that evidence. A decoder
can bias results by pulling different inputs toward the same prior. Bias is
a systematic displacement of estimates, not just random variation.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item A decoder that shrinks both regional posteriors toward a shared prior
reports \emph{compatibility} whatever the regions encode---having replaced two
local descriptions with one global one.
\item A decoder carrying independent per-region bias reports
\emph{incompatibility} whatever the regions encode.
\item \textbf{Circularity.} A compatibility statistic computed from phases
measures phase agreement---and the distinction between phase agreement and
overlap compatibility is the entire content of E78. An observable that read
compatibility off synchrony would confirm the framework's central distinction
by assuming its negation.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The first two are opposite failures of the same component, neither visible in
the statistic itself, which is why the constructed-answer validation is a
requirement and not a refinement.

\scope
Such an observable would \emph{not} establish E78, which asserts that coherence
drives a cover the theorem is given to its relaxed equilibrium, nor the cover's
own compatibility field. It would make the consequent
measurable and leave the implication a hypothesis. It bears on neither the
recovery prediction nor the identification of the glued section with
experience. What it supplies is the missing term in the dissociation test. A
null outcome---no reachable data separating compatible from incompatible
overlaps at achievable decoding quality---is itself informative, and would
place the interpretive commitment outside experimental reach rather than merely
outside the article.

\section{Relation to other accounts}

Integrated information theories associate consciousness with irreducible
cause-and-effect organization of a system. Global workspace accounts emphasize
information becoming broadly available to other processes, often called
broadcast. Predictive processing describes perception and action through models
that predict inputs and respond to discrepancies. A generative model specifies
how hidden conditions could produce observations. These are families of
accounts, so the comparison concerns these broad emphases.

The exclusion principle in integrated information theory selects a definite
experience and system among overlapping candidates. Ignition in workspace
theories refers to a marked transition to broad availability. The combination
problem asks how purportedly separate experiential constituents could form one
experience. None of those questions is settled simply by making a physical
field continuous.

\statement
The proposal differs in emphasis from integrated information
\citep{tononi2016}, global workspace \citep{baars1997,dehaene2014}, and
predictive processing \citep{friston2010}. It specifies a candidate interaction
medium, a mathematical compatibility condition and a local self-encoding.
Uniqueness follows within a chosen cover and contracting map; the empirical
choice of those objects and their relation to experience remain open.

\scope
It does not derive the exclusion principle of integrated information, identify
every broadcast with a field transition, or derive a generative model from a
dissipation bound (Section~\ref{sec:fep}). And continuity alone does not settle
the combination problem: continuous fields can carry incompatible local
descriptions, and discrete models can admit sheaf constructions. Compatibility
is what this account offers in place of continuity. These mathematical
differences do not settle which systems have conscious experience.

\section{Glossary}

These are short reminders of explanations developed in the text. The five
terms central to the Kuramoto walkthrough are \emph{order parameter},
\emph{rotating frame}, \emph{ansatz}, \emph{stationary branch} and
\emph{ramp speed}.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6cm,style=nextline]
\item[Adiabatic tracking] Remaining close to the fixed-control solution while
the control changes slowly. Requires adjustment faster than the target moves;
critical slowing down limits it.
\item[Agency model] Specified policy, world, observation and memory-update
channels. Permits action to change future inputs; supplies no learned objective.
\item[Amplitude] Size of an oscillation; length of its signal arrow.
\item[Analytic signal] The complex signal $A e^{i\phi}$ built from a real
signal by the Hilbert transform, supplying instantaneous amplitude and phase.
\item[Ansatz] A proposed form of solution, with parameters to determine and
conditions to check. The stationary ansatz here is a von Mises density
(Section~\ref{sec:vonmises}).
\item[Atomless measure] A measure giving zero mass to every single point.
Lebesgue measure is the example; finitely-sited kernels are invisible to
integrals against it.
\item[Branch] A continuous family of solutions as a control parameter varies.
\item[Barbalat's lemma] If a function is well-behaved and its integral
converges, the function tends to zero. Not in Mathlib; proved in the
development.
\item[Bessel ratio $R(a)$] $I_1(a)/I_0(a)$, the mean of $\cos\theta$ under a von
Mises law at concentration $a$. Strictly increasing.
\item[Coboundary] A family of offsets on overlaps expressible as differences
$\phi_i-\phi_j$ of per-patch values. Kuramoto phases always give one, which is
why twisted gluing adds nothing.
\item[Compatibility] Agreement of local descriptions on their shared domains.
\item[Complete space] A space containing the limit of every Cauchy sequence,
whose terms eventually become arbitrarily close to one another.
\item[Concentration $a$] The von Mises peakedness parameter, estimated here
from the slope of the log-density on $\cos\theta$.
\item[Conditional mutual information] Dependence between two variables
remaining when a third is known, averaged over that third variable's values.
\item[Contraction] A map shrinking all distances by a factor $\lambda<1$.
\item[Coupling] An influence of one oscillator's phase on another's speed;
$K$ controls its strength in the uniform model.
\item[Cover] A collection of regions together covering the chosen whole space.
\item[Critical exponent $\beta$] The power law $r\sim(K-\Kc)^\beta$ near
threshold. Mean-field value $1/2$.
\item[Critical slowing down] Divergence of relaxation time at a continuous
transition; what limits adiabatic tracking.
\item[Data processing inequality] Processing cannot create information:
$I(X;Z)\le I(X;Y)$ for a Markov chain $X\to Y\to Z$.
\item[Dale's law] The constraint that a neuron is either excitatory or
inhibitory for all its targets.
\item[Density] A function whose integral over a region gives its probability
or mass. Its value at one point is not that point's probability.
\item[Drift] Systematic change per unit time, before random increments are added.
\item[Equilibrium] A state that can remain unchanged under fixed conditions.
For noisy dynamics, the stationary object is a probability distribution.
\item[Estimator / estimand] The rule calculating an estimate / the underlying
quantity it aims to measure.
\item[Euler--Maruyama] The basic numerical scheme for SDEs; noise scales as
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$.
\item[Feedback] Influence of the internal state, through action, on the
future environmental state that the system observes.
\item[Finite-size floor] Fluctuating sample order even for independent uniform
phases; its root-mean-square size is $1/\sqrt N$. Not a hard minimum.
\item[Fixed point] A state unchanged by a specified map: $f(x)=x$.
\item[Fokker--Planck equation] A partial differential equation (PDE), tracking
changes in a probability density across position and time. The model's
zero-current stationary equation gives the von Mises form.
\item[Frustration] Interaction preferences that cannot all be satisfied at
once. It can permit competing patterns; it does not guarantee useful memories.
\item[Germ, stalk] Data ``arbitrarily near'' a point, obtained as a colimit
over its neighbourhoods.
\item[Gluing axiom] The sheaf condition: compatible local sections assemble
uniquely into one global section.
\item[Global section] An element of $\mathcal{F}(X)$ over the whole space,
restricting to the given local data.
\item[Kernel] A function $K(x,y)$ giving interaction strength for two locations.
\item[Kibble--Zurek mechanism] Failure to follow changing equilibrium conditions
near a transition because the adjustment timescale grows. Particular scaling
predictions require a specified model and driving protocol.
\item[Landauer's principle] Under its thermodynamic assumptions, resetting one
unbiased bit costs at least $k_BT\ln2$ in heat. The general bound depends on
the entropy actually erased.
\item[Lipschitz] Bounded rate of change: $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$.
\item[Local detailed balance] The physical relation between a transition's
forward/reverse probability ratio and reservoir heat at a specified temperature.
\item[\L{}ojasiewicz estimate] An inequality bounding a functional's excess
above its minimum by its current rate of decrease. Yields exponential
convergence without compactness.
\item[Lyapunov function] A quantity that never increases along trajectories.
\item[Mean field] The population's aggregate influence. Its finite-sample
formula is exact for uniform coupling; passing to a deterministic limiting
density requires a separate justification.
\item[Measure] A rule assigning weights, such as length, area or probability,
to sets. An integral totals values using those weights.
\item[Nonidentifiability] Different model mechanisms producing the same chosen
observations, so those observations alone cannot distinguish them.
\item[Order parameter $r$] Circular resultant length,
$|\frac1N\sum_j e^{i\theta_j}|$: the length of the average phase arrow.
One means perfect alignment; zero means cancellation, which can also occur
in structured opposing groups (Section~\ref{sec:kuramoto-walkthrough}).
\item[Path law] A probability distribution over transition records. The
finite thermodynamic model compares normalized forward and reverse path laws.
\item[Phase] Position within a repeating cycle, expressed as an angle.
\item[Phase diffusion $D$] Random timing spread: noise alone contributes
increment variance $2D\Delta t$ over duration $\Delta t$.
\item[Phase locking] Constant relative phases. Perfect in-phase locking is the
special case in which all phases coincide.
\item[Pitchfork bifurcation] A transition where a branch of solutions emerges
continuously from zero as a parameter crosses threshold, the old solution
persisting.
\item[Policy] A rule assigning action probabilities to internal states.
It may be specified directly; the word does not imply learning or optimality.
\item[Preconnected / connected] Topological notions of ``in one piece'';
continuous images of connected sets are connected---the whole proof of the
domain-wall theorem.
\item[Presheaf / sheaf] Data assigned to open sets with restriction maps; a
sheaf additionally satisfies the gluing axiom.
\item[Propagation of chaos] Asymptotic independence of particles in an
interacting system as $N\to\infty$.
\item[Ramp speed] Rate of change of a control parameter. Here $v=dK/dt$;
a finite ramp gives the population finite time to adjust (Section~\ref{sec:ramp}).
\item[Relaxation time] Timescale for returning toward a settled state after a
disturbance.
\item[Replica] One repeat of a stochastic simulation with its own random seed.
\item[Resultant length] See order parameter.
\item[Rotating frame] Coordinates turning at a chosen angular speed;
$\phi_i=\theta_i-\Omega t$ removes a shared natural frequency while preserving
relative phases and coherence (Section~\ref{sec:rotating}).
\item[Self-consistency] The density generated by a proposed mean field returns
the same mean field when its phases are averaged.
\item[Sliced Wasserstein distance] A tractable distance between
high-dimensional distributions, computed by averaging one-dimensional optimal
transport over random projections. Used---and shown to have a finite-sample
floor---in the propagation-of-chaos diagnostics.
\item[Stability] Whether small disturbances grow or decay under the dynamics.
Existence of a stationary solution alone does not establish it.
\item[Stationary branch] Stationary solutions collected across choices of a
fixed control parameter. It is a graph of solutions versus coupling, not a
time course (Section~\ref{sec:fold}).
\item[Stationary density] A probability pattern unchanged over time at fixed
controls in the chosen frame, even while individual oscillators move.
\item[Sufficient statistic] A function of the data carrying all the
information about a parameter. For von Mises it is $\sum_j\cos\theta_j$, i.e.\
$r$---which is why an ML concentration estimate would make the collapse test
circular.
\item[Trajectory] The path a system follows through its states over time.
\item[Vacuum manifold] The set of lowest-energy field configurations. Its
topology classifies the defects.
\item[von Mises distribution] The circular analogue of the Gaussian,
$\rho(\theta)\propto e^{a\cos\theta}$.
\item[Winding number] The integer count of how many times a circle-valued phase
wraps around a loop; non-zero winding obstructs a global real-valued lift.
\end{description}
