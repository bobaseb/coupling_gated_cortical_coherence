Unity requires physically enforced agreement

Why one physical state is not the same as many records kept in step

Sebastian Bobadilla-Suarez · Independent Researcher · ORCID 0000-0002-5951-0772 · sebastian.bobadilla.s@gmail.com

The companion paper supplies the definition of unity used here, agreement of local descriptions wherever they overlap, and the way that agreement is measured. This paper adds a condition on how the agreement is produced.

Abstract

A conscious scene is experienced as one scene, although many brain regions contribute to it. We propose that unity requires physically enforced agreement: changes in one region that exceed its own noise must reach overlapping regions and pull them toward agreement within a percept's time. The test is applied where each carrier of content is read. Machine-checked results show that, under noise, a small change moves content read from bits by at most the probability that a bit comes near its threshold. That bound is attained; bits read well clear of their thresholds fail the response test. In a fitted model of membranes recorded in awake mice, at least one of 10 independent carriers reaches a sensitive state within a percept's time with probability at least 0.91, while a clocked latch's read value never does. The paper considers a fast digital replacement with resolution finer than biological noise, then proposes reconnecting two cortical regions through analog and quantized loops. Bandwidth and quantization are varied separately and compared with a model based on bandwidth, delay and nonlinearity. Reports of unity require an explicit assumption linking them to experience. Simulations show that the loops can be matched on the information they carry; the coupling and experiential tests require the proposed preparation. The condition also predicts how fast and how far cortical coupling must restore agreement.

Two ways for many parts to agree

Physically enforced

Like weights joined by springs. Each part's graded state pulls on its neighbours. A change that exceeds the part's own noise moves the others, and the coupling restores agreement. The parts are coordinates of one physical state.

Computed

Like separate ledgers kept consistent by a clerk. Each part keeps its own record, held in place by a margin, and a rule compares and corrects the records at discrete steps. Below the margin the records vary independently. They are many systems kept in step by a protocol.

A clocked digital chip keeps its bits as separate records, and its program can bring their contents into agreement. The paper's premise asks whether the carriers of overlapping contents also respond to one another above noise and restore agreement within the content's time window.

Premise P (physical unity). A system's contents are unified only if, for every pair of regions whose contents overlap and in every window of the contents' own time scale, some content-bearing carrier of each responds gradedly, above noise, to a carrier of the other, and their coupling shrinks disagreement within that window. Different carriers and moments may serve the two directions.

P is proposed as a necessary condition for unity of contents already taken to be conscious. It specifies the physical grain at which carriers and their readers must respond. A simulation can reproduce a coupled field's course while internal bits held within their margins fail this response test. A converter that samples a graded signal finer than its noise is judged by the same test at its actual downstream reader.

The margin lemma and its noisy form

A bit is read through a noise margin: any voltage well above the threshold reads as 1, so small wobbles change nothing. That margin is what makes digital hardware reliable, and it is also what separates it from a coupled field. If content is read through a margin, small enough physical changes never reach it. The proof is short: continuity carries a small enough neighbourhood of each region's physical state into the neighbourhood on which the readout is constant. The result covers any content read from bits, whatever algorithm the bits run.

Real hardware is noisy, so the paper states the criterion on probabilities. The noisy margin theorem bounds the change in the next content's distribution by the margin exception probability: the chance that a noisy successor comes close to a threshold. The bound is attained. Near approaches count even when the bit remains correct, so this probability can exceed the logical error rate. A readout fails the response test when that probability is below the declared fluctuation yardstick and a change larger than the source's noise still fits inside its margin. A cortical spike time can pass the same test near threshold: one weak synaptic input shifts it by more than its jitter.

Each gate does pull a disturbed voltage back toward its rail, but only toward its own logical value. That restoring pull keeps each bit inside its margin and pulls no region toward agreement with another, so the chip's records stay separate even where its physics settles.

What P admits and what it excludes

The line P draws is between symbols, contents read through a margin, and physical contents, graded quantities the system's own dynamics reads. It concerns how a substrate is organised, not what it is made of.

Spike counts latched at a clock edgeColumn currents of an analog crossbar settling through shared conductances
A neural circuit read only from all-or-none events latched far from thresholdGraded currents of an analog circuit or phases of coupled oscillators, when their readers and restoring coupling pass the test

A switching node can be sensitive at its voltage threshold, but a clocked latch reads its restored bit outside the setup-and-hold window. The verdict depends on the actual reader, its noise and its integration time.

How far a coupling reaches

If each patch of cortex is tied only to its near neighbours, agreement passes from hand to hand and leaks away with distance. Picture the sheet as an electrical network in which each coupling is a wire: phase disagreement between two sites is controlled by the resistance between them. Under short-range coupling the bound grows with the logarithm of distance; sufficiently long-range coupling can sustain agreement across the sheet. Turning phase agreement into agreement in content requires an encoder connecting the two. Across centimetres that step rests on the companion paper's bridge assumption. The paper also predicts restoration within the content window and a cortical relaxation scale of 166 ms.

What would refute it

  1. Separate coupling from information. Reconnect two regions through an analog or a quantized loop, varying bandwidth and quantization separately. Select a pair in which the analog arm carries no more estimated transfer entropy or task information than the quantized arm. Certify the carrier response and restoration at each loop's downstream reader, including its memory and integration time. The two loops can match on those information summaries while a competing model based on bandwidth, delay and nonlinearity still predicts different outcomes.
  2. A weak nudge should spread. Intervene on a content-bearing variable by more than its own noise and below its threshold. Within the content window, some carrier in an overlapping region should respond by the declared yardstick, and the shared variable should move toward agreement. A measured upper bound below that yardstick challenges P's sensitivity condition.
  3. Agreement should be restored. After a perturbation, agreement should come back with a pull proportional to the perturbation, below any threshold as well as above.
  4. The field's causal cone. Re-agreement faster than synapses allow would reveal a non-synaptic part of the coupling at millimetre distances. At centimetre distances the proposed route is synaptic.

Assumption R links calibrated human reports of one simultaneous field to experienced unity under these interventions. Animal matching tests integration and the physical mechanism; it does not establish the experiential claim. Human reports are calibrated against simultaneous and alternating displays, and a switching model is tested on held-out reports; if the models cannot be distinguished at the measured time scale, the experiential comparison is inconclusive. Preserved unity reports when a bridge is certified to fail P challenge P and R together. An analog advantage favours P only if the rival model fails its predeclared prediction.

Scope

P is a proposed necessary condition for unity, not a derived result or a criterion for which contents are conscious. Its response bounds and bridge test do not settle why physical processes are experienced. The human bridge test depends on Assumption R and on controlling visibility, attention and report ability; the proposed animal preparation first tests the physical mechanism and behavioural integration. The paper's limitations section gives the assumptions and limits in detail.
