Unity requires physically enforced agreement

Why one physical state is not the same as many records kept in step

Abstract

Human conscious experience is unified, and we argue that unity requires physically enforced agreement: within every window of a content's time scale, some graded carrier of each region must register the graded state of every region it overlaps, above its own noise, through a coupling that pulls their contents together. The premise is a functional condition stated at the grain of graded physical dynamics, finer than the grain of an algorithm: at that grain cortex and digital chips come apart, while at the grain of an algorithm they do not. It makes a prediction that the algorithmic grain does not: when two cortical regions are bridged by an analog and by a quantized loop carrying the same information, unity follows the analog loop. We prove that under noise, content read from bits responds to a change of any region by at most its error rate, a bound that is attained, so a digital machine, clocked or clockless, fails the premise whatever algorithm it runs. Measured by one protocol over the states each occupies, a noisy cortical membrane passes near its threshold and reaches such a state within a percept's time scale, while a logic node passes at none of its states. On a noisy sheet of coupled oscillators, the coupling's reach decides how far phase agreement holds. We state the preparation that runs the bridge and the cortical outcomes that would refute the premise.

Two ways for many parts to agree

Physically enforced

Like weights joined by springs. Each part's state is a graded physical quantity that pulls on its neighbours continuously, so no part can move, however little, without pulling its neighbours after it. The parts are coordinates of one physical state.

Computed

Like separate ledgers kept consistent by a clerk. Each part keeps its own record, held in place by a margin, and a rule compares and corrects the records at discrete steps. Below the margin the records vary independently. They are many systems kept in step by a protocol.

A clocked digital chip is of the second kind: its bits are separate records, and whatever agreement its program produces is computed. The paper's premise is that the unity of an experience is oneness of the first kind.

Premise P (physical unity). A system's contents are unified only if each region's contents are read from graded carriers and, within every window of the contents' own time scale, some carrier of each region depends on the graded physical state of every region it overlaps, above the noise floor and through a coupling that contracts their disagreement.

P is a necessary condition for unity. It is itself a functional condition, stated at the grain of graded physical dynamics rather than of an algorithm. The two grains classify cortex alike and part on machines: a perfect simulation of a coupled cortical field reproduces the field's behaviour and is unified at the algorithm's grain, yet fails P, because its agreement is computed rather than enforced.

The margin lemma and its noisy form

A bit is read through a noise margin: any voltage well above the threshold reads as 1, so small wobbles change nothing. That margin is what makes digital hardware reliable, and it is also what separates it from a coupled field. If content is read through a margin, small enough physical changes never reach it. The proof is short: continuity carries a small enough neighbourhood of each region's physical state into the neighbourhood on which the readout is constant. The result covers any content read from bits, whatever algorithm the bits run.

Real hardware is noisy, so the paper states the criterion on probabilities, and the noisy form of the lemma carries the argument: under noise, a change to one region below the margin moves anything computed from bits by at most the error rate, and the bound is attained. A small change to one region therefore reaches a digital content only through the chance of a bit error, which for an ordinary logic node lies many orders of magnitude below the content's natural fluctuation. A cortical spike time passes the same test: one typical weak synaptic input shifts it by more than its jitter.

Each gate does pull a disturbed voltage back toward its rail, but only toward its own logical value. That restoring pull keeps each bit inside its margin and pulls no region toward agreement with another, so the chip's records stay separate even where its physics settles.

What P admits and what it excludes

The line P draws is between symbols, contents read through a margin, and physical contents, graded quantities the system's own dynamics reads. It concerns how a substrate is organised, not what it is made of.

Spike counts latched at a clock edgeColumn currents of an analog crossbar settling through shared conductances
A neural circuit read only from all-or-none events latched far from thresholdPhases of coupled electronic or optical oscillators

The clock is a noise margin in time: it removes from a digital circuit exactly the graded timing that spikes keep.

How far a coupling reaches

If each patch of cortex is tied only to its near neighbours, agreement passes from hand to hand and leaks away with distance. Picture the sheet as an electrical network in which each coupling is a wire: the disagreement between two sites is proportional to the resistance between them. Classical results on resistance then decide how far phase agreement reaches: under short-range coupling it fades with the logarithm of distance, and a coupling whose strength falls off more slowly than r−4 keeps it at every distance. An encoder that reads phases relative to its neighbourhood turns phase agreement into agreement in content, paying only for the field's local roughness; across centimetres that step rests on the companion paper's bridge assumption. In cortex the long-range coupling is synaptic.

What would refute it

  1. Separate coupling from information. In every manipulation of cortex so far, graded coupling and information flow move together. Bridge two disconnected regions through either an analog loop or a quantized loop carrying the same information. Unity that follows the analog loop is evidence that unity tracks the finer, graded grain; unity that is the same under both counts against P.
  2. A weak nudge should spread. Sub-threshold stimulation of one region should shift the graded content decoded in an overlapping region, toward agreement. No shift, at strengths that measurably move the stimulated region, counts against P.
  3. Agreement should be restored. After a perturbation, agreement should come back with a pull proportional to the perturbation, below any threshold as well as above.
  4. The field's causal cone. Re-agreement between nearby regions faster than synapses allow would reveal a non-synaptic part of the coupling and measure its reach directly.

The evidence about experience is fixed in advance: integrated responses across the regions when the task requires it, and reports of a single field of experience spanning them.

Scope

P is a premise, not a derived result: the formal results show what follows from it, not that it holds. It is necessary for unity and not sufficient for consciousness, since coupled pendulums satisfy it. Human data can falsify P but cannot on their own tell the graded grain from the algorithmic one until coupling and information flow are separated in a human preparation. The paper's limitations section lists every assumption in one place.
