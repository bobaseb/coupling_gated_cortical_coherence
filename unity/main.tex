\begin{abstract}
We prove that a content read through a noise margin, under continuous physical
dynamics, depends on the graded state of no region, and we derive how far a
physical coupling enforces agreement across a noisy sheet of coupled
oscillators: coupling whose strength falls off more slowly than $r^{-4}$ keeps
agreement at every distance, and coupling of finite second moment, short-range
in effect, lets it fade without limit. These results separate two ways in which
many local descriptions of one situation can agree. In one, a coupling ties the
graded state of each region to the regions it overlaps, so that any change in
one, however small, pulls the others toward agreement, and the regions are
parts of one physical state. In the other, found in a clocked digital chip, the
parts are separate records, its bits, each held in place by a noise margin that
makes it ignore every change smaller than the margin, and agreement is computed
by a rule applied at each clock step. By the theorem, content read from bits is
never physically enforced, whatever algorithm the bits run. Thermal noise
leaves this intact. A small change in one region reaches a digital content only
through the chance of a bit error, far below the content's own fluctuation,
while a single weak synaptic input moves a cortical spike by more than its
jitter. Human conscious experience is unified, and we argue that unity requires
its agreement to be physically enforced. The theorem is short because the
definitions carry its weight, and we argue for where they place the criterion.
The premise predicts that unity follows graded coupling when coupling and
information flow are separated experimentally, and we state the outcomes in
cortex that would refute it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A conscious scene is experienced as one scene. Many parts of the brain
contribute to it, each describing part of the same situation, and their
descriptions agree. This paper asks what kind of agreement that has to be.

Many parts can agree in two ways. In the first, the parts are tied together
physically, like weights joined by springs. Each part's state is a graded
physical quantity that pulls on its neighbours continuously, so no part can
move, by however little, without pulling its neighbours after it. The parts are
then coordinates of a single physical state, and they cannot vary independently
at any scale. We call this agreement \emph{physically enforced}. In the second,
each part keeps its own record, like separate ledgers kept consistent by a
clerk. A rule compares the records at discrete steps and corrects them where
they differ. Each record is held in place by a margin, and below that margin the
records vary independently of each other and of everything beneath them. We call
this agreement \emph{computed}. The first is one system in the sense physics
uses, a single state space with coupled coordinates. The second is many systems
kept consistent by a protocol. A clocked digital chip is of the second kind: its
bits are separate records, and whatever agreement its program produces is
computed. Our premise is that the unity of an experience is oneness of the first
kind.

\paragraph{Premise P (physical unity).} A system's contents are unified only if
each region's contents are read from graded carriers and, within every window of
the contents' own time scale, some carrier of each region depends on the graded
physical state of every region it overlaps, above the noise floor and through a
coupling that contracts their disagreement.

P is a necessary condition for unity. It is itself a functional condition, since
it picks out unity by what carriers do to one another, and it fixes that role at
the grain of graded physical dynamics instead of at the grain of an algorithm. It
therefore rejects computational functionalism about unity, the view that only
the computation matters: a perfect simulation of a coupled field
reproduces the field's content-level dynamics and still fails P, because its
agreement is computed. The reason to hold P is that unity decides how many
subjects there are, and parts are one system when they interact at every scale
above their own noise (Section~\ref{sec:enforcement}). Physical systems are noisy, so P is read on the
probability laws of contents. Graded dependence then counts above the noise
floor: every sub-threshold change larger than the noise of what a carrier depends
on must move the carrier's next content by at least as much as that content
fluctuates. A noisy carrier meets this near its threshold, so P asks it of some
carriers within each window rather than of every carrier at every
instant.

The paper makes three claims:
\begin{enumerate}
\claim{sec:enforcement}{Content read through a noise margin, under continuous physical dynamics, depends on the graded state of no region, so content read from bits is not physically enforced, whatever algorithm the bits run. Measured by one protocol over the states each occupies, a cortical membrane passes the noise-floor test near its threshold and visits such states within every window of a percept's time scale, while a logic node passes at none of its states}
\claim{sec:reach}{On a noisy coupled sheet, the reach of the coupling sets how fast agreement fades with distance: coupling that falls off more slowly than $r^{-4}$ keeps agreement at every distance, and coupling of finite second moment lets it fade without limit}
\claim{sec:tests}{P makes testable predictions for cortex: a weak, sub-threshold perturbation of one region shifts the graded content decoded in an overlapping region toward agreement, and when two regions are bridged at matched information flow, unity follows the bridge that couples them gradedly}
\end{enumerate}

Appendix~\ref{app:formal} maps each one to its declaration.

\section{Unity as agreement over a cover of contents}
\label{sec:cover}

Think of an atlas. Each page maps one patch of ground, neighbouring pages
overlap, and where they overlap they show the same roads. Pages that agree on
every overlap fit together into one map of the whole territory. We treat the
brain's regions the same way: each describes part of a shared situation, and
unity is the property that these local descriptions agree wherever they
overlap. When they do, they determine a single global state.

Synchrony is not unity. Two regions can oscillate in phase while one encodes a
stimulus at one location and the other at another, so equal phases coexist
with incompatible descriptions. Unity is a property of what overlapping regions
describe, and synchrony is at most a route to it.

A region's \emph{content} is a variable that the system itself reads, not merely
one an outside observer can decode. Two conditions define it: the regions this
region drives respond to it, and it determines the quantity the region
represents, such as a location or an orientation. A variable that meets both is
a \emph{carrier} of content. A variable that only an observer can decode is a
trace. The supply current of a digital circuit carries enough of its logical
state that a secret key can be recovered from it \citep{kocher1999}, yet no gate
of the circuit reads the current, so the current is a trace. In cortex the
membrane potential and the timing of spikes are carriers, because the
postsynaptic cells integrate them.

Agreement is measured between regions whose contents represent a shared
variable. The regions, and the content variable of each, are fixed by this
causal role and by decodability before agreement is measured, so that nobody can
make the measurement trivial by choosing the regions after the fact.

This definition of unity, and the measurement of agreement the tests below use,
come from a companion paper \citep{bobadillasuarez2026coherence}. It proves that
compatible local descriptions determine one global state, shows that phase
coherence limits their disagreement only through shared encoders of bounded
sensitivity, and measures agreement with decoders fitted independently on two
regions, counting only the agreement in excess of what their separate
accuracies imply. P adds a condition on how that agreement is produced.

\section{Physical enforcement versus routed agreement}
\label{sec:enforcement}

The argument needs five definitions. Each is stated in words first.

A system has a physical state, its \emph{micro state} $x=(x_i)_i$, made up of
the states $x_i$ of its regions. One step of its physics carries $x$ to $f(x)$.
A \emph{content map} $\pi$ reads each region's content off the micro state. The
content level is \emph{closed} when the next content can be computed from the
current content alone, without looking at the micro state:
$\pi\circ f = g\circ\pi$ for some content dynamics $g$. A digital computer is
built so that its logical level is closed in this sense.

A readout has a \emph{noise margin} on a set of states when every state in the
set can be nudged a little without changing what is read, that is, when each
has a neighbourhood on which the readout is constant. A bit is the standard
example: any voltage well above the threshold reads as 1.

Content \emph{depends gradedly} on a region when every change to that region,
however small, can move the next content. Formally, no neighbourhood of the
region's micro state leaves the next content unchanged while the other regions
are held fixed.

The \emph{overlap discrepancy} measures how much the contents of overlapping
regions disagree. It \emph{contracts} near a state when one step of $f$ shrinks
it by a factor below one throughout a neighbourhood of that state. Asking for a
whole neighbourhood matters: perturbations too small to cross any threshold are
then pulled back toward agreement along with larger ones.

Agreement is \emph{physically enforced} at a state when content depends
gradedly on each region there and the overlap discrepancy contracts near it.
P requires this at the states the system occupies while its contents are
unified.

\paragraph{The margin theorem.} In words: if content is read through a margin,
small enough physical changes never reach it. Precisely, if the micro dynamics
is continuous and carries a state into a margin's operating set, the next
content depends gradedly on no region's micro state. The proof is short:
continuity carries a small enough neighbourhood of each region's micro state
into the neighbourhood on which the readout is constant. A thresholded
continuous quantity has a margin away from its threshold, and so does a finite
word of such readouts, so the theorem covers any content read from bits. The
definitions carry the weight of the result, and the rest of this section argues
that they sit at the right level.

\paragraph{Why the criterion sits below the content level.} At the content
level a computer can imitate a coupled field exactly. A digital system running
a consensus algorithm, or integrating the equations of a coupled field, has a
content map whose next step shrinks the disagreement between overlapping
regions just as the field does. A criterion stated on the content dynamics
would put that system in the same class as the field. P is stated instead on
how content depends on graded physical state, and there the two differ. The
field's content moves with every graded change in the regions it overlaps. The
simulation's content is read from bits held inside their margins, and moves
with none of them.

This is the simulation objection. A perfect simulation of a coupled cortical
field reproduces the field's content-level trajectory and fails P, because its
agreement is computed from contents and not enforced by the graded state that
carries them. P denies the simulation unity, and the denial needs a reason.

\paragraph{Why unity tracks enforcement.} Unity settles how many subjects there
are. A unified experience is had by one subject and a divided one by two or
more, which is what the split-brain debate of Section~\ref{sec:tests} asks of a
single skull. Whatever makes contents unified therefore also decides whether
they belong to one system or to several. Agreement at the content level
cannot settle that count. Put the records of one program in different
buildings, stretch the clerk's step to a year, or let one processor visit the
records in turn: the content trajectory is unchanged while the physical system
that realises it goes from one chip to several machines to one record at a
time. Block's nation, whose citizens run a brain's functional organisation by
radio for an hour \citep{block1978}, is the same program spread over a country.
A criterion stated on content-level agreement gives all of these the same
number of subjects, without any physical fact to consult in doing so.

\paragraph{Counting systems at the noise floor.} Physics counts systems by
interaction: parts form one system when their state does not factor into
independent coordinates. Stated that crudely, the criterion makes every chip one
system, since wires, a shared supply and crosstalk couple all of its voltages,
and it makes Block's nation one system through its radios. The criterion needs
a scale, and the parts supply it. A difference smaller than a part's own noise
is erased by the part's own fluctuations, so the part does not hold it as a
state. A difference larger than that noise is one the part holds, and the
question is whether the other parts register it. Two carriers are one system
when every change of one above its noise moves the other by at least the other's
own fluctuation, and two systems when some such change leaves the other unmoved.
This is the noise-floor reading of graded dependence given below, and the
identity is proved: for carriers whose contents overlap, each depends gradedly
above the noise floor on every carrier it overlaps exactly when every
overlapping pair is one system in this sense. A region holds many carriers, and
a noisy carrier registers another only while it sits near its own threshold, as
measured below. Two regions are therefore one system when some carrier of each
registers some carrier of the other, and P asks this within every window of the
contents' time scale. Asking it of some carriers is strictly weaker than asking
it of all: a state exists at which the first holds and the second fails. Both
ends of the scale are the parts' own noise, so nobody chooses it to separate
brains from chips. A chip fails it by design. A bit's margin spans
\ueMarginToNoise{} of its noise amplitudes, and no other bit registers a change
anywhere in that range except through the error rate. By the noisy margin
theorem below, once that rate is under the fluctuation and each carrier has a
change above its noise inside the margin, no carrier of any region registers any
other, so the weaker form fails exactly as the stronger one does, and each part
is a system of its own. Its wires pass on only the changes that cross a
threshold, which is the rule applied at each clock step, so between thresholds,
where a working chip spends nearly all of its time, it is as many systems as it
has bits. The count looks strange because ordinary usage counts by program, and
one program uses all of the bits. Counted by physics, each bit is restored to
its own rail by its own gates, and what keeps the bits in step is the rule the
program applies, a protocol among separate records. The citizens of Block's nation likewise register each other only
through the messages they exchange. Cortex passes within every window, as shown
below. P asks exactly this of the regions whose contents are unified. The
premise is thus the conjunction of two more familiar ones: a unified experience
belongs to one system, and regions are one system when their carriers interact
at every scale their own noise lets them hold apart. It stands on the same
footing as a workspace
theory's identification of consciousness with broadcast, as a premise whose
consequences can be tested, which Section~\ref{sec:tests} does.

\paragraph{Categories.} Categorical contents have margins in every substrate,
brains included. The dominant percept in binocular rivalry, a perceived
category, or any class picked as the largest of several values stays the same
on each side of its boundary. Away from the boundary, the margin theorem
therefore denies it graded dependence in cortex exactly as in silicon. So P is
stated on the graded variables from which categories are read, such as a
location, an orientation, a time or the relative strength of competing
percepts. A category meets P through the variable that carries it, and depends
gradedly on a region only where that variable does. This opens no route for a
digital machine. Every variable it computes, whether a number, a vector of
numbers or a category, is a function of its bits, and after any number of steps
that keep its bits away from their thresholds such a function depends gradedly
on no region. The voltages beneath the bits are not carriers in the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:cover}. An observer may decode the machine's contents from
them, but the machine itself reads them only through the bits, at that step and
at every later one.

\paragraph{Noise.} Real physics is noisy, and noise changes the question. A
gate's chance of flipping is a smooth function of its input voltage and is never
exactly zero, so the \emph{probability} of a digital machine's next content
depends, however slightly, on every region. Read on exact contents, the margin
theorem would then pick out a difference lying below the noise floor of both
substrates. P is therefore read on probabilities. The \emph{response} of a
content to a region at scale $\eta$ is the largest change that moving the region
by at most $\eta$ makes to the probability of any outcome of the next content, a
total-variation distance. As the yardstick for a large response we take the one
that a shift of one standard deviation produces in a Gaussian carrier,
$2\Phi(1/2)-1=\ueFluctuationTV$: a response above it moves the content by as
much as the content naturally fluctuates. Graded dependence above the noise
floor asks the response to reach this fluctuation for every change of the region
that is larger than its own noise and smaller than the change that crosses one
of its thresholds. When the region moves a Gaussian carrier linearly, a larger
change moves the content at least as much as a smaller one, so the criterion
is met exactly when a change of one noise amplitude reaches the fluctuation.
The constants are a Gaussian identity and the system's own
scales, and none of them is fitted to either substrate.

The margin theorem has a noisy form. Let each realization of the noise fix an
$L$-Lipschitz step of the micro dynamics, one that stretches no distance by
more than a factor $L$. Let the content be constant within radius $r$ of the
noisy successor, except on a set of realizations of probability at most
$\varepsilon$. Then a change of one region smaller than $r/L$ moves the
probability of every outcome of the next content by at most $\varepsilon$. For
anything computed from bits, $\varepsilon$ is the probability that some bit of
the noisy successor lands within $\delta$ of its threshold, and the bound is
attained.

\paragraph{A logic gate in numbers.} On a logic node of \ueNodeCapFf{}~fF at
room temperature the thermal noise is \ueThermalNoiseMv{}~mV
\citep{johnson1928,nyquist1928}, and the textbook inverter's margin is
\ueMarginToNoise{} times that. After a change of one noise amplitude, the
probability of a flip, which bounds the response, is below
$10^{-\ueErrorOrders}$ \citep{kish2002}. The response reaches the fluctuation
only within \ueGradedWindowPercent\% of the margin's width from the threshold,
where the bit is in the middle of switching.

\paragraph{A neuron in numbers.} A spike time passes the same test. Suppose an
input $\Delta V$, too small to fire the cell on its own, arrives while the
membrane potential is rising through threshold at rate $\dot V$. It moves the
spike by $\Delta V/\dot V$. Voltage noise $\sigma_V$ jitters the spike by
$\sigma_V/\dot V$. The shift therefore exceeds the jitter exactly when
$\Delta V$ exceeds $\sigma_V$, whatever the slope, and spike generation adds
little noise of its own \citep{mainen1995}. A unitary excitatory potential
between cortical pyramidal cells averages \ueEpspMv{}~mV, with a coefficient of
variation of \ueEpspCv{} \citep{markram1997}, and the membrane noise near
threshold is \ueMembraneNoiseMv{}~mV \citep{jacobson2005}. Counting the input's
own variability as noise, one such input shifts the spike by
\ueCorticalShiftToJitter{} times its jitter, a response of \ueCorticalTV{}.
Every input larger than \ueEpspPassMv{}~mV passes, up to the input that fires
the cell.

\paragraph{A membrane over the states it occupies.} That estimate holds at the
moment the membrane rises through threshold. Measured over every state a
membrane occupies, by the protocol applied to the logic node, it holds near
threshold and nowhere else. We model the membrane as a leaky integrate-and-fire
cell driven by white noise, with time constants of \uOccTauFast{} to
\uOccTauSlow{}~ms, spanning the in vivo high-conductance state
\citep{destexhe1999,destexhe2003}, and stationary rates of \uOccRateLow{} to
\uOccRateHigh{}~Hz, spanning the sparse rates of awake cortex
\citep{hromadka2008}. Its next content is the time of its first spike within
\uOccNextShort{} or \uOccNextLong{}~ms, and every change of one to
\uOccShiftMax{} noise amplitudes, in either direction, must move that content by
the fluctuation. Propagating the membrane's probability exactly on a voltage
grid (Appendix~\ref{app:occupancy}), it sits on average \uOccDistanceMin{} to
\uOccDistanceMax{} noise amplitudes below threshold and passes at
\uOccPassMinPercent{} to \uOccPassMaxPercent\% of the states it occupies. A bit
restored to a rail at the logic node's margin passes at none of its states, and
its largest response over them is $10^{-\uOccBitOrders}$. No binary readout
passes at any margin, because the fluctuation is the largest response a
threshold under Gaussian noise can give to a change of one noise amplitude; only
a graded content, such as a spike time, can exceed it.

A membrane passes at a given instant rarely and within a window often. A
stationary membrane enters a passing state within \uOccContentLongMs{}~ms, the
integration time that precedes a conscious percept, with probability
\uOccHitMinLong{} to \uOccHitMaxLong{}, including the case in which the region
it depends on supplies only \uOccShareMin{} of its membrane's noise variance. If
two regions are joined by \uOccCarriersFew{} carriers that pass independently,
some carrier passes in such a window with probability at least
\uOccLinkFewLong{}; with \uOccCarriersMid{}, a window without one occurs less
than once in $10^{\uOccMissOrdersMidLong}$, and within \uOccContentShortMs{}~ms
the probability is at least \uOccLinkMidShort{}. At a single instant, even
\uOccCarriersMany{} such carriers link two regions with probability as low as
\uOccInstantLinkMin{}, which is why P is stated per window. The window bound is
proved for independent carriers, and the probability that no carrier links two
regions falls geometrically with their number. Reading the spike time through a
clock of \uOccLatchShortMs{} or \uOccLatchLongMs{}~ms leaves the passing
fraction unchanged. What separates the membrane from the bit is the distance
from threshold at which each carrier is held, a few noise amplitudes against
\ueMarginToNoise{}.

\section{How far a physical coupling enforces agreement}
\label{sec:reach}

P asks the coupling to contract disagreement between every pair of regions
whose contents are unified, and the regions a single scene joins lie up to
\ueFibreCm{}~cm apart, so P can hold for a whole scene only if the coupling
enforces agreement at those distances. The intuition is simple. If each patch of cortex is tied only to its near
neighbours, agreement has to pass from hand to hand, and a little of it leaks
away at every hand, so distant patches drift apart. If there are enough direct
ties across long distances, agreement can hold everywhere. This section makes
that precise for a noisy sheet of coupled oscillators and finds where the line
between the two falls.

\paragraph{An electrical picture.} Picture the sheet as an electrical network:
each coupling between two sites is a wire, with a conductance equal to the
coupling's strength. The \emph{effective resistance} between two sites is the
resistance a meter would read between them. When the oscillators share one
natural frequency, the noisy sheet is a gradient system, and in the harmonic
approximation the variance of the phase difference between two sites is the
noise strength times their effective resistance. Disagreement is therefore
resistance: sites that are well connected, directly or through many parallel
paths, agree.

\paragraph{Short-range and long-range coupling.} How the coupling strength falls
off with distance is called its \emph{kernel}. Under short-range coupling the
resistance grows as the logarithm of distance, with a coefficient inversely
proportional to the kernel's second moment, a measure of its width. The
logarithmic growth is a lower bound for every kernel of bounded range, so
agreement that does not fade with distance needs coupling of unbounded range. A
kernel whose tail falls off more slowly than $r^{-4}$ supplies it: such a tail
bounds the resistance at every distance. Both results match classical theory.
The logarithm is the Gaussian form of the fact that short-range coupling cannot
give true long-range order in two dimensions \citep{mermin1966}. The threshold
$r^{-4}$ is where a random walk with the kernel's jumps passes from returning to
its start to escaping \citep{chung1951,doyle1984}, and where the plane rotator
gains true long-range order at low noise \citep{kunz1976}.
Appendix~\ref{app:reach} gives the arguments and the classical correspondence.

\paragraph{What a finite cortex can tell apart.} Simulated sheets follow the
harmonic identity in the ordered phase, and the kernel's width sets both the
order--disorder crossover and the rate at which agreement fades
(Appendix~\ref{app:reach}). Across a cortex of finite size, a wide short-range
kernel and a slowly falling tail therefore differ only by a slowly growing
logarithm. Telling them apart takes a measurement of the kernel's second moment
as well as of its tail exponent.

\paragraph{Three facts that need no approximation.} First, adding coupling never
raises the effective resistance, so a long-range tail added to a short-range
kernel can only improve agreement, whatever its shape. Second, a direct coupling
between two sites caps their resistance at its reciprocal, at any distance.
Third, a Lipschitz encoder, one whose output changes by at most a fixed multiple
$L$ of any change in its input, carries phase agreement over to content
agreement: the mean-square content discrepancy is at most $L^2$ times the
mean-square phase difference. Combined with the harmonic identity, an encoder of
Lipschitz constant $L$ at noise strength $D$ has root-mean-square content
discrepancy at most $L\sqrt{D\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}}$ between two sites of effective
resistance $R_{\mathrm{eff}}$.

\paragraph{Content can agree where phases differ.} Picture a travelling wave
sweeping across the sheet. Every site has a different phase, yet every site
sees the same local pattern: the same wave passing at the same speed. An encoder
that reads the phases of a small neighbourhood relative to the phase at its
centre reports the same content at every site of such a plane wave, whatever its
gradient, and at every site of a winding on a ring or torus. For a wave plus a
departure from it, the content discrepancy between two sites of a Lipschitz
relative encoder is at most its Lipschitz constant times the departure's
relative readings at the two sites. The gradient of the wave and the
distance between the sites are absent from it. Travelling waves and windings,
the configurations that attain the worst case of a hop-by-hop chain bound, cost
a relative encoder nothing. What it pays for is the local roughness of the
field.

\section{The unity window}
\label{sec:window}

P asks the coupling to contract disagreement, and a contraction that finishes
after the content has changed enforces agreement only on a content that has
already been replaced. This section states when the contraction finishes in
time. Agreement between two regions has a timing problem at both ends. Too early, and
the influence of one region has not yet reached the other. Too late, and the
content has already changed. Between the two lies a window, and unity at a given
time scale requires that window to be open.

\paragraph{The early edge: signals must arrive.} Agreement cannot arrive before
the coupling's causal cone allows. Precisely, a report at one site that tracks a
variable at another to within $\varepsilon$, for two values of the variable more
than $2\varepsilon$ apart, is possible after $T$ rounds of the coupling only if
the second site lies in the first site's causal past for $T$ rounds.

\paragraph{The late edge: disagreement must settle.} Disagreement decays at the
relaxation rate of the coupling. If the coupling shrinks the overlap discrepancy
by a factor $\kappa<1$ at every step, on a set of states it keeps, a discrepancy
that starts at most $d_0$ is at most $\varepsilon$ from step
$\log(d_0/\varepsilon)/\log(1/\kappa)$ on. The estimate is sharp in form: a
discrepancy that shrinks by at most a factor $\rho$ per step is still above
$\varepsilon$ at every step before $\log(d_0/\varepsilon)/\log(1/\rho)$. The
window opens at the later of the causal deadline and the relaxation time, and
unity at the time scale of a content requires it to open before that content
changes.

\paragraph{A worked example for cortex.} The longest cortico-cortical fibres run
about \ueFibreCm{}~cm \citep{nunez2014}. Callosal axons conduct at median
velocities of \ueVelocitySlow{} to \ueVelocityFast{}~m/s, and the fastest of
them at up to \ueVelocityMax{}~m/s \citep{caminiti2009}. A chemical synapse
adds about \ueSynapseMs{}~ms \citep{katz1965,sabatini1996}. The earliest a
synaptic signal can cross the cortex is therefore \ueConeMaxMs{}~ms, along the
fastest axons, and typical arrival takes \ueConeFastMs{} to \ueConeSlowMs{}~ms.
For the time scale of a content, take the unconscious integration that precedes
a conscious percept, which lasts up to \ueContentMs{}~ms \citep{herzog2016}.
Count agreement as reached when disagreement has fallen \ueReduction{}-fold,
decaying exponentially. The window is then open when the coupling's relaxation
time is at most \ueTauSlowMs{}~ms with typical arrival along the slower fibres,
and \ueTauFastMs{}~ms along the faster. Signal travel thus uses only a small
part of the time available, and whether the window opens depends on how fast
the coupling settles.

\section{Current artificial systems}
\label{sec:ai}

A clocked digital chip is an assortment of separate records. Each bit is held
in place by its own noise margin, and below that margin it varies independently
of every other bit and of the voltages beneath it. The records agree only when
the program, applied at each clock step, makes them agree. A transformer running
on such hardware reads every content from bits held within their margins, so the
result of Section~\ref{sec:enforcement} applies to it: its agreement is
computed, whatever it computes. Hardware whose contents are graded physical
quantities coupled without barriers, such as analog, in-memory or neuromorphic
substrates, can satisfy P.

\paragraph{Symbols and physical contents.} The line P draws is between symbolic
and physical content, and the line between digital and analog hardware is its
most common instance. A \emph{symbol}, in the sense the margin theorem makes
precise, is a content read through a noise margin: a physical state whose
identity survives every small enough change of the state that carries it. Two
symbols therefore agree only when a rule applied at discrete steps makes them
agree. A \emph{physical content} is a graded quantity that the system's own
dynamics reads, so its agreement with the contents it overlaps is produced by
the coupling between them. Symbols are physical objects too; what sets them
apart is the margin through which they are read. A bit is a symbol, and so is a
spike count latched at a clock edge. A spike time, a membrane potential and the
phase of a coupled oscillator are physical contents. P requires physical
contents, and a system whose contents are all symbols computes its agreement,
whatever algorithm it runs.

\paragraph{Organisation, not material.} The criterion concerns how a substrate is
organised and says nothing of what it is made of. An analog crossbar whose
column currents carry content and settle through shared conductances depends
gradedly on every input voltage. An array of coupled electronic or optical
oscillators shrinks phase disagreement through a physical coupling exactly as a
cortical sheet does. A neuromorphic chip passes where its contents are read from
membrane potentials coupled continuously, and fails where they are read from
spike counts latched at a clock edge. Biological tissue passes or fails on the
same terms: a neural circuit whose contents were read only from all-or-none
events latched far from threshold would fall under the margin theorem as any
digital circuit does.

\paragraph{Why spikes are not bits.} Spikes are all-or-none events, and what
makes them graded carriers is their timing. When a membrane potential rises
through threshold, the moment it crosses moves continuously with any small
input, and the receiving cells integrate spike times as they arrive rather than
sampling them. Spike timing therefore carries graded dependence from one region
to the next. A clocked latch compares the time of a transition with the clock
edge, which puts a threshold on the transition time, so the margin theorem
applies with that time as the thresholded quantity. The clock alone does not
separate the substrates: a spike time read through a clock still passes near
threshold, as measured above. What a digital circuit adds is restoration. Each
gate drives its output back to a rail, so every transition the latch reads
starts and ends far from any threshold, and the clock then removes the graded
timing that spikes keep.

\paragraph{Clockless logic.} Asynchronous circuits have no clock. In a
quasi-delay-insensitive design each stage signals completion to the next
through a handshake, and correctness holds under every assignment of gate and
wire delays apart from a few forks assumed isochronic \citep{martin2006}. Their
transition times vary gradedly with every voltage, as a spike time does. The
design makes the computed values independent of those times: a value is
accepted once its code is complete, whatever delays produced it. The timing of
such a circuit is therefore a trace in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:cover}. It
moves with the physics, and the circuit's own contents are read from completed
codes, each held at a rail by restoring gates, so they meet the margin theorem
as a clocked circuit's contents do.

\paragraph{Leakage.} Real digital hardware leaks: supply noise, crosstalk and
temperature move the analog quantities behind every bit. The margin theorem says
where such leakage can reach content. If a content read as a word of
thresholded continuous quantities depends gradedly on any region, some bit of
the next state sits exactly on its threshold, and correct operation keeps every
state off that set. Digital logic does pull perturbations back, but each gate
does so alone: its gain pulls a disturbed voltage back toward its own rail. That
restores each bit toward its own logical value, pulls no region toward agreement
with another, and is what keeps the bits inside their margins. The chip's
records stay separate even in the one place where its physics contracts.

The margin also has a width. If every bit of the next state is at least
$\delta$ from its threshold, each bit is read from an $L_v$-Lipschitz quantity
and the micro dynamics is $L$-Lipschitz, then a change to one region smaller
than $\delta/(L L_v)$ changes no bit. Leakage below that scale reaches content
only through the error rate (Section~\ref{sec:enforcement}), and leakage above
it flips a bit, which is a logical error. Digital design keeps ordinary noise
well below that scale: a textbook inverter's static noise margin is
\ueMarginPercent{}\% of its supply voltage, against an expected supply
variation of \ueSupplyPercent{}\% \citep{rabaey2003}, so the expected variation
is \ueNoiseToMargin{} of the margin. The nondeterminism of floating-point
reduction order is a choice among logical trajectories and lies outside both
theorems: it changes which content is computed, not how content depends on
graded state.

\paragraph{The margin's width.} If P turned on how wide an engineer happened to
make a noise margin, that would be a reductio of P: two machines running one
program would differ in unity by a design parameter. The width is not free in
that way. By the noisy margin theorem a content's response to a region is at
most its error rate, so a machine whose contents reached the fluctuation at the
noise scale would err with probability at least \ueFluctuationTV{} at every
step. Its bits would fail to hold their values from one step to the next, its
program's trajectory would stop being reproducible, and whatever agreement
survived would come from the physics beneath the bits and not from the program.
The line P draws therefore coincides with the physical line between digital and
analog operation, and working systems sit far from it on either side: a logic
node's margin is \ueMarginToNoise{} noise amplitudes wide, while a cortical
membrane sits on average \uOccDistanceMin{} to \uOccDistanceMax{} noise
amplitudes below its threshold and reaches a passing state within every window
of a percept's time scale.

\paragraph{Attractor basins.} Cortex has margins of its own. An attractor
network's basin is a dynamical margin: every state in it flows to the same
attractor, so which attractor the network settles in survives every small
enough change. This is the categorical content of Section~\ref{sec:enforcement}
in dynamical form. The basin gives the attractor's label a margin and leaves
graded the state that carries the label: the position along a continuous
attractor, such as the bump of activity that encodes a direction, the membrane
potentials within the basin, and the spike times on the way to the attractor.
Downstream cells integrate those. The voltage beneath a bit has no such reader,
since the chip reads it only through the threshold. A cortical circuit whose
contents were read only as attractor labels, far from the basins' boundaries,
would fall under the margin theorem as a chip does, and the weak nudge of
Section~\ref{sec:tests} tests which case cortex is in.

\section{Tests}
\label{sec:tests}

\paragraph{What counts as evidence about experience.} Every test below measures
decoded agreement: agreement of the contents available to readout. If a split in
readout could always be dismissed as a split in access alone, with experience
still unified behind it, no observation would bear on P. So the evidence about
experience itself is fixed in advance. It consists of responses that combine
stimuli across the regions in question when the task requires it, and reports of
a single field of experience spanning them. A dissociation counts as evidence
against P if such responses and reports split while graded coupling between the
regions is intact, or stay integrated after graded coupling between them is
removed.

\paragraph{Separating coupling from information.} This is the test that decides
between P and computational functionalism. In every manipulation of cortex available so far,
graded coupling and information flow move together: anaesthesia and callosal
section remove both. A test that confirms P over computational functionalism
must change one
while holding the other fixed. Stimulating the regions from outside does not
separate them. Coherence between cortical regions arises largely as a
consequence of communication between them \citep{schneider2021}, and
transcranial alternating current applied to two regions drives both from a
common source: where in-phase stimulation raised their synchrony, it raised
directed information flow with it \citep{reinhart2019}. The coupling has to be
supplied instead.

The setup is a bridge. Two regions whose natural connection is interrupted are
reconnected by stimulating each in proportion to the activity recorded at the
other, through one of two loops. The \emph{analog loop}'s output moves with every
graded change in its input. It is analog end to end, an analog filter and
amplifier driving the stimulator with no digitizing stage, because a loop that
digitizes the recording before driving the stimulator would run the quantized
condition in both arms. The \emph{quantized loop}'s output is read from bits, so
it has a margin.

The two loops must carry the same information and fall on opposite sides of P.
Section~\ref{sec:enforcement} fixes what the second condition means: a change
of the recorded region by its own noise amplitude must move the analog loop's
output by at least the fluctuation, and the quantized loop's output by less.
The analog loop passes exactly when its noise, in the region's units, is at
most the region's own. The quantized loop fails at every state farther from a
quantization edge than the region's noise plus \ueEdgeQuantile{} times the
recording noise, so its step must be many times both. Matching information by
adding noise to the analog loop defeats this. At matched information the added
noise is \ueMatchedNoiseToStep{} of the quantizer's step, so an analog loop
that still passes caps the step at \ueNoiseMatchMaxStep{} noise amplitudes of
the region, and the quantized loop then fails at no more than
\ueNoiseMatchFailPercent\% of its states, taking states as uniform within a
step. The analog loop's bandwidth is lowered instead, with no noise added,
until the transfer entropy it carries, estimated from the recorded activity,
matches the quantized loop's. The step is then free: at a step of
\ueBridgeStepToNoise{} noise amplitudes, with recording noise no larger than
the region's own, the quantized loop fails at \ueBandMatchFailPercent\% of its
states or more. Stepping the quantized loop's resolution checks that its
transfer entropy rises with the resolution, so that the resolution, and not
recording noise, sets the matched value. The two loops then carry the same
information, and only the analog one couples the regions gradedly.

The readout is unity, with the evidence fixed at the start of this section. Unity that follows
the analog loop at matched transfer entropy supports P over computational
functionalism, and
unity that is the same under both loops counts against P. Intracortical
stimulation in animals supports the design with decoded agreement as the
measure; reported unity needs a bridged human preparation.

\paragraph{A weak nudge should spread.} Weak stimulation of one region, below the
level that changes behaviour, should shift the content decoded in an overlapping
region, in proportion to the stimulation's strength and toward agreement with
the stimulated region. Transcranial alternating current and weak intracortical
fields supply the nudge. The decoded content must be a graded variable, such as
a location, an orientation or the strength of evidence for a percept, read from
the population activity that the overlapping region's targets integrate, so that
it is a carrier in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:cover}. A decoded category would
not do: it is constant away from its boundaries in any substrate, and would stay
unchanged whether P holds or not. If the decoded content of the overlapping
region stays unchanged at strengths that measurably move the stimulated one,
that counts against P, since P then denies unity to the very system whose unity
motivates it.

\paragraph{Agreement should be restored.} Perturb one region and measure whether
its agreement with its overlap partners comes back, and how fast. In cortex the
perturbation is stimulation, and the measure is the disagreement between
contents decoded from the perturbed region and from its partners. P predicts
restoration at the relaxation rate of Section~\ref{sec:window}, with a restoring
pull proportional to the size of the perturbation, below any threshold as well
as above. The same protocol run on an artificial system, by patching the
activations of one layer, head or position, has an outcome fixed in advance by
the margin theorem. A digital system can restore agreement by computation, above
the scale at which its logical trajectory changes, and below that scale nothing
moves. Observing this checks that the hardware operates inside its margins. It
is also the contrast that makes the cortical outcome informative: a restoring
pull that stays graded below every threshold is the signature P attributes to
enforcement.

\paragraph{The field's causal cone.} Brain tissue carries an electric field as
well as synapses. At the frequencies of neural activity that field is
quasi-static \citep{gratiy2017}: it reaches every point of the tissue it spans
with no propagation delay. Synaptic influence between distant regions, by
contrast, arrives only after axonal conduction and synaptic transmission, no
sooner than \ueConeMaxMs{}~ms across the longest cortico-cortical fibres. If two
regions re-agree after a local perturbation faster than the synaptic route
allows, the coupling has a non-synaptic part, and the timing measures the reach
of the kernel tail of Section~\ref{sec:reach} directly.

The field's strength sets the distance at which this test can decide anything.
Endogenous fields in cortex peak near \ueFieldPeak{}~mV/mm in vivo
\citep{frohlich2010}, networks of neurons detect fields from
\ueFieldDetection{}~mV/mm \citep{francis2003}, and the potential of a local
cortical source decays as $r^{-2.1}$ across millimetres \citep{rebollo2021}.
With the peak placed at the nearest distance of that measurement, the field of a
local source falls to the detection threshold within \ueFieldReachMm{}~mm, and
misses it by \ueFieldDeficitOrders{} orders of magnitude at \ueFibreCm{}~cm.
The field's cone is therefore tested at millimetre distances, where activity
has been observed to synchronize across a cut through cortex by the field alone
\citep{rebollo2021}. At centimetre distances P predicts that re-agreement
arrives through synaptic coupling, which is graded as well.

The timing can be measured with intracortical recording, with care. The
stimulation artefact saturates clinical amplifiers during the first
milliseconds, masking the earliest monosynaptic responses \citep{keller2014},
and early responses at distant contacts carry a volume-conducted component that
scales with distance as the artefact does \citep{prime2020}. Three measures
separate active re-agreement from the passive field that carries the stimulus:
template subtraction of the artefact \citep{trebaul2016}; multi-unit activity or
current-source density at the distant site, which the passive spread of the
local field potential does not reach \citep{kajikawa2011}; and a deadline set
for each subject from the tractography length of the connecting fibres
\citep{caminiti2013}.

\paragraph{Split brain.} Cutting the corpus callosum removes the main route of
both information and graded coupling between the hemispheres. Patients respond
to stimuli in the two visual fields as if perception were divided, yet can
report the presence of stimuli in either field with either hand. This has been
read as divided perception with undivided consciousness \citep{pinto2017}.
Whether the field of experience divides is contested \citep{dehaan2020}, and on
a rival switch model consciousness alternates between the hemispheres
\citep{bayne2010}. The remaining subcortical routes are synaptic, so they couple
gradedly and carry information at once. The case therefore removes coupling and
information together, which is exactly the confound the bridged preparation
above is built to break.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Computational closure.} The closest formal neighbour is the account of
\citet{rosas2024}. On it, a macroscopic level that is informationally, causally
and computationally closed marks emergent software in a physical system. This
closure is the property Section~\ref{sec:enforcement} places at the content
level of a digital system, and P reads it the other way round: a level closed
off from the graded state beneath it is one whose agreement is computed, not
enforced.

\paragraph{Designed to suppress its physics.} \citet{kleiner2024} reach a related
exclusion from a different premise. If the physical dynamics of a system is
relevant to its consciousness, processors designed and verified to suppress
deviations from their computational dynamics cannot be conscious. The margin
theorem states that suppression formally. P confines the premise to unity and
ties it to a coupling that shrinks disagreement, which is what makes it testable
on both substrates.

\paragraph{Biology and the medium.} \citet{seth2026} argues that consciousness
may depend on biological properties that computation does not capture. P
locates the relevant property in how the substrate is organised, and admits
non-biological hardware that has it (Section~\ref{sec:ai}). Mortal computation,
whose parameters are useful only on the hardware instance that learned them
\citep{hinton2022,ororbia2023}, shares P's refusal to separate the computation
from the medium.

\paragraph{The grain of a function.} Functionalism leaves open the grain at which
functional roles are stated. \citet{block1978} showed what the coarse grain
admits, a nation realizing a brain's program, and \citet{godfreysmith2016} argues
from the biological basis of cognition against the multiple realizability of
mental states. P is a condition of the finer kind: the role it asks of a carrier is to
register the graded state of another, which a program running on separate
records does not realize.

\paragraph{Integrated information.} Integrated information theory also denies
experience to digital computers running faithful simulations of a brain
\citep{tononi2015,findlay2024}. It does so through the causal structure of the
hardware at the grain of its logic gates. P turns instead on how content depends
on graded physical state, and on whether disagreement is pulled back.

\paragraph{Field theories.} The electromagnetic field theory of
\citet{mcfadden2020} identifies consciousness with the brain's field, supported
by evidence that endogenous fields entrain cortical activity
\citep{frohlich2010}. P treats the field as one way among several to realize
graded coupling.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The premise.} P is a premise and is not derived from the dynamics.
The formal results show what follows from it, not that it holds. It is necessary
for unity and not sufficient for consciousness: coupled pendulums satisfy it.
The tests separate P from computational functionalism. A functionalism that
states its roles at the grain of graded physical state agrees with P on every
test here, and the paper does not choose between the two.

\item \emph{Hardware near its thresholds.} Under noise the margin theorem bounds a
digital content's response by its error rate. Hardware run so close to its
thresholds that a change of one noise amplitude moves its contents by a
fluctuation would escape the bound. The thermal estimate declares the node
capacitance it uses.

\item \emph{The cortical estimates.} The single-input estimate combines in vitro
measurements of one connection type and one noise recording, and reads the
spike-time shift through a linear threshold crossing. The occupancy result models
one membrane as a leaky integrate-and-fire cell under white noise, with a
threshold that has no dynamics of its own. Its time constants, rates, windows,
noise shares and carrier counts are declared ranges, not fits. The window bound
treats carriers as independent, and the membranes of nearby cells are
correlated, which is why the carrier counts it uses stay far below the
anatomical ones.

\item \emph{Reach.} The identification of phase variance with effective
resistance rests on the harmonic approximation at identical natural frequencies.
The logarithmic lower bound on resistance is proved for kernels of bounded
range. The bound at every distance under a tail slower than $r^{-4}$ is proved
for a sheet that supplies the site counts and couplings of a dyadic chain of
discs. That a kernel of finite second moment leaves the resistance unbounded is
the classical recurrence criterion, for translation-invariant kernels on the
lattice; that the growth is logarithmic is established numerically on finite
sheets. Above the order--disorder crossover of Section~\ref{sec:reach} the
harmonic identity fails and agreement is lost at every distance.

\item \emph{Attractors.} The answer for attractor networks takes the unified
contents to be carried by the graded state within a basin. A cortex whose
unified contents were basin labels alone would fail P.

\item \emph{Which variables are contents.} Which variables a system's own
dynamics reads, and so which are its contents, is settled system by system from
its circuitry. The margin theorem takes that identification as given.

\item \emph{Artificial systems.} The predictions for artificial systems are
consequences of the margin theorem. Confirming them checks that hardware runs
inside its margins and leaves P untested; the tests of P are the cortical ones.

\item \emph{The unity window.} Its edges are proved for a given contraction
factor; identifying that factor with the coupling's spectral gap is linear
response. The relaxation time of cortical coupling is unmeasured, so the window
estimate states the condition for the window to open at cortical time scales and
leaves open whether cortex meets it. It also reads agreement as a declared
reduction factor of disagreement.

\item \emph{From phases to contents.} The step from phase agreement to content
agreement assumes one oscillator population per content patch, which is a
modelling choice about cortex.

\item \emph{The field.} A long-range tail improves agreement only in proportion
to its conductance against the short-range part. The field of a local cortical
source falls below the detection threshold within millimetres, by an estimate
that places the field's peak at a declared distance. Across centimetres graded
coupling is carried chiefly by synapses, and the field's contribution there is a
quantity to be measured.

\item \emph{Human evidence.} The dissociation of coupling from information has,
so far, no human preparation in which reported unity can be measured across a
bridged connection. Human data can falsify P but cannot by themselves confirm it
against computational functionalism, because graded coupling and information flow co-vary in
every manipulation available so far.
\end{itemize}

\section{Resistance bounds and simulations}
\label{app:reach}

The logarithmic growth is a lower bound for every kernel of bounded range. Group
the sites into shells around a site $a$, so that the coupling joins only sites
in the same or adjacent shells. The shells then act as conductors in series,
and the effective conductance from $a$ to any site beyond shell $n$ is at most
$\bigl(\sum_{k<n} 1/C_k\bigr)^{-1}$, where $C_k$ is the coupling crossing from
shell $k$ to shell $k+1$. On a planar sheet
the perimeter of shell $k$ grows linearly in $k$, so $C_k \le c\,(k+1)$ for a
constant $c$ set by the kernel's strength and width, and the effective
resistance is at least $\log(n+1)/c$.
Widening the kernel enlarges its shells and raises $c$, which slows the growth
and leaves it unbounded. Distance-independent agreement on a sheet therefore
needs coupling of unbounded range.

A tail slower than $r^{-4}$ bounds the resistance at every distance. Run a chain
of site sets out from $a$ through discs of radius $2^j$ and back in to $b$, the
two widest discs spanning the separation. The phase difference across $a$ and
$b$ telescopes through the discs' mean phases, and the drop between the means of
two consecutive discs is bounded by the coupling energy between them, shared
among the pairs of their sites. A disc of radius $2^j$ on a planar sheet holds at
least $\alpha\,4^j$ sites, and a kernel $K(r)\gtrsim r^{-(2+\sigma)}$ couples
two sites of consecutive discs by at least $\beta\,2^{-(2+\sigma)j}$. The
number of pairs grows as $16^j$ and their coupling falls as $2^{-(2+\sigma)j}$,
so the drops shrink geometrically with ratio $q=2^{-(2-\sigma)/2}$, which is
below one for $\sigma<2$, and the effective conductance is at least
$(1-q)^2\alpha^2\beta/8$ however far apart $a$ and $b$ lie.

Both results match classical potential theory. Imagine a random walker on the
lattice that jumps between sites with probabilities proportional to the kernel,
which is the same at every site. The resistance from a site to infinity is
infinite exactly when that walker is sure to return to its start, that is, when
the walk is recurrent \citep{doyle1984}. Shorting every site outside a disc
around $a$ can only lower the resistance from $a$ to a site $b$ beyond the disc,
so under recurrence that resistance grows without bound as $b$ recedes. A
symmetric kernel of finite second moment, which includes every tail steeper
than $r^{-4}$, gives a recurrent walk in two dimensions \citep{chung1951}. The
bound above is the matching statement for tails slower than $r^{-4}$, whose
walks escape. The same threshold holds beyond the harmonic approximation: the
plane rotator in two dimensions with coupling falling as $r^{-\alpha}$,
$2<\alpha<4$, has true long-range order at low noise \citep{kunz1976}, which
short-range coupling forbids. The plane-rotator sheet also has a
Kosterlitz--Thouless transition \citep{frohlich1981}, an order--disorder
crossover that the simulations below locate. When natural frequencies differ
from site to site, short-range coupling fares worse still: phases then
desynchronize in every dimension up to four \citep{hong2005}.

Simulated sheets at identical frequencies, of side \uSideSmall{} and
\uSideLarge{}, with nearest-neighbour, exponential and power-law kernels of
tail exponent $\sigma=1$ and $\sigma=3$ at a common row sum, follow the harmonic
identity in the ordered phase. The measured phase variance is at most
\uHarmonicRatioLow{} times $D\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}$ at $D=\uNoiseLow$ and at most
\uHarmonicRatioMid{} times at $D=\uNoiseMid$, the excess rising with noise as
the cosine coupling softens, and a logarithm fits its growth with distance
better than a line in \uLogarithmicRuns{} of the \uOrderedRuns{} runs at those
noise levels. At $D=\uNoiseHigh$ the nearest-neighbour sheet disorders: the
mean chord discrepancy $\langle 1-\cos(\theta_a-\theta_b)\rangle$ at the
largest separation is \uChordNearestHigh{}. The exponential and $\sigma=1$
sheets stay ordered at that noise (\uChordExponentialHigh{} and
\uChordSigmaOneHigh{}), and the $\sigma=3$ sheet nearly disorders
(\uChordSigmaThreeHigh{}), so the kernel's width sets the order--disorder
crossover as well as the rate of decay. Exact resistance sums at sheet sides
\uScalingSideA{}, \uScalingSideB{} and \uScalingSideC{} separate width from
tail. The exponential kernel, with \uStiffnessRatio{} times the
nearest-neighbour stiffness, gives resistance at half the side of
\uFarExponentialA{}, \uFarExponentialB{} and \uFarExponentialC{}, still
growing; the $\sigma=1$ tail gives \uFarSigmaOneA{}, \uFarSigmaOneB{} and
\uFarSigmaOneC{}, and the $\sigma=3$ tail, steeper than $r^{-4}$, gives
\uFarSigmaThreeA{}, \uFarSigmaThreeB{} and \uFarSigmaThreeC{}, growing as a
short-range kernel does.

\section{The occupancy computation}
\label{app:occupancy}

The membrane is an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process in units of its own stationary
standard deviation, with the threshold at zero. After a spike it is refractory
and then reset to its mean, and the mean is calibrated so that the stationary
rate equals the declared rate. Probability is propagated on a voltage grid
through the exact Ornstein--Uhlenbeck step, and a crossing between two sampled
times is counted with the Brownian-bridge probability
$\exp(-2xy/s^2)$ for sub-threshold samples $x$ and $y$ and step deviation $s$.
Nothing is sampled. From each grid state, the law of the first spike time in
the window, with its absence as one more outcome, is propagated in one pass, and
the response to a change is the total-variation distance between two such laws.
A state passes when every admissible change moves the law by at least the
fluctuation, where a change is admissible if it is at least one noise amplitude
of the carrier and does not carry the membrane across threshold. The fraction of
occupied states that pass is the stationary mass of the passing states. The
probability of entering a passing state within a window is computed on the same
chain with the passing states made absorbing. A carrier that supplies a share
$\varphi$ of the membrane's noise variance has noise amplitude $\sqrt{\varphi}$
in the membrane's units, and its changes are scaled accordingly. The bit is a
carrier restored each step to a rail at a declared margin, read after one step of
unit Gaussian noise, with occupancy given by that noise around the rail. Halving
both the grid spacing and the time step changes the passing fraction by
\uOccResolutionPercent\%.
