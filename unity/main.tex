\begin{abstract}
Human conscious experience is unified, and we argue that unity requires
physically enforced agreement: within every window of a content's time scale,
some graded carrier of each region must register the graded state of every
region it overlaps, above its own noise, through a coupling that pulls their
contents together. The premise is a functional condition stated at the grain of
graded physical dynamics, finer than the grain of an algorithm: at that grain
cortex and digital chips come apart, while at the grain of an algorithm they do
not. It makes a prediction that an account stated on the information regions
exchange does not: when two cortical regions are bridged by an analog and by a
quantized loop that carry the same information, in total and about the task,
unity follows the analog loop. We prove that under noise, content read
from bits responds to a change of any region by at most its error rate, a bound
that is attained, so a digital machine, clocked or clockless, fails the premise
whatever algorithm it runs. Measured by
one protocol over the states each occupies, a noisy cortical membrane passes
near its threshold and reaches such a state within a percept's time scale,
while the value a clocked latch reads passes at none of its states. Enforced agreement must arrive
before the content changes, which bounds how slowly cortical coupling may relax,
and on a noisy sheet of coupled oscillators the coupling's reach decides how far
phase agreement holds. We
state the preparation that runs the bridge and the
cortical outcomes that would refute the premise.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A conscious scene is experienced as one scene. Many parts of the brain
contribute to it, each describing part of the same situation, and their
descriptions agree. This paper asks what kind of agreement that has to be.

Many parts can agree in two ways. In the first, the parts are tied together
physically, like weights joined by springs. Each part's state is a graded
physical quantity that pulls on its neighbours continuously, so no part can
move, by however little, without pulling its neighbours after it. The parts are
then coordinates of a single physical state, and they cannot vary independently
at any scale. We call this agreement \emph{physically enforced}. In the second,
each part keeps its own record, like separate ledgers kept consistent by a
clerk. A rule compares the records at discrete steps and corrects them where
they differ. Each record is held in place by a margin, and below that margin the
records vary independently of each other and of everything beneath them. We call
this agreement \emph{computed}. The first is one system in the sense physics
uses, a single state space with coupled coordinates. The second is many systems
kept consistent by a protocol. A clocked digital chip is of the second kind: its
bits are separate records, and whatever agreement its program produces is
computed. Our premise is that the unity of an experience is oneness of the first
kind.

\paragraph{Premise P (physical unity).} A system's contents are unified only if
each region's contents are read from graded carriers and, within every window of
the contents' own time scale, some carrier of each region depends on the graded
physical state of every region it overlaps, above the noise floor and through a
coupling that contracts their disagreement.

P is a necessary condition for unity. It is itself a functional condition, since
it picks out unity by what carriers do to one another, and it fixes that role at
the grain of graded physical dynamics instead of at the grain of an algorithm.
Functionalism leaves that grain open. At a coarse enough grain a nation whose
citizens exchange messages by radio realizes a brain's program
\citep{block1978}, and the biological basis of cognition has been argued to
tie mental states to a finer one \citep{godfreysmith2016}. P and computational
functionalism about unity, on which only the computation matters, are
therefore two grains of one kind of claim, and they classify cortex alike. They
part where an algorithm is carried by separate records: a perfect simulation of a coupled
field reproduces the field's content-level dynamics and is unified at the
algorithm's grain, and it fails P, because its agreement is computed. At P's
grain cortex and mainstream digital chips come apart; at the algorithm's they
do not. The bridge of Section~\ref{sec:tests} holds fixed the information two
regions exchange and varies the grain of their coupling, so it asks whether
unity tracks P's grain or the information exchanged.

P rests on two premises, argued in Section~\ref{sec:enforcement}. The first is
an account of unity: unified contents are parts of one total experience, so
they need one physical system to carry them. The second is how physics counts
systems: parts are one system when they interact at every scale between their
own noise and their own thresholds. Everything else in the argument is a
theorem. Content read through a margin responds to a region by at most its
error rate, a rule that computes agreement among such records only adds another
record and joins none of them into one system, and a graded reader joins the
regions it reads.

Physical systems are noisy, so P is read on the
probability laws of contents. Graded dependence then counts above the noise
floor: every sub-threshold change larger than the noise of what a carrier depends
on must move the carrier's next content by at least as much as that content
fluctuates. P asks this of some carrier of each region within each window, and
that is what one total state amounts to for regions made of noisy parts: two
regions are one system when, within the window, some carrier of each registers
the other above its noise, so that no change of either region above its noise
is sealed off from the other. A region
is many carriers, and a content is held over its own time scale, not at an
instant. A noisy carrier registers its inputs near its threshold and not
between spikes, so asking every carrier at every instant would split a single
cortical neuron from its own inputs for most of each interspike interval and
count no network of spiking cells as one system; the window form is the
condition on the region and its time scale that the strong form states for a
single carrier at a single step. P is neutral on where unified contents live: it constrains whichever
regions carry them, whether they span the cortex or are confined to a posterior
zone of it \citep{koch2016}.

The paper makes three claims:
\begin{enumerate}
\claim{sec:tests}{When two cortical regions are bridged by an analog and by a quantized loop that carry the same information, in total and about the task, P predicts that unity follows the loop that couples them gradedly, which tests whether unity tracks the graded grain or the information the regions exchange; a weak, sub-threshold perturbation of one region should also shift the graded content decoded in an overlapping region toward agreement}
\claim{sec:enforcement}{Under noise, content read from bits responds to a change of any region below its margin by at most its error rate, a bound that is attained, so content read from bits is not physically enforced, whatever algorithm the bits run. A region held by a margin drops out of every chain of couplings, corrections it writes back included, while a graded reader joins the regions it reads. Measured by one protocol over the states each occupies, a cortical membrane passes the noise-floor test near its threshold and visits such states within every window of a percept's time scale, while the value a clocked latch reads passes at none of its states, although the chip's nodes cross their thresholds as often}
\claim{sec:reach}{Enforced agreement must arrive before the content changes, so P predicts that cortical coupling relaxes within \ueTauFastMs{}~ms across the cortex; on a noisy coupled sheet, disagreement in phase is effective resistance, so the coupling's reach decides how far phase agreement holds}
\end{enumerate}

Every formal result in the paper is machine-checked in Lean~4, and
Appendix~\ref{app:formal} maps each one to its declaration. The two premises
are the steps that are argued rather than proved, and the bridge tests them
together.

\section{Unity as agreement over a cover of contents}
\label{sec:cover}

Think of an atlas. Each page maps one patch of ground, neighbouring pages
overlap, and where they overlap they show the same roads. Pages that agree on
every overlap fit together into one map of the whole territory. We treat the
brain's regions the same way: each describes part of a shared situation, and
unity is the property that these local descriptions agree wherever they
overlap. When they do, they determine a single global state.

Synchrony is not unity. Two regions can oscillate in phase while one encodes a
stimulus at one location and the other at another, so equal phases coexist
with incompatible descriptions. Unity is a property of what overlapping regions
describe, and synchrony is at most a route to it.

A region's \emph{content} is a variable that the system itself reads, not merely
one an outside observer can decode. Two conditions define it: the regions this
region drives respond to it, and it determines the quantity the region
represents, such as a location or an orientation. A variable that meets both is
a \emph{carrier} of content. A variable that only an observer can decode is a
trace. The supply current of a digital circuit carries enough of its logical
state that a secret key can be recovered from it \citep{kocher1999}, yet no gate
of the circuit reads the current, so the current is a trace. In cortex the
membrane potential and the timing of spikes are carriers, because the
postsynaptic cells integrate them.

The first condition fixes when a variable is a carrier as well as which one. A
reader responds to a variable at the times it takes it, so the variable carries
content at those times and is a trace between them. A cortical cell integrates
a presynaptic spike as it arrives, so the time of each spike is taken when it
happens. A clocked latch responds to its input only inside a short aperture at
the clock edge, so the input's value there is a carrier, and the course of the
voltage between edges, which no reader takes, is a trace, as the supply current
is. A carrier's \emph{next content} is therefore what its readers take from it
next, at the time they take it: for a membrane the time of its next spike, and
for a node that feeds a latch its value at the next clock edge.

Agreement is measured between regions whose contents represent a shared
variable. The regions, and the content variable of each, are fixed by this
causal role and by decodability before agreement is measured, so that nobody can
make the measurement trivial by choosing the regions after the fact.

\paragraph{A running example.} A bird flies across the visual field. A visual
area represents its location on a retinotopic map, and a parietal area
represents the same location while planning a reach toward it. The two areas
overlap in one variable, the bird's location. Each area's content is its
estimate of that location, and its carriers are the membrane potentials and
spike times of the cells that encode it, which the cells each area drives
integrate. An estimate an experimenter decodes from the area's summed activity,
and that no cell reads, is a trace. Unity, here, is the two estimates agreeing.
The sections below return to the bird: Section~\ref{sec:enforcement} asks how
that agreement is produced, and Section~\ref{sec:ai} asks it of a chip that
tracks the same bird.

\paragraph{The definition, stated precisely.} Region $i$ carries a content
$s_i$, a description of the variables it represents, and regions $i$ and $j$
overlap in the variables both represent. Restricting a content to an overlap
keeps only the shared variables. The contents are \emph{compatible} when every
two agree on their overlap, $s_i|_{i\cap j}=s_j|_{i\cap j}$, and compatible
contents extend to exactly one global description whose restriction to each
region is that region's content. Unity is compatibility, and its degree is the
\emph{overlap discrepancy}, the largest distance between two contents restricted
to a common overlap. Approximate agreement still fixes one description to
within its tolerance: for real-valued contents in the uniform distance, if every
overlap discrepancy is at most $\varepsilon$,
averaging the contents that cover each variable, with weights that sum to one,
gives a global description within $\varepsilon$ of every region's content, and
any two such descriptions lie within $\varepsilon$ of each other. Agreement is
measured by decoding. Decoders $d_A$ and $d_B$ fitted independently on two
regions estimate a shared variable $x$ with errors $e_A$ and $e_B$, and
$|d_A-d_B|\le|e_A|+|e_B|$, so two accurate decoders agree whatever produced
their agreement. The measure is therefore the agreement in excess of what the
two errors imply, against a null that pairs decodes across trials with the same
stimulus and the same errors. These results are proved in a companion paper
\citep{bobadillasuarez2026coherence}, and P adds a condition on how that
agreement is produced.

The companion proposes agreement on overlaps as a correlate of unity, to be
tested, and P is conditional on that identification. The two papers therefore
form a programme in two stages. The companion's test asks whether decoded
agreement in excess of single-region accuracy is larger on trials the subject
perceives than on trials it does not, at matched coherence. A positive result
makes the bridge of Section~\ref{sec:tests} the next question: whether that
agreement has to be physically enforced. A negative one removes decoded
agreement as a neural measure of unity and leaves the bridge its behavioural
readout, so the second stage does not wait on the first.

\section{Physical enforcement versus routed agreement}
\label{sec:enforcement}

\paragraph{The argument in outline.} This section argues for P from two
premises and proves the rest. The first, \emph{wholeness}, is an account of
unity: unified contents are parts of one total experience, and being a part of
a whole is not a further part of it \citep{bayne2010}. The second,
\emph{counting}, is how physics counts systems once the parts fix the scale:
two carriers are one system when every change of one between its own noise and
its nearest threshold moves the other by at least the other's own fluctuation.
Three results are then proved from the definitions below. The margin lemma and
its noisy form bound the response of content read from bits by its error rate.
Counted by the second premise, a region held by a margin is one system only
with itself and drops out of every chain of couplings, corrections written back
through it included, while a graded reader joins the regions it reads. And
graded dependence above the noise floor at every overlap holds exactly when
every overlapping pair is one system. Wholeness says that a unified experience
needs one physical system to carry it, counting says what one system is, and the
theorems say that records never make one while graded coupling does. Where
cortex and a chip hold their carriers is measured, by the occupancy computation
at the end of the section.

The argument needs five definitions. Each is stated in words first.

A system has a physical state, its \emph{micro state} $x=(x_i)_i$, made up of
the states $x_i$ of its regions. One step of its physics carries $x$ to $f(x)$.
A \emph{content map} $\pi$ reads each region's content off the micro state. The
content level is \emph{closed} when the next content can be computed from the
current content alone, without looking at the micro state:
$\pi\circ f = g\circ\pi$ for some content dynamics $g$. A digital computer is
built so that its logical level is closed in this sense. The next content
$\pi(f(x))$ of a carrier is what its readers take from it next, as the carrier
condition of Section~\ref{sec:cover} fixes.

A readout has a \emph{noise margin} on a set of states when every state in the
set can be nudged a little without changing what is read, that is, when each
has a neighbourhood on which the readout is constant. A bit is the standard
example: any voltage well above the threshold reads as 1.

Content \emph{depends gradedly} on a region when every change to that region,
however small, can move the next content. Formally, no neighbourhood of the
region's micro state leaves the next content unchanged while the other regions
are held fixed.

The \emph{overlap discrepancy} measures how much the contents of overlapping
regions disagree. It \emph{contracts} near a state when one step of $f$ shrinks
it by a factor below one throughout a neighbourhood of that state. Asking for a
whole neighbourhood matters: perturbations too small to cross any threshold are
then pulled back toward agreement along with larger ones.

Agreement is \emph{physically enforced} at a state when content depends
gradedly on each region there and the overlap discrepancy contracts near it.
P requires this at the states the system occupies while its contents are
unified.

\paragraph{The margin lemma.} In words: if content is read through a margin,
small enough physical changes never reach it. Precisely, if the micro dynamics
is continuous and carries a state into a margin's operating set, the next
content depends gradedly on no region's micro state. The proof is short:
continuity carries a small enough neighbourhood of each region's micro state
into the neighbourhood on which the readout is constant. A thresholded
continuous quantity has a margin away from its threshold, and so does a finite
word of such readouts, so the lemma covers any content read from bits. The
lemma is elementary by design: once the definitions are fixed the separation
follows in a line, so a reader who disputes it disputes a definition, and the
rest of this section argues for each.

\paragraph{Why the criterion sits below the content level.} At the content
level a computer can imitate a coupled field exactly. A digital system running
a consensus algorithm, or integrating the equations of a coupled field, has a
content map whose next step shrinks the disagreement between overlapping
regions just as the field does. A criterion stated on the content dynamics
would put that system in the same class as the field. P is stated instead on
how content depends on graded physical state, and there the two differ. The
field's content moves with every graded change in the regions it overlaps. The
simulation's content is read from bits held inside their margins, and moves
with none of them.

This is the simulation objection. A perfect simulation of a coupled cortical
field reproduces the field's content-level trajectory and fails P, because its
agreement is computed from contents and not enforced by the graded state that
carries them. P denies the simulation unity, and the denial needs a reason.

\paragraph{Why unity tracks enforcement.} Unity settles how many subjects there
are. A unified experience is had by one subject and a divided one by two or
more, which is what the split-brain debate of Section~\ref{sec:tests} asks of a
single skull. Whatever makes contents unified therefore also decides whether
they belong to one system or to several. Agreement at the content level
cannot settle that count. Put the records of one program in different
buildings, stretch the clerk's step to a year, or let one processor visit the
records in turn: the content trajectory is unchanged while the physical system
that realises it goes from one chip to several machines to one record at a
time. Block's nation, whose citizens run a brain's functional organisation by
radio for an hour \citep{block1978}, is the same program spread over a country.
A criterion stated on content-level agreement gives all of these the same
number of subjects, without any physical fact to consult in doing so.

\paragraph{Unity is not one more content.} Wholeness takes experiences to be
unified when they are parts of a single total experience, the subject's experience at that time
\citep{bayne2010}. Being a part of a whole is not a further part of it, so the
unity of two contents is not a third content alongside them. That fixes what can
realise it. A \emph{record} here is a content held by a margin: its identity
survives every change of its carrier below the margin, so it is sealed off from
the graded state of everything else. A rule that compares records and corrects
them reads records and writes records, and whatever it produces, a flag, a
merged value or a corrected copy, is one more record. Whether records can join
anything is a theorem once systems are counted as the next paragraph counts
them, by chains of couplings at the noise floor. A region whose content is
held by a margin, with an error rate below the fluctuation yardstick, is coupled
to no region that admits a change above its noise inside the margin, whatever
the regions it reads carry, so it is one system only with itself. For any set of
records, records that read records included, two regions are one system exactly
when a chain of couplings that avoids every record joins them: records drop out
of every chain, and the regress of rules on rules adds nothing at any depth.
Writing corrections back changes nothing: when one region's next content
depends on another only through a record's symbol, a change of the other inside
the margin moves it by at most the record's error rate, so the rule couples the
two no more than the record does. A graded reader stays in the chain. A region
whose content moves with two regions above their noise, and moves them in turn,
is coupled to both and joins them into one system when they share no coupling
of their own; under linear dynamics with uniform noise such a reader joins two
regions that a record reading the same two leaves apart
(Appendix~\ref{app:formal}). The regress stops at a reader on the chain, not at
a record beside it. In the running example, a register holding the bird's
location as a number is a record, and a cell in a third area that integrates
spike times from the visual and the parietal area, and projects back to both,
is a reader. A synapse, and a membrane integrating spike times near its
threshold, which a cortical membrane visits within every window as measured
below, are readers in this sense. A workspace that makes every record available
to a common reader is the case of the rule again: what the reader receives is a
set of records, each held by its own margin. Computation therefore adds contents
and never produces the whole they are parts of. What can make contents parts of
one whole without adding a content is the physical state that carries them:
carriers that are coordinates of a single state, whose agreement is a fact about
where that state sits and is written nowhere. P asks for exactly this, which is
why it is stated on how carriers depend on one another and not on what any rule
computes about them.

\paragraph{Counting systems at the noise floor.} The second premise is
argued in three steps from the criterion physics already uses. Physics counts
systems by interaction: parts form one system when their joint state does not
factor into independent coordinates. Applied at no particular scale, the
criterion makes every chip one system, since wires, a shared supply and
crosstalk couple all of its voltages, and it makes Block's nation one system
through its radios. Of the voltages this is correct. Unity, though, is a
property of contents, a chip reads its contents from its voltages through
margins, and whether two coordinates factor depends on the resolution at which
they are taken. The first step fixes the lower end of that resolution. A
difference smaller than a part's own noise is not a state the part holds: its
own fluctuations move its next state by more than the difference does, so the
difference does not survive the part's next step as a distinguishable state,
and a coordinate the part does not hold is not one along which it can be
independent of anything. The second step fixes the upper end. A change that
crosses one of a part's thresholds changes its symbol, and what another part
registers of the crossing is the symbol, the same whatever graded state
produced it. That registration is a message, the exchange of records whose
agreement, by the previous paragraph, is one more record, so crossings join
nothing that records do not. The third step applies the criterion in the range
that remains. There physics asks for a product decomposition: two parts are
separate systems at a resolution when the dynamics of each is independent of
the other's state at that resolution. A record meets it outright. Every change
of another region inside the record's margin leaves the record's next content
where it was, up to its error rate, so at that resolution the record's dynamics
is independent of the other region's whole graded state and the joint state is
a product. A graded reader is the opposite case: every change of the region it
reads between noise and threshold moves it by at least its own fluctuation, so
the region has no coordinate at that resolution that the reader's dynamics
ignores. The count draws the line between these two cases at its strict end.
Two carriers are one system when every change of one between its noise and its
nearest threshold moves the other by at least the other's own fluctuation, and
two when some such change leaves the other unmoved, a coordinate one of them
holds and the other does not register. Records and graded readers are classed
alike by the count and by a product decomposition, and they are the two cases
the argument turns on. Both ends of the range
are properties of each part, the size of its noise and the position of its
thresholds, so nobody chooses the scale to separate brains from chips.

This is the noise-floor reading of graded dependence given below, and the
identity is proved: for carriers whose contents overlap, each depends gradedly above the
noise floor on every carrier it overlaps exactly when every overlapping pair is
one system in this sense. A region holds many carriers, and a noisy carrier
registers another only while it sits near its own threshold, as measured below.
Two regions are therefore one system when some carrier of each registers some
carrier of the other, and P asks this within every window of the contents' time
scale. Asking it of some carriers is strictly weaker than asking it of all: a
state exists at which the first holds and the second fails.

\paragraph{The chip, counted.} A chip fails the count by design. A bit's margin
spans \ueMarginToNoise{} of its noise amplitudes, and no other bit registers a
change anywhere in that range except through the error rate. By the noisy
margin theorem below, once that rate is under the fluctuation and each carrier
has a change above its noise inside the margin, no carrier of any region
registers any other, so the weaker form fails exactly as the stronger one does,
and each part is a system of its own. Its wires pass on only the changes that
cross a threshold, which is the rule applied at each clock step, so between
thresholds, where a working chip spends nearly all of its time, its contents
are as many systems as it has bits, carried on voltages that form one.
Crosstalk and supply noise below the margin are real interactions, and they
move the voltages; by the noisy margin theorem they move the contents by at
most the error rate, which is the quantity a working chip is designed to keep
small. The count looks strange because ordinary usage counts by program, and
one program uses all of the bits. Counted by physics, each bit is restored to
its own rail by its own gates, and what keeps the bits in step is the rule the
program applies, a protocol among separate records. The citizens of Block's
nation likewise register each other only through the messages they exchange.

\paragraph{Where the line falls.} A criterion built to exclude chips would draw
its line between substrates. This one draws it inside each, at a carrier's
distance from its own threshold, and it discards threshold crossings in cortex
as it does in silicon. A spike is all-or-none, so by the count above the
occurrence of a spike is a message, and a spike count latched at a clock edge is
a record. Cortex passes on what the count leaves it, the range below threshold in
which a membrane's content moves with each input above its noise, and, as
measured below, it passes there only near threshold. The verdict reverses within
each substrate: an analog crossbar whose column currents drive the next array
directly passes, and a neural circuit read only from latched counts fails
(Section~\ref{sec:ai}). What
separates a working chip from cortex is where each holds its carriers, a fact
about the system that the criterion reads and does not set.

\paragraph{What the premise rests on.} Cortex passes the count within every
window, as shown below, and P asks exactly this of the regions whose contents
are unified. P thus rests on wholeness and counting, and every step between
them is proved: wholeness asks for one physical state, and counting says at
which scale physics counts one. The bridge of Section~\ref{sec:tests} tests
them together.

\paragraph{Gradual replacement.} The standard objection to a substrate
condition replaces a brain's neurons one at a time by digital units that compute
the same input--output function \citep{chalmers1995}. If behaviour and report
are preserved throughout while unity fades or divides, the subject is wrong
about its own experience at the intermediate stages, which the argument takes to
be absurd. P answers in two parts. First, exact replacement is unavailable at
the grain P concerns: by the margin lemma, a unit that reads its inputs through
margins reproduces a neuron's function to the resolution of its margins and
discards the graded dependence below it. Whether discarding it changes
behaviour is an empirical question, and the bridge of Section~\ref{sec:tests}
asks it for one connection, which makes the bridge a local, testable instance of
replacement. Second, where replacement preserves behaviour and report exactly,
P accepts the conclusion the argument calls absurd and gives its reason: reports
are contents computed from contents, and unity is not one more content, so
reports can stay the same while unity changes. Behaviour is evidence about unity
in a system whose contents are carried by its own coupling, and a system built
to reproduce that evidence by computation is the simulation objection again. A
bridged cortex is of the first kind, so at the bridge P stakes itself on
behaviour (Section~\ref{sec:tests}).

\paragraph{Categories.} Categorical contents have margins in every substrate,
brains included. The dominant percept in binocular rivalry, a perceived
category, or any class picked as the largest of several values stays the same
on each side of its boundary. Away from the boundary, the margin lemma
therefore denies it graded dependence in cortex exactly as in silicon. So P is
stated on the graded variables from which categories are read, such as a
location, an orientation, a time or the relative strength of competing
percepts. A category meets P through the variable that carries it, and depends
gradedly on a region only where that variable does. This opens no route for a
digital machine. Every variable it computes, whether a number, a vector of
numbers or a category, is a function of its bits, and after any number of steps
that keep its bits away from their thresholds such a function depends gradedly
on no region. The voltages beneath the bits are not carriers in the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:cover}. An observer may decode the machine's contents from
them, but the machine itself reads them only through the bits, at that step and
at every later one.

\paragraph{Noise.} Real physics is noisy, and noise changes the question. A
gate's chance of flipping is a smooth function of its input voltage and is never
exactly zero, so the \emph{probability} of a digital machine's next content
depends, however slightly, on every region. Read on exact contents, the margin
lemma would then pick out a difference lying below the noise floor of both
substrates. P is therefore read on probabilities. The \emph{response} of a
content to a region at scale $\eta$ is the largest change that moving the region
by at most $\eta$ makes to the probability of any outcome of the next content, a
total-variation distance. As the yardstick for a large response we take the one
that a shift of one standard deviation produces in a Gaussian carrier,
$2\Phi(1/2)-1=\ueFluctuationTV$: a response of at least this size moves the
content by as much as the content naturally fluctuates. Graded dependence above the noise
floor asks the response to reach this fluctuation for every change of the region
that is larger than its own noise and smaller than the change that crosses one
of its thresholds. When the region moves a carrier linearly under additive noise
whose density has a single peak, Gaussian or heavy-tailed, a larger change moves
the content at least as much as a smaller one, so the criterion is met exactly
when a change of one noise amplitude reaches the fluctuation. When the law of the
noise changes with the state, as the variance of a spike count does, the same
holds on each side of the state for laws with a monotone likelihood ratio, and
the criterion is met exactly when a change of one noise amplitude up and one down
both reach the fluctuation.
The constants are a Gaussian identity and the system's own
scales, and none of them is fitted to either substrate.

The margin lemma has a noisy form, the noisy margin theorem, and it is this
form that carries the argument, since real physics is noisy. Let each realization of the noise fix an
$L$-Lipschitz step of the micro dynamics, one that stretches no distance by
more than a factor $L$. Let the content be constant within radius $r$ of the
noisy successor, except on a set of realizations of probability at most
$\varepsilon$. Then a change of one region smaller than $r/L$ moves the
probability of every outcome of the next content by at most $\varepsilon$. For
anything computed from bits, $\varepsilon$ is the probability that some bit of
the noisy successor lands within $\delta$ of its threshold, and the bound is
attained.

\paragraph{A logic gate in numbers.} On a logic node of \ueNodeCapFf{}~fF at
room temperature the thermal noise is \ueThermalNoiseMv{}~mV
\citep{johnson1928,nyquist1928}, and the textbook inverter's margin is
\ueMarginToNoise{} times that. After a change of one noise amplitude, the
probability of a flip, which bounds the response, is below
$10^{-\ueErrorOrders}$ \citep{kish2002}. The response reaches the fluctuation
only within \ueGradedWindowPercent\% of the margin's width from the threshold,
where the bit is in the middle of switching.

\paragraph{A neuron in numbers.} A spike time passes the same test. Suppose an
input $\Delta V$, too small to fire the cell on its own, arrives while the
membrane potential is rising through threshold at rate $\dot V$. It moves the
spike by $\Delta V/\dot V$. Voltage noise $\sigma_V$ jitters the spike by
$\sigma_V/\dot V$. The shift therefore exceeds the jitter exactly when
$\Delta V$ exceeds $\sigma_V$, whatever the slope, and spike generation adds
little noise of its own \citep{mainen1995}. A unitary excitatory potential
between cortical pyramidal cells averages \ueEpspMv{}~mV, with a coefficient of
variation of \ueEpspCv{} \citep{markram1997}, and the membrane noise near
threshold is \ueMembraneNoiseMv{}~mV \citep{jacobson2005}. Counting the input's
own variability as noise, one such input shifts the spike by
\ueCorticalShiftToJitter{} times its jitter, a response of \ueCorticalTV{}.
Every input larger than \ueEpspPassMv{}~mV passes, up to the input that fires
the cell.

\paragraph{A membrane over the states it occupies.} That estimate holds at the
moment the membrane rises through threshold. Measured over every state a
membrane occupies, by the protocol applied to the logic node, it holds near
threshold and nowhere else. We model the membrane as a leaky integrate-and-fire
cell driven by white noise, with time constants of \uOccTauFast{} to
\uOccTauSlow{}~ms, spanning the in vivo high-conductance state
\citep{destexhe1999,destexhe2003}, and stationary rates of \uOccRateLow{} to
\uOccRateHigh{}~Hz, spanning the sparse rates of awake cortex
\citep{hromadka2008}. Its next content is the time of its first spike within
\uOccNextShort{} or \uOccNextLong{}~ms, and every change of one to
\uOccShiftMax{} noise amplitudes, in either direction, must move that content by
the fluctuation. Propagating the membrane's probability exactly on a voltage
grid (Appendix~\ref{app:occupancy}), it sits on average \uOccDistanceMin{} to
\uOccDistanceMax{} noise amplitudes below threshold and passes at
\uOccPassMinPercent{} to \uOccPassMaxPercent\% of the states it occupies. A bit
restored to a rail at the logic node's margin passes at none of its states, and
its largest response over them is below $10^{-\uOccBitOrders}$. No binary readout
passes at any margin: under Gaussian noise, its response to a change of one
noise amplitude, asked in both directions as the criterion asks, is at most
$\Phi(1)-1/2=\uYardBinaryCeiling$. A graded content exceeds the fluctuation
by a wide margin: at its best state the membrane responds by at least
\uYardBestMin{} in every regime and at every noise share.

A membrane passes at a given instant rarely and within a window often. A
stationary membrane enters a passing state within \uOccContentLongMs{}~ms, the
integration time that precedes a conscious percept, with probability
\uOccHitMinLong{} to \uOccHitMaxLong{}, including the case in which the region
it depends on supplies only \uOccShareMin{} of its membrane's noise variance. If
two regions are joined by \uOccCarriersFew{} carriers that pass independently,
some carrier passes in such a window with probability at least
\uOccLinkFewLong{}, and with \uOccCarriersMid{} within \uOccContentShortMs{}~ms
with probability at least \uOccLinkMidShort{}; for independent carriers the
probability that none passes falls geometrically with their number. At a single
instant, even \uOccCarriersMany{} such carriers link two regions with
probability as low as \uOccInstantLinkMin{}, which is why P is stated per
window. Reading the spike time through a clock of \uOccLatchShortMs{} or
\uOccLatchLongMs{}~ms leaves the passing fraction unchanged.

Neighbouring membranes share input, and in awake cortex their potentials are
highly correlated during quiet wakefulness and less so during active behaviour
\citep{poulet2008}. We model this as a shared component carrying a fraction $c$
of every membrane's noise variance, so that two membranes correlate by $c$ and
each keeps its own law (Appendix~\ref{app:occupancy}). At least one of
\uOccCarriersMid{} such carriers enters a passing state within
\uOccContentLongMs{}~ms with probability at least \uCorLinkMidTol{} for every
share up to $c=\uCorShareTol$, in every regime, and
at least one of \uOccCarriersMany{} with probability at least \uCorLinkManyTol{}. The chance
that \uOccCarriersMid{} carriers leave the window without a link is at most
\uCorMissA{} at $c=\uCorShareA$, \uCorMissB{} at $c=\uCorShareB$ and
\uCorMissC{} at $c=\uCorShareTol$. The shorter window is more sensitive to
correlation: within \uOccContentShortMs{}~ms the link falls to
\uCorLinkShortTol{} at $c=\uCorShareTol$, so P's per-window condition is met at
the time scale of a percept rather than of its fastest components. What
separates the membrane from the restored bit is the distance from threshold at
which each carrier is held when it is read, a few noise amplitudes against
\ueMarginToNoise{}.

\paragraph{A switching chip, counted the same way.} A chip's nodes also pass
through their thresholds, many times in every window, and the protocol counts
them too (Appendix~\ref{app:occupancy}). Take a \uSwClockGHz{}~GHz clock and
nodes that switch in \uSwActivityLow{} to \uSwActivityHigh{} of its cycles, each
transition lasting \uSwTransitionPs{}~ps. On the rising half of a transition a
change of one noise amplitude moves the time of the crossing by one jitter, as
it moves a spike time. If a node's next content were that time, the node would
pass there, at \uSwNodePassMinPercent{} to \uSwNodePassMaxPercent\% of its
states, within the membrane's range, and within every window of
\uOccContentShortMs{}~ms except with probability below
$10^{-\uSwNoTransitionOrders}$. Counted at the voltage, a chip passes per window
as a membrane does. By the carrier condition of Section~\ref{sec:cover}, its
next content is what its latch reads at the clock edge, and timing closure keeps every transition on a clocked path outside the latch's
setup-and-hold window \citep{ginosar2011}, so the latch reads the node at its
rail: the restored bit above, which passes at none of its states and in no
window. The crossing a latch discards is the content a membrane delivers,
because a membrane's readers integrate its spike times as they arrive. The one
place a working chip latches a transition is a synchronizer between clock
domains. With a \uSwAperturePs{}~ps aperture and data changing every tenth
cycle \citep{ginosar2011}, its first stage samples inside the aperture at most
\uSwSyncPassPercent\% of the time, $\uSwSyncEventsLong$ times in a window of
\uOccContentLongMs{}~ms, and which way it resolves moves with the timing of the
transition; a second stage given one cycle to settle leaves it unresolved once
in more than $10^{\uSwMtbfOrders}$ years. What that second stage takes is the bit
the first resolves to, and no binary readout reaches the fluctuation: at the
centre of the aperture a change of one noise amplitude in the transition's
timing moves the probability of either resolution by at most
$\Phi(1)-1/2=\uYardBinaryCeiling$, and the stage's own noise only lowers that.
The synchronizer therefore fails graded dependence at every sample, those inside
its aperture included, whichever protocol carries the data across the boundary,
and so does a random source that reads its bits from how a metastable stage
resolves. The value a chip reads from one bit passes nowhere, within a clock
domain or across its boundaries.

\paragraph{The yardstick.} The separation holds at every yardstick from
\uYardLow{} to \uYardHigh{} (Figure~\ref{fig:yardstick}). Across that range the logic node passes at none of
its states, and the membrane enters a passing state within
\uOccContentLongMs{}~ms with probability at least \uYardHitMinHigh{} at the
highest yardstick, rising as the yardstick is lowered. The formal results take
the yardstick as a parameter and hold at every value of it. The fluctuation is
the value that makes \emph{large} mean what it means for the carrier itself: a
change that moves the content by as much as the content moves of its own
accord.

\section{When and how far a coupling enforces agreement}
\label{sec:reach}

P asks two things of the coupling: that it pull the disagreement of unified
contents back before the content changes, and that it reach every pair of
regions whose contents are unified. The first gives the prediction that the
restoration test of Section~\ref{sec:tests} checks, and the second says over
what distances that test should find restoration.

\paragraph{Agreement in time.} A contraction that finishes after the content has
changed enforces agreement on a content already replaced, so enforced agreement
has a timing condition at both ends. It arrives no sooner than the coupling's
causal cone allows: a report at one site that tracks a variable at another to
within $\varepsilon$, for two values more than $2\varepsilon$ apart, is possible
after $T$ rounds of the coupling only if the second site lies in the first
site's causal past for $T$ rounds. It arrives at the coupling's relaxation rate:
if the coupling shrinks the overlap discrepancy by a factor $\kappa<1$ at every
step, on a set of states it keeps, a discrepancy that starts at most $d_0$ is at
most $\varepsilon$ from step $\log(d_0/\varepsilon)/\log(1/\kappa)$ on, and one
that shrinks by at most a factor $\rho$ per step is still above $\varepsilon$ at
every step before $\log(d_0/\varepsilon)/\log(1/\rho)$. Unity at the time scale
of a content requires the later of the two edges to fall before the content
changes. In cortex, signal travel uses at most \ueConeSlowMs{}~ms of the
\ueContentMs{}~ms that precede a conscious percept, so the condition falls on
the coupling's relaxation time (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). P therefore
predicts a cortical relaxation time of at most \ueTauFastMs{}~ms across the
cortex and \ueTauLocalMs{}~ms within a posterior zone, and a slower restoration
counts against it.

\paragraph{Reach.} The pairs lie up to \ueFibreCm{}~cm apart if a scene's
contents span the cortex, and about \ueLocalCm{}~cm if they are confined to a
posterior zone. On a noisy sheet of coupled oscillators sharing one natural
frequency, in the harmonic approximation, the variance of the phase difference
between two sites is the noise strength times their \emph{effective
resistance}: the resistance a meter would read between them if each coupling
were a wire whose conductance is the coupling's strength. Classical results on
resistance then decide how far phase agreement reaches
(Appendix~\ref{app:reach}). Under coupling of bounded range the resistance
grows at least logarithmically with distance, the Gaussian form of the absence
of true long-range order in two dimensions \citep{mermin1966}, and a kernel
whose tail falls off more slowly than $r^{-4}$ bounds it at every distance
\citep{chung1951,kunz1976}. A Lipschitz encoder, whose output changes by at
most $L$ times any change in its input, carries phase agreement to content
agreement: at noise strength $D$ the root-mean-square content discrepancy
between two sites is at most $L\sqrt{D\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}}$. In cortex the
long-range part of the coupling is synaptic, since the field of a local source
falls below detection within millimetres (Section~\ref{sec:tests}), so at
centimetre distances the restoration test measures synaptic coupling.

\section{Current artificial systems}
\label{sec:ai}

A clocked digital chip is an assortment of separate records. Each bit is held
in place by its own noise margin, and below that margin it varies independently
of every other bit and of the voltages beneath it. The records agree only when
the program, applied at each clock step, makes them agree. A transformer running
on such hardware reads every content from bits held within their margins, so the
result of Section~\ref{sec:enforcement} applies to it: its agreement is
computed, whatever it computes. Hardware whose contents are graded physical
quantities coupled without barriers, such as analog, in-memory or neuromorphic
substrates, can satisfy P.

A chip tracking the bird of Section~\ref{sec:cover} holds each area's estimate
as a word of bits. The two words agree when a step of the program copies or
averages one into the other, and between steps neither moves with the voltages
beneath the other, however close the bird comes to a boundary between two of
its values.

\paragraph{Symbols and physical contents.} The line P draws is between symbolic
and physical content, and the line between digital and analog hardware is its
most common instance. A \emph{symbol}, in the sense the margin lemma makes
precise, is a content read through a noise margin: a physical state whose
identity survives every small enough change of the state that carries it. Two
symbols therefore agree only when a rule applied at discrete steps makes them
agree. A \emph{physical content} is a graded quantity that the system's own
dynamics reads, so its agreement with the contents it overlaps is produced by
the coupling between them. Symbols are physical objects too; what sets them
apart is the margin through which they are read. A bit is a symbol, and so is a
spike count latched at a clock edge. A spike time, a membrane potential and the
phase of a coupled oscillator are physical contents. P requires physical
contents, and a system whose contents are all symbols computes its agreement,
whatever algorithm it runs.

\paragraph{Organisation, not material.} The criterion concerns how a substrate is
organised and says nothing of what it is made of. An analog crossbar whose
column currents carry content and settle through shared conductances depends
gradedly on every input voltage. An array of coupled electronic or optical
oscillators shrinks phase disagreement through a physical coupling exactly as a
cortical sheet does. A neuromorphic chip passes where its contents are read from
membrane potentials coupled continuously, and fails where they are read from
spike counts latched at a clock edge. Biological tissue passes or fails on the
same terms: a neural circuit whose contents were read only from all-or-none
events latched far from threshold would fall under the margin lemma as any
digital circuit does.

\paragraph{Analog in-memory accelerators.} In analog in-memory hardware a
layer's products are currents summed on the lines of a memory array, which is
graded, and are then digitized. In a 64-core phase-change chip each row's
current is converted by its own analog-to-digital converter into integers, and
activation functions and the traffic between cores run on those integers
\citep{legallo2023}. The next layer's content is then read from bits, so the
margin lemma applies to every content that crosses a converter, and such a chip
fails P between layers as a digital chip does; its graded stage lies inside
each product and couples no two contents. A design passes only where coupled
graded contents meet with no restoring stage between them: column currents
driving the next array directly, or oscillators and membranes coupled through
shared conductances, as above.

\paragraph{Why spikes are not bits.} Spikes are all-or-none events, and what
makes them graded carriers is their timing. When a membrane potential rises
through threshold, the moment it crosses moves continuously with any small
input, and the receiving cells integrate spike times as they arrive rather than
sampling them. Spike timing therefore carries graded dependence from one region
to the next. A clocked latch compares the time of a transition with the clock
edge, which puts a threshold on the transition time, so the margin lemma
applies with that time as the thresholded quantity. The clock alone does not
separate the substrates: a spike time read through a clock still passes near
threshold, as measured above. What a digital circuit adds is restoration. Each
gate drives its output back to a rail, so every transition the latch reads
starts and ends far from any threshold, and the clock then removes the graded
timing that spikes keep.

\paragraph{Clockless logic.} Asynchronous circuits have no clock. In a
quasi-delay-insensitive design each stage signals completion to the next
through a handshake, and correctness holds under every assignment of gate and
wire delays apart from a few forks assumed isochronic \citep{martin2006}. Their
transition times vary gradedly with every voltage, as a spike time does. The
design makes the computed values independent of those times: a value is
accepted once its code is complete, whatever delays produced it. The timing of
such a circuit is therefore a trace in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:cover}. It
moves with the physics, and the circuit's own contents are read from completed
codes, each held at a rail by restoring gates, so they meet the margin lemma
as a clocked circuit's contents do.

\paragraph{Leakage.} Real digital hardware leaks: supply noise, crosstalk and
temperature move the analog quantities behind every bit. The margin lemma says
where such leakage can reach content. If a content read as a word of
thresholded continuous quantities depends gradedly on any region, some bit of
the next state sits exactly on its threshold, and correct operation keeps every
state off that set. Digital logic does pull perturbations back, but each gate
does so alone: its gain pulls a disturbed voltage back toward its own rail. That
restores each bit toward its own logical value, pulls no region toward agreement
with another, and is what keeps the bits inside their margins. The chip's
records stay separate even in the one place where its physics contracts.

The margin also has a width. If every bit of the next state is at least
$\delta$ from its threshold, each bit is read from an $L_v$-Lipschitz quantity
and the micro dynamics is $L$-Lipschitz, then a change to one region smaller
than $\delta/(L L_v)$ changes no bit. Leakage below that scale reaches content
only through the error rate (Section~\ref{sec:enforcement}), and leakage above
it flips a bit, which is a logical error. Digital design keeps ordinary noise
well below that scale: a textbook inverter's static noise margin is
\ueMarginPercent{}\% of its supply voltage, against an expected supply
variation of \ueSupplyPercent{}\% \citep{rabaey2003}, so the expected variation
is \ueNoiseToMargin{} of the margin. The nondeterminism of floating-point
reduction order is a choice among logical trajectories and lies outside both
results: it changes which content is computed, not how content depends on
graded state.

\paragraph{The margin's width.} If P turned on how wide an engineer happened to
make a noise margin, that would be a reductio of P: two machines running one
program would differ in unity by a design parameter. The width is not free in
that way. By the noisy margin theorem a content's response to a region is at
most its error rate, so a machine whose contents reached the fluctuation at the
noise scale would err with probability at least \ueFluctuationTV{} at every
step. Its bits would fail to hold their values from one step to the next, its
program's trajectory would stop being reproducible, and whatever agreement
survived would come from the physics beneath the bits and not from the program.
The line P draws therefore coincides with the physical line between digital and
analog operation, and working systems sit far from it on either side: a logic
node's margin is \ueMarginToNoise{} noise amplitudes wide, while a cortical
membrane sits on average \uOccDistanceMin{} to \uOccDistanceMax{} noise
amplitudes below its threshold and reaches a passing state within every window
of a percept's time scale.

\paragraph{Attractor basins.} Cortex has margins of its own. An attractor
network's basin is a dynamical margin: every state in it flows to the same
attractor, so which attractor the network settles in survives every small
enough change. This is the categorical content of Section~\ref{sec:enforcement}
in dynamical form. The basin gives the attractor's label a margin and leaves
graded the state that carries the label: the position along a continuous
attractor, such as the bump of activity that encodes a direction, the membrane
potentials within the basin, and the spike times on the way to the attractor.
Downstream cells integrate those. The voltage beneath a bit has no such reader,
since the chip reads it only through the threshold. A cortical circuit whose
contents were read only as attractor labels, far from the basins' boundaries,
would fall under the margin lemma as a chip does, and the weak nudge of
Section~\ref{sec:tests} tests which case cortex is in.

\section{Tests}
\label{sec:tests}

\paragraph{What counts as evidence about experience.} Every test below measures
decoded agreement: agreement of the contents available to readout. If a split in
readout could always be dismissed as a split in access alone, with experience
still unified behind it, no observation would bear on P. So the evidence about
experience itself is fixed in advance. It consists of responses that combine
stimuli across the regions in question when the task requires it, and reports of
a single field of experience spanning them. A dissociation counts as evidence
against P if such responses and reports split while graded coupling between the
regions is intact, or stay integrated after graded coupling between them is
removed.

This standard binds P. The answer to gradual replacement in
Section~\ref{sec:enforcement} lets reports stay the same while unity changes
only in a system built to reproduce them by computation. A bridged cortex is
not such a system: each region still reads its contents from its own coupled
carriers, only the connection between two regions changes, and the quantized
loop is built to match the analog loop's information, not its effect on
behaviour. In such a system behaviour is evidence about unity, so if P holds,
removing graded coupling between two regions shows in the responses and
reports fixed above, and a split of unity behind unchanged reports is no
answer P can give to a null result.

\paragraph{Separating coupling from information.} This test asks whether unity
tracks the graded grain or the information the regions exchange. In every manipulation of cortex available so far, graded coupling
and information flow move together: anaesthesia and callosal section remove
both. A test of the grain must change one while holding the other fixed. Stimulating the regions from outside does not
separate them. Coherence between cortical regions arises largely as a
consequence of communication between them \citep{schneider2021}, and
transcranial alternating current applied to two regions drives both from a
common source: where in-phase stimulation raised their synchrony, it raised
directed information flow with it \citep{reinhart2019}. The coupling has to be
supplied instead.

The setup is a bridge (Figure~\ref{fig:bridge}). Two regions whose natural
connection is interrupted are
reconnected by stimulating each in proportion to the activity recorded at the
other, through one of two loops. The \emph{analog loop}'s output moves with every
graded change in its input. It is analog end to end, an analog filter and
amplifier driving the stimulator with no digitizing stage, because a loop that
digitizes the recording before driving the stimulator would run the quantized
condition in both arms. The \emph{quantized loop} is the same filter and
amplifier with a quantizer between them, so its output is read from bits and has
a margin. The two loops share one filter shape, and they differ in the
quantizer and in one cutoff frequency, set below.

The two loops must carry the same information and fall on opposite sides of P.
Section~\ref{sec:enforcement} fixes what the second condition means: a change
of the recorded region by its own noise amplitude must move the analog loop's
output by at least the fluctuation, and the quantized loop's output by less.
The analog loop passes exactly when its noise, in the region's units, is at
most the region's own. The quantized loop fails at every state farther from a
quantization edge than the region's noise plus \ueEdgeQuantile{} times the
recording noise, so its step must be many times both. Matching information by
adding noise to the analog loop defeats this. At matched information the added
noise is \ueMatchedNoiseToStep{} of the quantizer's step, so an analog loop
that still passes caps the step at \ueNoiseMatchMaxStep{} noise amplitudes of
the region, and the quantized loop then fails at no more than
\ueNoiseMatchFailPercent\% of its states, taking states as uniform within a
step. The analog loop's bandwidth is lowered instead, with no noise added,
until the transfer entropy it carries, estimated from the recorded activity,
matches the quantized loop's. Lowering the bandwidth slows the loop, and a loop
that settles after the content has changed fails P's timing condition
(Section~\ref{sec:reach}) however gradedly it couples. A first-order filter
with cutoff $f_c$ settles with time constant $1/(2\pi f_c)$, and the bridged
coupling relaxes no faster than its loop, so the analog loop's cutoff is kept
at or above \ueBridgeMinCutoffHz{}~Hz, whose time constant equals the shortest
relaxation time that brings agreement in time in Appendix~\ref{app:window}. If
matching would take the cutoff lower, the quantizer's step is coarsened
instead, which lowers the quantized loop's transfer entropy and raises the
share of its states that fail. A null result then cannot come from an analog
loop too slow for the content's window. Above that floor the step is free: at a step of
\ueBridgeStepToNoise{} noise amplitudes, with recording noise no larger than
the region's own, the quantized loop fails at \ueBandMatchFailPercent\% of its
states or more. Stepping the quantized loop's resolution checks that its
transfer entropy rises with the resolution, so that the resolution, and not
recording noise, sets the matched value.

Transfer entropy counts everything one region's activity tells about the
other's, and the loops can match it while differing in how much of the task
variable gets through, since the quantizer and the lower cutoff distort the
waveform differently. A behavioural difference between the loops would then
have an explanation at the level of information. So the task variable is also
decoded from the current each loop delivers, by a decoder fitted on other
blocks of the same loop, and the loops count as matched only when its
decodability, as well as the transfer entropy, agrees between them within the
confidence interval of the difference. The step and the cutoff are two free
settings for these two conditions: a step coarse enough to fail P, and a cutoff
above the floor, are chosen together on pilot blocks, and the check is repeated
on the blocks that are compared. The two loops then carry the same information,
in total and about the task, through the same filter shape, and only the analog
one couples the regions gradedly. An account on which unity follows the
information regions exchange predicts no difference between them.

The readout is unity, with the evidence fixed at the start of this section. The
bridge holds information flow fixed and varies the grain of the coupling. Unity
that follows the analog loop at matched transfer entropy and task
decodability is evidence that unity
tracks the finer grain, and not only the information the regions exchange;
unity that is the same under both loops counts against P. In animals the
design measures unity by integration across the bridged connection in behaviour
and by decoded agreement in the recordings; reported unity needs a bridged human
preparation.

\paragraph{A first preparation.} Closed loops that bridge two cortical sites
already run for days in behaving animals. Spikes recorded at one site have
triggered stimulation at another in freely moving monkeys \citep{jackson2006}
and in rats after cortical injury \citep{guggenmos2013}. Those loops are
triggered by events, which is the quantized condition; the analog arm replaces
the trigger with a continuous drive. A first preparation places chronic arrays in
two areas that represent a shared variable, such as the visual and parietal
areas that both represent the bird's location in the running example, interrupts the natural connection
between them, and alternates the two loops across blocks in the same animal.
Transfer entropy \citep{schreiber2000} is estimated from each block's
recordings, and the analog loop's cutoff and the quantizer's step are set on
pilot blocks so that the loops match in transfer entropy and in the
decodability of the task variable from their delivered currents, with the
cutoff above the floor that keeps the analog loop in time. The primary readout is
behavioural: a task that requires the animal to combine the values the two
areas represent, such as judging whether stimuli in their two fields match,
whose accuracy is compared between the loops. The companion's excess decoded
agreement between the two areas is the neural readout, and the number of trials
follows from the power of the weaker of the two comparisons.

\paragraph{Stimulation artefact.} Event-triggered loops blank the recording
around each pulse, and a continuous drive leaves no interval to blank. The loops
therefore record and stimulate in separate frequency bands. Each loop reads
multi-unit activity, above a few hundred hertz, takes its envelope, and drives
the other region with a current that follows the envelope and so lies in the
band below it. Rectifying and smoothing are analog operations, so the analog
loop stays analog end to end. What leaks across the bands is removed by
subtracting the loop's own output, which the loop knows exactly, passed through
the transfer from stimulator to recording contact measured on open-loop
calibration blocks. Both loops end in the same output stage, which smooths the
quantizer's steps into the stimulation band, and share the stimulator, the
bands and the subtraction, so a residual artefact reaches both arms by one
path. Open-loop blocks in which each stimulator follows activity recorded in
another block measure that residual in each arm, and the comparison of the
loops is made after it is removed.

\paragraph{A weak nudge should spread.} Weak stimulation of one region, below the
level that changes behaviour, should shift the content decoded in an overlapping
region, in proportion to the stimulation's strength and toward agreement with
the stimulated region. Transcranial alternating current and weak intracortical
fields supply the nudge. The decoded content must be a graded variable, such as
a location, an orientation or the strength of evidence for a percept, read from
the population activity that the overlapping region's targets integrate, so that
it is a carrier in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:cover}. A decoded category would
not do: it is constant away from its boundaries in any substrate, and would stay
unchanged whether P holds or not. If the decoded content of the overlapping
region stays unchanged at strengths that measurably move the stimulated one,
that counts against P, since P then denies unity to the very system whose unity
motivates it. A change alone confirms little, because most accounts expect
stimulating one region to alter activity in regions connected to it. P predicts
the change's form. The decoded content of the overlapping region shifts with
the sign of the shift induced in the stimulated region, as agreement requires,
and in proportion to stimulation strength down to the weakest strength that
measurably moves the stimulated region, with no strength below which the shift
vanishes. A shift of the wrong sign, or one that appears only above a threshold
strength, counts against P.

\paragraph{Agreement should be restored.} Perturb one region and measure whether
its agreement with its overlap partners comes back, and how fast. In cortex the
perturbation is stimulation, and the measure is the disagreement between
contents decoded from the perturbed region and from its partners. P predicts
restoration within the relaxation time of Section~\ref{sec:reach}, with a restoring
pull proportional to the size of the perturbation, below any threshold as well
as above. The same protocol run on an artificial system, by patching the
activations of one layer, head or position, has an outcome fixed in advance by
the margin lemma. A digital system can restore agreement by computation, above
the scale at which its logical trajectory changes, and below that scale nothing
moves. Observing this checks that the hardware operates inside its margins. It
is also the contrast that makes the cortical outcome informative: a restoring
pull that stays graded below every threshold is the signature P attributes to
enforcement.

\paragraph{The field's causal cone.} Brain tissue carries an electric field as
well as synapses. At the frequencies of neural activity that field is
quasi-static \citep{gratiy2017}: it reaches every point of the tissue it spans
with no propagation delay. Synaptic influence between distant regions, by
contrast, arrives only after axonal conduction and synaptic transmission, no
sooner than \ueConeMaxMs{}~ms across the longest cortico-cortical fibres. If two
regions re-agree after a local perturbation faster than the synaptic route
allows, the coupling has a non-synaptic part, and the timing measures the reach
of the kernel tail of Section~\ref{sec:reach} directly.

The field's strength sets the distance at which this test can decide anything.
Endogenous fields in cortex peak near \ueFieldPeak{}~mV/mm in vivo
\citep{frohlich2010}, networks of neurons detect fields from
\ueFieldDetection{}~mV/mm \citep{francis2003}, and the potential of a local
cortical source decays as $r^{-2.1}$ across millimetres \citep{rebollo2021}.
With the peak placed at the nearest distance of that measurement, the field of a
local source falls to the detection threshold within \ueFieldReachMm{}~mm, and
misses it by \ueFieldDeficitOrders{} orders of magnitude at \ueFibreCm{}~cm and
by \ueFieldDeficitLocalOrders{} at \ueLocalCm{}~cm.
The field's cone is therefore tested at millimetre distances, where activity
has been observed to synchronize across a cut through cortex by the field alone
\citep{rebollo2021}. At centimetre distances P predicts that re-agreement
arrives through synaptic coupling, which is graded as well.

The timing can be measured with intracortical recording, with care. The
stimulation artefact saturates clinical amplifiers during the first
milliseconds, masking the earliest monosynaptic responses \citep{keller2014},
and early responses at distant contacts carry a volume-conducted component that
scales with distance as the artefact does \citep{prime2020}. Three measures
separate active re-agreement from the passive field that carries the stimulus:
template subtraction of the artefact \citep{trebaul2016}; multi-unit activity or
current-source density at the distant site, which the passive spread of the
local field potential does not reach \citep{kajikawa2011}; and a deadline set
for each subject from the tractography length of the connecting fibres
\citep{caminiti2013}.

\paragraph{Split brain.} Cutting the corpus callosum removes the main route of
both information and graded coupling between the hemispheres. Patients respond
to stimuli in the two visual fields as if perception were divided, yet can
report the presence of stimuli in either field with either hand. This has been
read as divided perception with undivided consciousness \citep{pinto2017}.
Whether the field of experience divides is contested \citep{dehaan2020}, and on
a rival switch model consciousness alternates between the hemispheres
\citep{bayne2010}. The remaining subcortical routes are synaptic, so they couple
gradedly and carry information at once. The case therefore removes coupling and
information together, which is exactly the confound the bridged preparation
above is built to break.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Indicator properties.} \citet{butlin2023} assess current AI systems
against indicator properties of consciousness drawn from scientific theories and
stated computationally, and they adopt computational functionalism as a working
hypothesis, which states every indicator at the grain of an algorithm. P adds to
any list of indicators one stated at the grain of graded dynamics, a condition
on how the substrate realises them, so a system can have every computational
indicator and fail P, as a faithful simulation of cortex does
(Section~\ref{sec:enforcement}). The bridge of Section~\ref{sec:tests} holds
the information two regions exchange fixed and asks whether unity tracks the
graded grain.

\paragraph{Computational closure.} The closest formal neighbour is the account of
\citet{rosas2024}. On it, a macroscopic level that is informationally, causally
and computationally closed marks emergent software in a physical system. This
closure is the property Section~\ref{sec:enforcement} places at the content
level of a digital system, and P reads it the other way round: a level closed
off from the graded state beneath it is one whose agreement is computed, not
enforced.

\paragraph{Designed to suppress its physics.} \citet{kleiner2024} reach a related
exclusion from a different premise. If the physical dynamics of a system is
relevant to its consciousness, processors designed and verified to suppress
deviations from their computational dynamics cannot be conscious. The margin
lemma states that suppression formally, and three things are added to it
here. The criterion's scale is fixed by the parts' own noise, so it counts
systems instead of asking whether physics is relevant at all. The coupling must
contract disagreement, which a substrate meets or fails separately from how it
suppresses noise. And the bridge of Section~\ref{sec:tests} asks in cortex
whether unity tracks the physical dynamics or the information they carry.

\paragraph{Biology and the medium.} \citet{seth2026} argues that consciousness
may depend on biological properties that computation does not capture. P
locates the relevant property in how the substrate is organised, and admits
non-biological hardware that has it (Section~\ref{sec:ai}). Mortal computation,
whose parameters are useful only on the hardware instance that learned them
\citep{hinton2022,ororbia2023}, shares P's refusal to separate the computation
from the medium.

\paragraph{Analog and digital.} On the mechanistic account of
\citet{piccinini2008}, a digital computer manipulates strings of digits, states
its components distinguish reliably, and which class a system belongs to is
fixed by its mechanism. P's symbol, a content read through a noise margin, is
that reliability stated as a property of the readout, with the margin measured
in the parts' own noise; P then asks what the reliability costs, namely graded
dependence below the margin. \citet{maley2011} separates analog from continuous
and digital from discrete representation, and P's line follows the second
pair, not the first: a spike time is a physical content whether or not it
represents by analogy, and a continuous quantity latched through a margin is a
symbol. \citet{maudlin1989} argues that a computation's counterfactual
structure can be carried by machinery that is idle on the actual run, which
sits badly with consciousness supervening on present physical activity. P
states its counterfactual condition on present activity: graded dependence
asks how the actual carriers respond to changes of the actual state, above
their noise, and idle machinery responds to none.

\paragraph{Integrated information.} Integrated information theory also denies
experience to digital computers running faithful simulations of a brain
\citep{tononi2015,findlay2024}. It does so through the causal structure of the
hardware at the grain of its logic gates. P turns instead on how content depends
on graded physical state, and on whether disagreement is pulled back. Its
verdict on a machine is read from a measurement that runs on both substrates:
the noise-floor test of Section~\ref{sec:enforcement}, applied to a cortical
membrane and to a logic node by one protocol.

\paragraph{Field theories.} The electromagnetic field theory of
\citet{mcfadden2020} identifies consciousness with the brain's field, supported
by evidence that endogenous fields entrain cortical activity
\citep{frohlich2010}. \citet{miller2026} place cognition and consciousness in
travelling waves that synaptic and ephaptic coupling sustain together, and take
consciousness to arise when those waves bring cortex into a globally integrated
state. Their analog computation is set apart from digital computation by
running in parallel. P treats the field, and the waves it carries, as ways
among several to realize graded coupling, and draws the line between analog
and digital at the noise margin, which a parallel digital machine keeps.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The premise.} P is a premise and is not derived from the dynamics.
The formal results show what follows from it, and the cortical tests of
Section~\ref{sec:tests} bear on whether it holds. It is necessary
for unity and not sufficient for consciousness: coupled pendulums satisfy it.
The argument for it takes the mereological account of phenomenal unity, and an
account on which unity is joint access to contents rejects its first step.
The count makes two carriers two systems when a single change of one between
its noise and its threshold leaves the other unmoved, which is stricter than a
product decomposition; the two agree on records and graded readers, and a carrier that
registers some changes in that range and not others is two systems by the count
and may be one by a product decomposition.
A functionalism that states its roles at the grain of graded physical state
agrees with P on every test here, and it is the intended reading of P: the
tests ask which grain unity tracks, not whether a functional account of unity
is correct.

\item \emph{Hardware near its thresholds.} The noisy margin theorem bounds a
digital content's response by its error rate. Hardware run so close to its
thresholds that a change of one noise amplitude moves its contents by a
fluctuation would escape the bound. One bit cannot, under Gaussian noise, since
no binary readout reaches the fluctuation, so such hardware would read one
graded quantity through several bits held near their thresholds together, a
design the switching count does not cover. The thermal estimate declares the node capacitance
it uses, and the switching count declares its clock, activity factors,
transition time and synchronizer.

\item \emph{The cortical estimates.} The single-input estimate combines in vitro
measurements of one connection type and one noise recording, and reads the
spike-time shift through a linear threshold crossing. The occupancy result models
one membrane as a leaky integrate-and-fire cell under white noise, with a
threshold that has no dynamics of its own. Its time constants, rates, windows,
noise shares and carrier counts are declared ranges, not fits. Correlation
between carriers is modelled as one shared component with the membrane's own
time constant, at declared shares; the measured correlations it is compared with
are those of neighbouring cells, and the single spikes of awake cortex are
driven by input that neighbouring cells do not share \citep{poulet2008}, which
that model omits. The carrier counts stay far below the anatomical ones, and the
per-window bound is proved formally in two forms: for independent carriers,
and without independence, where the chance that no carrier passes is at most
the variance of the number that pass over the square of its mean, which
correlation between carriers keeps above a floor however many there are.

\item \emph{Reach.} The identification of phase variance with effective
resistance rests on the harmonic approximation at identical natural frequencies.
The logarithmic lower bound on resistance is proved for kernels of bounded
range. The bound at every distance under a tail slower than $r^{-4}$ is proved
for a sheet that supplies the site counts and couplings of a dyadic chain of
discs. That a kernel of finite second moment leaves the resistance unbounded is
the classical recurrence criterion, for translation-invariant kernels on the
lattice; that the growth is logarithmic is established numerically on finite
sheets. Above the order--disorder crossover of Appendix~\ref{app:reach} the
harmonic identity fails and agreement is lost at every distance. The step from
phase agreement to content agreement assumes one oscillator population per
content patch, which is a modelling choice about cortex, and across centimetres
it is the phase-to-content assumption of Appendix~\ref{app:reach}.

\item \emph{Which variables are contents.} Which variables a system's own
dynamics reads, and so which are its contents, is settled system by system from
its circuitry. The margin lemma takes that identification as given. For
attractor networks the answer takes the unified contents to be carried by the
graded state within a basin; a cortex whose unified contents were basin labels
alone would fail P.

\item \emph{Artificial systems.} The predictions for artificial systems are
consequences of the margin lemma. Confirming them checks that hardware runs
inside its margins and leaves P untested; the tests of P are the cortical ones.

\item \emph{Agreement in time.} Its edges are proved for a given contraction
factor; identifying that factor with the coupling's spectral gap is linear
response. The relaxation time of cortical coupling is unmeasured, so the cortical
estimate states the condition for agreement to arrive in time, and whether cortex
meets it is the prediction the restoration test checks. It also reads agreement as a declared
reduction factor of disagreement.

\item \emph{The field.} A long-range tail improves agreement only in proportion
to its conductance against the short-range part. The field of a local cortical
source falls below the detection threshold within millimetres, by an estimate
that places the field's peak at a declared distance. Across centimetres graded
coupling is carried chiefly by synapses, and the field's contribution there is a
quantity to be measured.

\item \emph{The bridge's reach.} The bridge holds information flow fixed and
varies the grain of the coupling, so it separates the graded grain from the
grain of information exchanged. A functional account that counts the
quantizer's nonlinearity, or the cutoff that matches information, as a
difference between the loops can accommodate either outcome. Such an account
individuates roles finely enough to see a quantizer, which is the grain P names,
so the bridge tells P's grain from the grain of information and leaves that
account on P's side of the line.

\item \emph{Human evidence.} The dissociation of coupling from information has,
so far, no human preparation in which reported unity can be measured across a
bridged connection. Human data can falsify P but cannot by themselves tell the
graded grain from the grain of information exchanged, because graded coupling and information
flow co-vary in every manipulation available so far.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reach: resistance, encoders and simulations}
\label{app:reach}

The logarithmic growth is a lower bound for every kernel of bounded range. Group
the sites into shells around a site $a$, so that the coupling joins only sites
in the same or adjacent shells. The shells then act as conductors in series,
and the effective conductance from $a$ to any site beyond shell $n$ is at most
$\bigl(\sum_{k<n} 1/C_k\bigr)^{-1}$, where $C_k$ is the coupling crossing from
shell $k$ to shell $k+1$. On a planar sheet
the perimeter of shell $k$ grows linearly in $k$, so $C_k \le c\,(k+1)$ for a
constant $c$ set by the kernel's strength and width, and the effective
resistance is at least $\log(n+1)/c$.
Widening the kernel enlarges its shells and raises $c$, which slows the growth
and leaves it unbounded. Distance-independent agreement on a sheet therefore
needs coupling of unbounded range.

A tail slower than $r^{-4}$ bounds the resistance at every distance. Run a chain
of site sets out from $a$ through discs of radius $2^j$ and back in to $b$, the
two widest discs spanning the separation. The phase difference across $a$ and
$b$ telescopes through the discs' mean phases, and the drop between the means of
two consecutive discs is bounded by the coupling energy between them, shared
among the pairs of their sites. A disc of radius $2^j$ on a planar sheet holds at
least $\alpha\,4^j$ sites, and a kernel $K(r)\gtrsim r^{-(2+\sigma)}$ couples
two sites of consecutive discs by at least $\beta\,2^{-(2+\sigma)j}$. The
number of pairs grows as $16^j$ and their coupling falls as $2^{-(2+\sigma)j}$,
so the drops shrink geometrically with ratio $q=2^{-(2-\sigma)/2}$, which is
below one for $\sigma<2$, and the effective conductance is at least
$(1-q)^2\alpha^2\beta/8$ however far apart $a$ and $b$ lie.

Both results match classical potential theory. Imagine a random walker on the
lattice that jumps between sites with probabilities proportional to the kernel,
which is the same at every site. The resistance from a site to infinity is
infinite exactly when that walker is sure to return to its start, that is, when
the walk is recurrent \citep{doyle1984}. Shorting every site outside a disc
around $a$ can only lower the resistance from $a$ to a site $b$ beyond the disc,
so under recurrence that resistance grows without bound as $b$ recedes. A
symmetric kernel of finite second moment, which includes every tail steeper
than $r^{-4}$, gives a recurrent walk in two dimensions \citep{chung1951}. The
bound above is the matching statement for tails slower than $r^{-4}$, whose
walks escape. The same threshold holds beyond the harmonic approximation: the
plane rotator in two dimensions with coupling falling as $r^{-\alpha}$,
$2<\alpha<4$, has true long-range order at low noise \citep{kunz1976}, which
short-range coupling forbids. The plane-rotator sheet also has a
Kosterlitz--Thouless transition \citep{frohlich1981}, an order--disorder
crossover that the simulations below locate. When natural frequencies differ
from site to site, short-range coupling fares worse still: phases then
desynchronize in every dimension up to four \citep{hong2005}.

Two further properties bound the resistance from above. Adding coupling never
raises it, and a direct coupling caps it at its reciprocal. Across a finite
cortex a wide short-range kernel and a slow tail differ by a slowly growing
logarithm, so telling them apart takes a measurement of the kernel's second
moment as well as of its tail.

An encoder that reads the phases of a small neighbourhood relative to the phase
at its centre does better than the Lipschitz bound of Section~\ref{sec:reach}.
It reports one content at every site of a travelling plane wave or a winding,
whatever the gradient, and for a wave plus a departure from it its discrepancy
between two sites is at most its Lipschitz constant times the departure's
relative readings there, so it pays for the local roughness of the field and
for nothing else. Both bounds read one oscillator population per content patch.
Across centimetres, where two territories describing one quantity need not be
neighbours on the sheet, that reading is an assumption, the
\emph{phase-to-content assumption}: each territory's content is read, by
encoders of Lipschitz constant $L$ that are equal where territories overlap,
from the phase of one oscillator population assigned to it, so that the phase
agreement of the two populations bounds the agreement of the two contents. It
is a modelling choice about cortex, since the coupling of a field is spatial
while two territories describing one quantity need not be neighbours. Under it
the coupling's reach bounds phase disagreement between distant territories
directly and content disagreement through it.

Simulated sheets at identical frequencies, of side \uSideSmall{} and
\uSideLarge{}, with nearest-neighbour, exponential and power-law kernels of
tail exponent $\sigma=1$ and $\sigma=3$ at a common row sum, follow the harmonic
identity in the ordered phase. The measured phase variance is at most
\uHarmonicRatioLow{} times $D\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}$ at $D=\uNoiseLow$ and at most
\uHarmonicRatioMid{} times at $D=\uNoiseMid$, the excess rising with noise as
the cosine coupling softens, and a logarithm fits its growth with distance
better than a line in \uLogarithmicRuns{} of the \uOrderedRuns{} runs at those
noise levels. At $D=\uNoiseHigh$ the nearest-neighbour sheet disorders: the
mean chord discrepancy $\langle 1-\cos(\theta_a-\theta_b)\rangle$ at the
largest separation is \uChordNearestHigh{}. The exponential and $\sigma=1$
sheets stay ordered at that noise (\uChordExponentialHigh{} and
\uChordSigmaOneHigh{}), and the $\sigma=3$ sheet nearly disorders
(\uChordSigmaThreeHigh{}), so the kernel's width sets the order--disorder
crossover as well as the rate of decay. Exact resistance sums at sheet sides
\uScalingSideA{}, \uScalingSideB{} and \uScalingSideC{} separate width from
tail. The exponential kernel, with \uStiffnessRatio{} times the
nearest-neighbour stiffness, gives resistance at half the side of
\uFarExponentialA{}, \uFarExponentialB{} and \uFarExponentialC{}, still
growing; the $\sigma=1$ tail gives \uFarSigmaOneA{}, \uFarSigmaOneB{} and
\uFarSigmaOneC{}, and the $\sigma=3$ tail, steeper than $r^{-4}$, gives
\uFarSigmaThreeA{}, \uFarSigmaThreeB{} and \uFarSigmaThreeC{}, growing as a
short-range kernel does.

\section{Agreement in time for cortex}
\label{app:window}

The longest cortico-cortical fibres run
about \ueFibreCm{}~cm \citep{nunez2014}. Callosal axons conduct at median
velocities of \ueVelocitySlow{} to \ueVelocityFast{}~m/s, and the fastest of
them at up to \ueVelocityMax{}~m/s \citep{caminiti2009}. A chemical synapse
adds about \ueSynapseMs{}~ms \citep{katz1965,sabatini1996}. The earliest a
synaptic signal can cross the cortex is therefore \ueConeMaxMs{}~ms, along the
fastest axons, and typical arrival takes \ueConeFastMs{} to \ueConeSlowMs{}~ms.
For the time scale of a content, take the unconscious integration that precedes
a conscious percept, which lasts up to \ueContentMs{}~ms \citep{herzog2016}.
Count agreement as reached when disagreement has fallen \ueReduction{}-fold,
decaying exponentially. Agreement then arrives in time when the coupling's relaxation
time is at most \ueTauSlowMs{}~ms with typical arrival along the slower fibres,
and \ueTauFastMs{}~ms along the faster. Over a localized extent of
\ueLocalCm{}~cm the earliest arrival is \ueConeLocalMaxMs{}~ms, typical arrival
along the faster fibres is \ueConeLocalFastMs{}~ms, and the relaxation time may
reach \ueTauLocalMs{}~ms. Signal travel thus uses only a small
part of the time available, and whether agreement arrives in time depends on how fast
the coupling settles.

\section{The occupancy computation}
\label{app:occupancy}

The membrane is an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process in units of its own stationary
standard deviation, with the threshold at zero. After a spike it is refractory
and then reset to its mean, and the mean is calibrated so that the stationary
rate equals the declared rate. Probability is propagated on a voltage grid
through the exact Ornstein--Uhlenbeck step, and a crossing between two sampled
times is counted with the Brownian-bridge probability
$\exp(-2xy/s^2)$ for sub-threshold samples $x$ and $y$ and step deviation $s$.
Nothing is sampled. From each grid state, the law of the first spike time in
the window, with its absence as one more outcome, is propagated in one pass, and
the response to a change is the total-variation distance between two such laws.
A state passes when every admissible change moves the law by at least the
fluctuation, where a change is admissible if it is at least one noise amplitude
of the carrier and does not carry the membrane across threshold. The fraction of
occupied states that pass is the stationary mass of the passing states. The
probability of entering a passing state within a window is computed on the same
chain with the passing states made absorbing. A carrier that supplies a share
$\varphi$ of the membrane's noise variance has noise amplitude $\sqrt{\varphi}$
in the membrane's units, and its changes are scaled accordingly. The bit is a
carrier restored each step to a rail at a declared margin, read after one step of
unit Gaussian noise, with occupancy given by that noise around the rail. The
same computation is repeated at yardsticks from \uYardLow{} to \uYardHigh{} in
place of the fluctuation, at every regime and noise share. For correlated
carriers, each membrane is the sum of a shared Ornstein--Uhlenbeck component of
variance $c$ and a private one of variance $1-c$, both with the membrane's time
constant. Given the shared path the carriers are independent, so the
probability that none of $K$ passes within a window is the average of
$(1-h)^K$ over shared paths, where $h$ is one carrier's chance of passing given
the path. For each of \uCorPaths{} shared paths from a fixed seed, the
membrane's probability is propagated through the exact step with the private
noise, its centre moved by the shared path's increment, after a burn-in of five
time constants. Halving
both the grid spacing and the time step changes the passing fraction by
\uOccResolutionPercent\%, and the correlation of two carriers' passing by at
most \uCorResolutionRho{}.

The switching chip is counted in closed form. On the rising half of a
transition the node's voltage crosses its threshold at a slew $\dot V$, a change
$\Delta V$ moves the crossing by $\Delta V/\dot V$ and the node's noise jitters
it by $\sigma/\dot V$, so the response is $2\Phi(\Delta V/2\sigma)-1$ whatever
the slew. Every admissible change of at least one noise amplitude then reaches
the fluctuation, exactly at one amplitude, so the node passes at the
fluctuation and at no higher yardstick. A node that switches in a fraction
$\alpha$ of the cycles of a clock of frequency $f$, with transitions of
duration $t$, spends $\alpha f t/2$ of its time on the rising half of one, and a
window of $N$ cycles holds no transition with probability $(1-\alpha)^N$. A
synchronizer with aperture $T_W$, clock $f_C$ and data changing at rate $f_D$
samples inside its aperture at the rate $T_W f_C f_D$, so at most a share $T_W
f_D$ of its samples do. Each sample is read as one bit, so it passes at no
yardstick above $\Phi(1)-1/2$, and at lower yardsticks that share bounds its
passing fraction. A second stage allowed a time $S$ to resolve fails
once in $e^{S/\tau}/(T_W f_C f_D)$ on average, with $\tau$ the stage's
resolution time constant \citep{ginosar2011}; the declared values are that
tutorial's 28~nm example, $\tau=\uSwTauPs$~ps and $f_D=\uSwDataMHz$~MHz, with
$S$ one cycle.
